\chapter{Signal, Noise, and Detection in CCD Photometry}
\label{ch:ccd}

Chapter~\ref{ch:photometry} measured the light of a star as a flux and wrote it as a magnitude. Chapter~\ref{ch:resolution} focused that light into an image on a detector. Most optical and infrared images today are taken with a CCD (charge-coupled device). It is a thin slab of silicon divided into a grid of pixels. Light frees electrons in the silicon, each pixel holds its electrons during the exposure, and at the end the electrons in every pixel are counted.

A count of electrons is a whole number, and it changes from one exposure to the next even when the star does not. How much it changes decides whether a faint star is seen at all, how well its magnitude is known, and whether one long exposure is better than several short ones. All three answers come from one equation for the signal-to-noise ratio, built from the statistics of counting.

% ==============================================================================
\section{Counting Photoelectrons}
\label{cc:sec:counting}

Chapter~\ref{ch:photometry} gave the light of a star as a flux $F$ in $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, and Chapter~\ref{ch:resolution} gave the size of its image on the detector.

A CCD does not report watts. It reports a number of electrons in each pixel, and part of that number did not come from the star at all.

Picture a grid of buckets on a lawn, with a garden sprinkler running over one spot during a light drizzle. After ten minutes the buckets near the sprinkler hold most of its water. Every bucket also holds the same depth of drizzle, and a little water that has condensed on its inside. To read a bucket we pour it into a measuring jug and read the scale, which adds a small error of its own. To find what the sprinkler gave, we add up the buckets around it and take away what the drizzle and the condensation would have put there.

The pixels are the buckets. The star is the sprinkler, the faint glow of the night sky is the drizzle, electrons freed by heat in the silicon are the condensation, and the electronics that measure each pixel's charge are the jug. The signal is the number of electrons that the star alone put into the pixels around its image.

In numbers, a star that frees $50$ electrons per second in its image gives $50\times60=3000$ electrons in a one-minute exposure. If its image covers $20$ pixels and the sky frees $2$ electrons per second in each pixel, the sky adds $2\times20\times60=2400$ electrons under the star.

Picture the image as a small hill of electrons standing on a wide, flat plain of sky electrons. The signal is the volume of the hill above the plain.

A Geiger counter measures a radioactive sample in the same way, on top of the counts from cosmic rays and the walls of the room. So does a rain gauge in a garden that is also watered. Here we name the rates and times in which the noise of a count is written.

\paragraph{Photons become electrons.} A photon that enters a pixel frees one electron with a probability $\eta(\lambda)$, the \textbf{quantum efficiency} of the detector. For modern astronomical CCDs $\eta$ is about $0.5$ to $0.85$. So
\begin{equation*}
	\text{number of electrons} = \eta\times\text{number of photons that reach the pixel},
\end{equation*}
and $1000$ photons give about $800$ electrons when $\eta=0.8$. The factor $\eta$ multiplies every rate that comes from light, so we fold it into those rates and count only electrons from here on. Every rate below is a rate of detected electrons.

\paragraph{The rates.} The light of the star is spread over several pixels by the image blur. We therefore take $R_{\rm src}$ to be the rate at which the star frees electrons in all the pixels of the measuring aperture together. The sky is the same in every pixel, so $R_{\rm sky}$ is a rate per pixel. Heat frees \textbf{dark electrons} at the rate $R_{\rm dark}$ in every pixel, light or no light, and they look exactly like electrons freed by photons. The aperture contains $n_{\rm pix}$ pixels. Detector manuals often quote the dark rate per hour, and dividing by $3600$ turns it into a rate per second. All the symbols are collected in Table~\ref{tab:ccd_notation}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
	\centering
	\begin{tabular}{@{}l p{9.8cm} l@{}}
		\toprule
		\textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Meaning} & \textbf{Units} \\

\paragraph{The signal.} Over a total time $t$ the star gives
\begin{equation}
	S = R_{\rm src}\,t
	\label{eq:ccd_signal_total}
\end{equation}
electrons. In practice $t$ is often split into $N_{\rm exp}$ exposures of length $t_{\rm exp}$ each, which are added afterwards,
\begin{equation}
	t = N_{\rm exp}\,t_{\rm exp}, \qquad S = R_{\rm src}\,N_{\rm exp}\,t_{\rm exp}.
	\label{eq:ccd_signal_partition}
\end{equation}
The difference between $t$ and $N_{\rm exp}$ matters. Electrons pile up in proportion to the total time only. The electronics add their error once at every reading, so that part grows with the number of exposures.

\section{Shot Noise and the Poisson Distribution}
\label{cc:sec:poisson}

We can now write the signal as a mean number of electrons, $S=R_{\rm src}t$.

A real exposure does not give the mean. It gives one count, and a second exposure of the same steady star gives a different count. To say how far a count can stray from its mean we need the probability of every possible count.

Stand by a quiet road at night and count the cars that pass in each minute. The traffic is steady, about ten cars a minute on average. Yet one minute brings seven and the next thirteen. Nothing about the road changed. The cars simply come one at a time, each at its own moment, without arranging themselves to arrive evenly.

Photons from a steady star arrive in the same way, one at a time and independently. So the number of electrons in a fixed exposure scatters about its mean. The scatter is called \textbf{shot noise}. It comes from counting, not from any fault of the detector, and its size is the square root of the mean count.

In numbers, a mean of $10$ cars a minute gives a scatter of about $\sqrt{10}\approx3.2$, so most minutes bring between $7$ and $13$ cars. A mean of $100$ gives a scatter of $10$, which is $10$ per cent of the mean. A mean of $10\,000$ gives $100$, only $1$ per cent. The scatter grows with the mean, but more slowly, so it becomes a smaller fraction of it.

Picture a histogram of many repeated counts. It is a hump centred on the mean count, with a width equal to its square root. For a larger mean the hump moves right and gets wider, but it gets narrower compared with its distance from zero.

The same counting statistics give the shot noise of an electric current, first studied by Schottky in vacuum tubes, and the scatter in the clicks of a Geiger counter. The total count is a sum of many independent small pieces, and its variance grows in proportion to the number of pieces. This is the mathematics of the random walk, principle P5 (Section~\ref{pr:sec:p5}), where the square of the distance grows in proportion to the number of steps. Here it gives the Poisson distribution of a count, its mean and variance, and the Gaussian shape it takes when the count is large.

\paragraph{Three assumptions.} The derivation rests on two physical facts about the arrivals and one mild mathematical condition.
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Independence.} One arrival says nothing about when the next one comes. There is no memory and no bunching.
	\item \textbf{Constant rate.} The source does not change its brightness during the exposure, so the chance of an arrival is the same in every interval of the same length.
	\item \textbf{Smoothness.} The probability $f(\Delta t)$ of an arrival in an interval of length $\Delta t$ is a smooth function near $\Delta t=0$, so it has a Taylor series there.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{One short interval.} An interval of zero length holds nothing, so $f(0)=0$. For a short interval,
\begin{align}
	f(\Delta t)
	&\eqby{1} f(0) + f'(0)\,\Delta t + O(\Delta t^2) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} f'(0)\,\Delta t + O(\Delta t^2).
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Taylor series about $\Delta t=0$, allowed by the smoothness condition.
	\item $f(0)=0$.
\end{explain}
So for any short interval the chance of an arrival is proportional to its length. This is true of any probability that starts from zero, not just of light. Two arrivals in the same interval need two independent rare events at once, so their probability is of order $\Delta t^2$.

\paragraph{Many short intervals.} Divide the exposure $t$ into $N$ equal intervals of length $\Delta t=t/N$. In each one, an arrival happens with probability
\begin{equation*}
	p = f'(0)\,\frac tN \equiv \frac{\bar n}{N}, \qquad \bar n\equiv f'(0)\,t ,
\end{equation*}
and two or more arrivals happen with a probability of order $1/N^2$, which we drop as $N\to\infty$. Each interval then holds either no arrival or one. The intervals are independent, so the probability of a particular pattern with arrivals in $n$ chosen intervals is $p^n(1-p)^{N-n}$, and there are $\binom Nn$ ways to choose the $n$ intervals. The count $n$ therefore follows the binomial distribution. Letting $N\to\infty$ with $\bar n$ fixed,
\begin{align}
	P(n)
	&\eqby{1} \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{N!}{n!\,(N-n)!}\left(\frac{\bar n}{N}\right)^{n}\left(1-\frac{\bar n}{N}\right)^{N-n} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \frac{\bar n^{\,n}}{n!}\,\lim_{N\to\infty}\left[\frac{N!}{(N-n)!\,N^n}\right]\left(1-\frac{\bar n}{N}\right)^{N}\left(1-\frac{\bar n}{N}\right)^{-n} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \frac{\bar n^{\,n}}{n!}\cdot 1\cdot e^{-\bar n}\cdot 1 .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The binomial distribution with $p=\bar n/N$.
	\item Regroup, taking out the factors that do not depend on $N$.
	\item $N!/[(N-n)!\,N^n]=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-j/N)$ is a product of a fixed number $n$ of factors that each tend to $1$. The logarithm of the middle factor is $N\ln(1-\bar n/N)=N(-\bar n/N-\bar n^2/2N^2-\dots)\to-\bar n$. The last factor has a fixed power $n$ and tends to $1$.
\end{explain}
This is the \textbf{Poisson distribution},
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{P(n) = \frac{\bar n^{\,n}\,e^{-\bar n}}{n!}.}
	\label{eq:poisson_dist}
\end{equation}
It depends on the rate and the time only through $\bar n$. Its probabilities add up to one (Problem~\ref{cc:prob:poisson}).

\paragraph{Mean.} The mean count is
\begin{align}
	\avg{n}
	&\eqby{1} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} n\,\frac{\bar n^{\,n}e^{-\bar n}}{n!} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} e^{-\bar n}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\bar n^{\,n}}{(n-1)!} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \bar n\,e^{-\bar n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\bar n^{\,j}}{j!} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{4} \bar n .
	\label{eq:poisson_mean}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The definition of the mean, with Eq.~\eqref{eq:poisson_dist}.
	\item The $n=0$ term is zero, and $n/n!=1/(n-1)!$.
	\item Put $j=n-1$ and take one factor $\bar n$ out of the sum.
	\item The sum is the series of $e^{\bar n}$.
\end{explain}
So $\bar n$ is the mean count, and $f'(0)=\bar n/t$ is the mean number of arrivals per second. For the star it is $R_{\rm src}$, and for the sky and the dark current it is $n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm sky}$ and $n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark}$.

\paragraph{Variance.} The variance is $\sigma^2\equiv\avg{(n-\avg n)^2}$. The factor $n(n-1)$ cancels cleanly against $n!$, so we first find the average of $n(n-1)$,
\begin{align}
	\avg{n(n-1)}
	&\eqby{1} e^{-\bar n}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\bar n^{\,n}}{(n-2)!} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \bar n^2\,e^{-\bar n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\bar n^{\,j}}{j!} = \bar n^2 .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The terms $n=0$ and $n=1$ are zero, and $n(n-1)/n!=1/(n-2)!$.
	\item Put $j=n-2$, take $\bar n^2$ out, and sum the series of $e^{\bar n}$.
\end{explain}
Then
\begin{align}
	\sigma^2
	&\eqby{1} \avg{n^2}-\avg{n}^2 \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \avg{n(n-1)}+\avg{n}-\avg{n}^2 \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \bar n^2+\bar n-\bar n^2 = \bar n .
	\label{eq:poisson_var}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Expand the square. $\avg n$ is a constant, so $\avg{2n\avg n}=2\avg n^2$.
	\item $n^2=n(n-1)+n$.
	\item The two averages just found, Eq.~\eqref{eq:poisson_mean} and the one above.
\end{explain}
The shot noise, the standard deviation of the count, is therefore
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\sigma=\sqrt{\bar n}.}
	\label{eq:shot_noise_sigma}
\end{equation}
The noise grows as the square root of the mean, so the fractional noise $\sigma/\bar n=1/\sqrt{\bar n}$ falls as the count grows. A computer algebra check of the three sums gives $1$, $\bar n$ and $\bar n$ exactly. Figure~\ref{fig:poisson_distributions} shows the distribution for three means.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figs/fig0_poisson.png}
	\caption{The Poisson distribution for mean counts of $5$, $20$ and $80$ (labelled $\mu$ in the plot). A dotted line marks each mean, and a double arrow spans one standard deviation on each side of it.}
	\label{fig:poisson_distributions}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why the variance grows as the count.} The same variance follows from a second route, which shows where the square root comes from. We need one general fact first. Let a quantity $X=\sum_iX_i$ be a sum of independent parts, and write $\delta X_i=X_i-\avg{X_i}$ for the deviation of each part from its mean. Then
\begin{align}
	\sigma_X^2
	&\eqby{1} \Big\langle\Big(\sum_i\delta X_i\Big)^2\Big\rangle \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \sum_i\sum_j\avg{\delta X_i\,\delta X_j} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \sum_i\avg{\delta X_i^2}+\sum_{i\neq j}\avg{\delta X_i}\avg{\delta X_j} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{4} \sum_i\sigma_i^2 .
	\label{cc:eq:var_add}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $X-\avg X=\sum_i\delta X_i$, because the mean of a sum is the sum of the means.
	\item Expand the square of the sum into a double sum.
	\item Split off the terms with $i=j$. For $i\neq j$ the parts are independent, so the average of the product is the product of the averages.
	\item $\avg{\delta X_i}=0$ by its definition, and $\avg{\delta X_i^2}=\sigma_i^2$.
\end{explain}
The variances of independent parts add, whatever the distribution of each part. Standard deviations therefore add \textbf{in quadrature}, $\sigma_X=\sqrt{\sum_i\sigma_i^2}$. The cross terms die for the same reason as in the random walk, where they gave $R_{\rm rms}=\sqrt N\,s$ (Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:sqrtN}).

Now let $X_i$ be the number of arrivals in the $i$-th short interval. It is $1$ with probability $p$ and $0$ otherwise, so $\avg{X_i}=p$ and $\avg{X_i^2}=p$, which gives $\sigma_i^2=p-p^2$. The count is $n=\sum_iX_i$, and
\begin{align}
	\sigma^2
	&\eqby{1} \sum_{i=1}^{N}p\,(1-p) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \bar n\left(1-\frac{\bar n}{N}\right) \nonumber\\
	&\to \bar n \quad (N\to\infty).
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:var_add}, with the same variance $p(1-p)$ for every interval.
	\item $Np=\bar n$.
\end{explain}
This agrees with Eq.~\eqref{eq:poisson_var}. Each interval adds the same small variance, so the variance grows in proportion to the number of intervals, and so in proportion to the mean count. The noise grows only as its square root.

\paragraph{Large counts look Gaussian.} For a large mean the Poisson hump becomes the Gaussian bell curve. To see this we write $n=\bar n+x$ and use Stirling's formula, $\ln n!\approx n\ln n-n+\tfrac12\ln(2\pi n)$, for large $n$.
\begin{align}
	\ln P(n)
	&\eqby{1} n\ln\bar n-\bar n-n\ln n+n-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi n) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} -(\bar n+x)\ln\!\left(1+\frac{x}{\bar n}\right)+x-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi n) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} -(\bar n+x)\left(\frac{x}{\bar n}-\frac{x^2}{2\bar n^2}+\dots\right)+x-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\bar n)+\dots \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{4} -\frac{x^2}{2\bar n}-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\bar n)+O\!\left(\frac{x^3}{\bar n^2},\frac{x}{\bar n}\right).
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The logarithm of Eq.~\eqref{eq:poisson_dist}, with Stirling's formula for $\ln n!$.
	\item $n\ln\bar n-n\ln n=-n\ln(n/\bar n)$ with $n=\bar n+x$, and $-\bar n+n=x$.
	\item Taylor series $\ln(1+y)=y-y^2/2+\dots$, and $\ln(2\pi n)=\ln(2\pi\bar n)+\ln(1+x/\bar n)$.
	\item Multiply out. The terms in $x$ cancel, and $x^2/2\bar n-x^2/\bar n=-x^2/2\bar n$.
\end{explain}
Dropping the small terms,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{P(n)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\bar n}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(n-\bar n)^2}{2\bar n}\right].}
	\label{cc:eq:gauss_limit}
\end{equation}
This is a Gaussian with mean $\bar n$ and variance $\bar n$, as it must be. The dropped term $x^3/\bar n^2$ is $1/\sqrt{\bar n}$ at one standard deviation, $x=\sqrt{\bar n}$. So the Gaussian is good when $\bar n\gg1$ and within a few standard deviations of the mean. The Poisson distribution is defined only at whole numbers and the Gaussian is smooth, but when $\sqrt{\bar n}\gg1$ the steps of one electron are tiny compared with the width, and the difference cannot be seen (Figure~\ref{cc:fig:poisson_gauss} in Problem~\ref{cc:prob:poisson}). A computer algebra expansion of $\ln P(n)$ to second order in $x$ gives the same leading terms.

\section{The Noise Budget and the Signal-to-Noise Equation}
\label{cc:sec:budget}

We now know that a count with mean $\bar n$ has a standard deviation $\sqrt{\bar n}$.

The number we read from a CCD is not one clean count. It is the star's electrons on top of sky and dark electrons, measured by electronics that add an error of their own. We need the noise of the star's share alone, after the rest has been taken away.

Weigh yourself on a bathroom scale while holding a cat, then weigh yourself alone, and subtract. Each reading of the scale wanders by about $0.2\,\mathrm{kg}$. Your own weight cancels in the difference, but the wandering of the second reading does not. The cat's weight carries both.

In an image, the star's electrons sit on top of the sky and dark electrons. We subtract the mean sky and dark level, but their scatter stays in the result, and the readout adds more. The idea is that the variances of independent sources of noise add.

In numbers, the cat's weight has an error of $\sqrt{0.2^2+0.2^2}=0.28\,\mathrm{kg}$, not $0.4\,\mathrm{kg}$. Two errors that are independent partly cancel as often as they add.

Picture each source of noise as a side of a box, all at right angles to each other. The total noise is the long diagonal of the box, as in Pythagoras' theorem.

Every laboratory error budget adds independent errors in this way, and so does an engineer adding noise powers in a circuit. It is the same rule that made the random walk grow as $\sqrt N$ (P5, Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:sqrtN}), and it was proved for any independent parts in Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:var_add}. Applied to the four sources of electrons in an aperture, it gives the signal-to-noise equation of a CCD, which every exposure time calculator evaluates.

\paragraph{Signal-to-noise ratio.} Let $\sigma_{\rm tot}$ be the standard deviation of the measured signal $S$, after the mean sky and dark levels have been subtracted. The measurement is reported as $S\pm\sigma_{\rm tot}$, and its \textbf{signal-to-noise ratio} is
\begin{equation}
	\SNR \equiv \frac{S}{\sigma_{\rm tot}}.
	\label{eq:snr_def}
\end{equation}
Its inverse $1/\SNR=\sigma_{\rm tot}/S$ is the fractional error of the flux. Our goal is $\sigma_{\rm tot}$ in terms of the rates and times of Table~\ref{tab:ccd_notation}.

\paragraph{Shot noise of the star.} The electrons from the star in time $t$ have the mean $S=R_{\rm src}t$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:ccd_signal_total}). The rate $R_{\rm src}$ is already summed over the aperture, so no factor $n_{\rm pix}$ appears. Eq.~\eqref{eq:shot_noise_sigma} gives
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{\rm src} = \sqrt{S} = \sqrt{R_{\rm src}\,t}.
	\label{eq:sigma_src}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Shot noise of the sky.} The sky gives $R_{\rm sky}$ per pixel, so the aperture collects a mean of $n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm sky}t$ sky electrons. We assume that the mean sky level is measured over a large empty area nearby, so that its own error is small. Subtracting it removes the mean, but the Poisson scatter of the sky electrons inside the aperture remains,
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{\rm sky} = \sqrt{n_{\rm pix}\,R_{\rm sky}\,t}.
	\label{eq:sigma_sky}
\end{equation}
The aperture must hold the blurred image, so its area grows as the square of the seeing, $n_{\rm pix}\propto\theta_{\rm seeing}^2$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:seeing_resolution}). Good seeing means fewer pixels and less sky noise.

\paragraph{Shot noise of the dark current.} Heat in the silicon frees electrons at random moments, independently and at a steady rate, just as photons arrive. So the dark electrons are also Poisson counts, with mean $n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark}t$ in the aperture,
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{\rm dark} = \sqrt{n_{\rm pix}\,R_{\rm dark}\,t}.
	\label{eq:sigma_dark}
\end{equation}
Cooling the detector lowers $R_{\rm dark}$, which is why astronomical CCDs are kept cold.

\paragraph{Readout noise.} The readout noise is not a count of anything. Dark current adds extra charge to a pixel before it is measured. Readout noise is an error in the measurement itself, after the charge is fixed. The charge of each pixel is turned into a voltage by an output transistor and then into a number by an analogue-to-digital converter. Both have their own electrical noise, the thermal (Johnson--Nyquist) noise of any resistor and a slowly wandering ``$1/f$'' noise. Each reading of a pixel therefore gets a random error with a Gaussian distribution and a standard deviation $\sigma_{\rm RON}$, whatever charge the pixel holds and however long the exposure was. Every reading is independent, and the aperture is read $n_{\rm pix}$ times in each of $N_{\rm exp}$ exposures, so
\begin{align}
	\sigma_{\rm read}^2
	&\eqby{1} \sum_{\text{readings}}\sigma_{\rm RON}^2 \nonumber\\
	&= n_{\rm pix}\,N_{\rm exp}\,\sigma_{\rm RON}^2 . \nonumber
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:var_add}, with the same variance for every reading.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\sigma_{\rm read} = \sqrt{n_{\rm pix}\,N_{\rm exp}}\;\sigma_{\rm RON}.}
	\label{eq:sigma_read}
\end{equation}
The readout variance grows with $N_{\rm exp}$, not with $t$. Splitting a total time into $N_{\rm exp}$ exposures pays the readout variance $N_{\rm exp}$ times.

\paragraph{Adding the four.} The star, the sky, the heat in the silicon and the readout electronics are independent of each other, so Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:var_add} adds their variances,
\begin{align}
	\sigma_{\rm tot}^2
	&\eqby{1} \sigma_{\rm src}^2 + \sigma_{\rm sky}^2 + \sigma_{\rm dark}^2 + \sigma_{\rm read}^2 \label{eq:variance_addition}\\
	&\eqby{2} R_{\rm src}\,t + n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm sky}\,t + n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark}\,t + n_{\rm pix}N_{\rm exp}\,\sigma_{\rm RON}^2 . \nonumber
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:var_add} for four independent parts.
	\item Eqs.~\eqref{eq:sigma_src}--\eqref{eq:sigma_read}.
\end{explain}
Putting this and $S=R_{\rm src}t$ into Eq.~\eqref{eq:snr_def} gives the \textbf{CCD signal-to-noise equation},
\begin{keyresult}
	\begin{equation}
		\SNR = \frac{R_{\rm src}\,t}{\sqrt{R_{\rm src}\,t \;+\; n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm sky}\,t \;+\; n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark}\,t \;+\; n_{\rm pix}N_{\rm exp}\,\sigma_{\rm RON}^2}},
		\qquad t=N_{\rm exp}t_{\rm exp}.
		\label{eq:master_ccd_snr}
	\end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
The signal and the first three variances grow in proportion to $t$. The last variance grows with $N_{\rm exp}$. So the signal-to-noise ratio depends on how the time is split as well as on the total time. Figure~\ref{fig:noise_budget} shows the share of each variance in three typical observations.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figs/fig1_budget.png}
	\caption{Share of each term in the total variance $\sigma_{\rm tot}^2$, in per cent, for three observations. A bright source in one $60\,\mathrm s$ exposure, a sky-limited source in four $300\,\mathrm s$ exposures, and a faint source in forty $30\,\mathrm s$ exposures.}
	\label{fig:noise_budget}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Dark current and read noise from two dark frames.} The detector's own two terms can be measured with the shutter closed. Such a \textbf{dark frame} has no star and no sky. Its variance over an area of $n_{\rm pix}$ pixels, for an exposure of length $t$, is the last two terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:variance_addition} with $N_{\rm exp}=1$,
\begin{equation*}
	\sigma^2(t) = n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark}\,t + n_{\rm pix}\sigma_{\rm RON}^2 .
\end{equation*}
This is a straight line in $t$. Its slope is the dark variance per unit time and its intercept is the read variance. Two unknowns need two measurements, and two dark frames of lengths $t_1$ and $t_2$ with variances $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$ give
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{pmatrix} t_1 & 1\\ t_2 & 1\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{pmatrix} n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark}\\ n_{\rm pix}\sigma_{\rm RON}^2\end{pmatrix}
	=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_1^2\\ \sigma_2^2\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
The determinant of the matrix is $t_1-t_2$. Two frames of the same length give the same equation twice and cannot separate the two terms. For $t_1\neq t_2$,
\begin{align}
	\begin{pmatrix} n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark}\\ n_{\rm pix}\sigma_{\rm RON}^2\end{pmatrix}
	&\eqby{1} \frac{1}{t_1-t_2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1\\ -t_2 & t_1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_1^2\\ \sigma_2^2\end{pmatrix} \nonumber\\
	&= \frac{1}{t_2-t_1}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_2^2-\sigma_1^2\\ t_2\sigma_1^2-t_1\sigma_2^2\end{pmatrix}.
	\label{cc:eq:dark_solution}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The inverse of a $2\times2$ matrix, $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$.
\end{explain}
The dark variance per unit time is the rise in variance divided by the rise in time, the slope of the line in Figure~\ref{cc:fig:dark_frames}. The read variance is where the line meets $t=0$. A third frame of another length tests whether the points really lie on a straight line. Problem~\ref{cc:prob:dark} puts in numbers.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figs/fig_ch7_dark_frames.pdf}
	\caption{Variance of a dark frame against exposure time. The filled points are frames of $1$ and $3$ minutes with variances $a$ and $2.6a$. The straight line through them meets the vertical axis at $0.2a$ (open circle).}
	\label{cc:fig:dark_frames}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Defects that are not noise.} Some flaws of a CCD frame are not random scatter and must be found and removed before Eq.~\eqref{eq:variance_addition} means anything.
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Hot and cold pixels.} Single pixels with a far higher dark rate (hot) or a far lower quantum efficiency (cold). They are found in calibration frames and flagged or filled in from their neighbours.
	\item \textbf{Saturation and bleeding.} A pixel holds only a limited charge, its \textbf{full well} (Section~\ref{sec:regime1}). Near it the response stops being linear, and beyond it extra charge spills into neighbouring pixels along a column, leaving a bright \emph{bleed trail}.
	\item \textbf{Cosmic rays.} Fast charged particles leave a sharp spike of charge in one frame only. Comparing several exposures (Section~\ref{sec:regime2}) or the shape of the spike, which is sharper than any star, removes them.
	\item \textbf{Airglow and city lights.} Light emitted by the upper atmosphere and light scattered from towns raise $R_{\rm sky}$ itself, and with it $\sigma_{\rm sky}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigma_sky}, above the level of a dark site on a moonless night.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Dithering}, moving the telescope by a few arcseconds between exposures, deals with several of these at once. A given point of the sky then falls on a different pixel in each exposure. A bad pixel or a bleed trail stays fixed on the detector and a cosmic ray appears in one frame only, so when the frames are lined up on the sky and combined they can be rejected.

\section{Three Regimes of the Signal-to-Noise Equation}
\label{cc:sec:regimes}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:master_ccd_snr} gives the signal-to-noise ratio of any exposure.

As it stands it is hard to plan with. Should we take one long exposure or many short ones? Does good seeing matter? Does a larger telescope help a faint star more than a bright one? The answer depends on which of the four terms under the square root is largest.

Think of a taxi fare. There is a fixed charge for each ride and a charge for each minute. On one long ride the minutes dominate and the fixed charge hardly matters. Split the same trip into many short rides and the fixed charges pile up. Whether splitting matters depends on which part of the fare is larger.

In the signal-to-noise equation the star, the sky and the dark current charge their variance per second, and the readout charges its variance per exposure. When one term is much larger than the others, the equation simplifies and tells us directly what to do. Which term wins is decided by comparing two numbers, principle P6 (Section~\ref{pr:sec:p6}).

In numbers, take a taxi that charges $50$ rupees per ride and $10$ rupees per minute. One $20$-minute ride costs $250$ rupees, while ten rides of $2$ minutes cost $700$. For a CCD with $\sigma_{\rm RON}=5\,\mathrm{e^-}$, an aperture of $20$ pixels and a sky of $2\,\mathrm{e^-\,s^{-1}}$ per pixel, each reading adds a variance of $20\times25=500\,\mathrm{e^{-2}}$ and the sky adds $40\,\mathrm{e^{-2}}$ per second. A $300\,\mathrm s$ exposure collects a sky variance of $12\,000\,\mathrm{e^{-2}}$, so its one reading hardly matters. A $10\,\mathrm s$ exposure collects only $400\,\mathrm{e^{-2}}$ of sky variance, less than the readout.

Picture the four variances as bars, three that grow with time and one that grows with the number of exposures. The tallest bar sets how the signal-to-noise ratio responds to any change.

Ratios of this kind choose the regime everywhere in physics, from the Reynolds number of a flow to the optical depth $\tau$ of a dust cloud (Chapter~\ref{ch:photometry}). Here they give the shortest useful exposure and the three limits of the equation, each with its own way of observing.

\paragraph{How long one exposure should be.} For a faint star, $R_{\rm src}$ is small next to the sky and dark rates. Then the variance in Eq.~\eqref{eq:variance_addition} is
\begin{align}
	\sigma_{\rm tot}^2
	&\relby{1}{\approx} n_{\rm pix}(R_{\rm sky}+R_{\rm dark})\,t + n_{\rm pix}N_{\rm exp}\,\sigma_{\rm RON}^2 \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} n_{\rm pix}N_{\rm exp}\left[(R_{\rm sky}+R_{\rm dark})\,t_{\rm exp} + \sigma_{\rm RON}^2\right] \nonumber\\
	&= n_{\rm pix}(R_{\rm sky}+R_{\rm dark})\,t\left[1+\frac{\sigma_{\rm RON}^2}{(R_{\rm sky}+R_{\rm dark})\,t_{\rm exp}}\right].
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Drop the star's own shot noise, $R_{\rm src}t$.
	\item $t=N_{\rm exp}t_{\rm exp}$.
\end{explain}
The last factor compares the read variance of one pixel with the sky and dark variance that the pixel collects in one exposure. Splitting the time costs little when that ratio is small,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{t_{\rm exp}\gg\frac{\sigma_{\rm RON}^2}{R_{\rm sky}+R_{\rm dark}}.}
	\label{cc:eq:texp_min}
\end{equation}
For the numbers above the right side is $25/2=12.5\,\mathrm s$. Exposures of $300\,\mathrm s$ lower the signal-to-noise ratio by a factor $(1+12.5/300)^{-1/2}=0.98$, only $2$ per cent. The bound depends only on the detector and the sky, not on the star.

\subsection{Regime 1: Bright Source, Limited by Its Own Shot Noise}
\label{sec:regime1}

For a bright star the first term under the root is much larger than the others, $R_{\rm src}t\gg n_{\rm pix}(R_{\rm sky}+R_{\rm dark})t+n_{\rm pix}N_{\rm exp}\sigma_{\rm RON}^2$. Then
\begin{align}
	\SNR
	&\relby{1}{\approx} \frac{R_{\rm src}\,t}{\sqrt{R_{\rm src}\,t}} \nonumber\\
	&= \sqrt{R_{\rm src}\,t}, \nonumber
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Keep only the star's own term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:master_ccd_snr}.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\SNR \approx \sqrt{R_{\rm src}\,t}\propto\sqrt t.}
	\label{eq:regime_bright}
\end{equation}
The signal-to-noise ratio is the square root of the number of electrons from the star. It does not depend on the sky, the detector or the way the time is split. Reaching a given ratio takes $t=\SNR^2/R_{\rm src}$, so ten times the precision costs a hundred times the time.

Here the difficulty is not to collect enough light but to avoid too much. A pixel saturates at its full well, typically about $10^5$ electrons, and its response stops being linear before that. A bright star is therefore observed with shorter exposures, which cost little here, or with the telescope slightly out of focus, which spreads the light over more pixels.

\subsection{Regime 2: Faint Source on a Bright Sky}
\label{sec:regime2}

For a faint star seen through a broad optical filter, the sky term is the largest, $n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm sky}t\gg R_{\rm src}t,\ n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark}t,\ n_{\rm pix}N_{\rm exp}\sigma_{\rm RON}^2$. Then
\begin{align}
	\SNR
	&\relby{1}{\approx} \frac{R_{\rm src}\,t}{\sqrt{n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm sky}\,t}} \nonumber\\
	&= \frac{R_{\rm src}}{\sqrt{n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm sky}}}\,\sqrt t , \nonumber
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Keep only the sky term under the root in Eq.~\eqref{eq:master_ccd_snr}.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\SNR \approx \frac{R_{\rm src}\sqrt t}{\sqrt{n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm sky}}}\propto\frac{\sqrt t}{\sqrt{n_{\rm pix}}}.}
	\label{eq:regime_sky}
\end{equation}
The ratio still grows as $\sqrt t$, but now in proportion to $R_{\rm src}$, not its square root. A star four times fainter has a quarter of the signal-to-noise ratio and needs sixteen times the time.

\paragraph{Seeing.} Solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:regime_sky} for the time needed to reach a given ratio,
\begin{align}
	t
	&\eqby{1} \SNR^2\,\frac{n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm sky}}{R_{\rm src}^2} \nonumber\\
	&\relby{2}{\propto} \theta_{\rm seeing}^2 .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Square Eq.~\eqref{eq:regime_sky} and solve for $t$.
	\item The aperture must hold the seeing disc, so $n_{\rm pix}\propto\theta_{\rm seeing}^2$, with the star and the sky fixed.
\end{explain}
Seeing of $0.6''$ instead of $1.2''$ needs a quarter of the time for the same result, or doubles the signal-to-noise ratio in the same time. On a bright sky, good seeing is worth as much as a larger telescope.

\paragraph{Splitting is free.} Here the signal-to-noise ratio depends on $t$ and not on $N_{\rm exp}$, as long as each exposure satisfies Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:texp_min}. Splitting $3000\,\mathrm s$ into ten exposures of $300\,\mathrm s$ costs almost nothing. It allows cosmic rays to be rejected and the pointing to be dithered, and it keeps bright stars in the field below saturation.

\subsection{Regime 3: Faint Source Limited by Readout Noise}

When the background is very low, as through a narrow filter, in a spectrograph that spreads the light thinly, or in very short exposures, the readout term is the largest, $n_{\rm pix}N_{\rm exp}\sigma_{\rm RON}^2\gg(R_{\rm src}+n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm sky}+n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark})\,t$. Then
\begin{align}
	\SNR
	&\relby{1}{\approx} \frac{R_{\rm src}\,t}{\sqrt{n_{\rm pix}N_{\rm exp}}\,\sigma_{\rm RON}} , \nonumber
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Keep only the readout term under the root in Eq.~\eqref{eq:master_ccd_snr}.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\SNR \approx \frac{R_{\rm src}\,t}{\sqrt{n_{\rm pix}N_{\rm exp}}\,\sigma_{\rm RON}}\propto\frac{t}{\sqrt{n_{\rm pix}N_{\rm exp}}}.}
	\label{eq:regime_read}
\end{equation}
The noise no longer depends on the light at all, so the ratio grows in proportion to the signal. At a fixed number of exposures it grows as $t$, faster than in the other two regimes. At a fixed total time it falls as $1/\sqrt{N_{\rm exp}}$. A star twice as far away gives a quarter of the electrons and a quarter of the signal-to-noise ratio (Problem~\ref{cc:prob:distance}).

\paragraph{Binning.} The charges of a block of $2\times2$ pixels can be added on the chip before they are read. The block is then read once, so the number of readings in the aperture falls by $4$. The read variance $n_{\rm pix}N_{\rm exp}\sigma_{\rm RON}^2$ falls by $4$, while the shot-noise terms stay the same, because the electrons are the same. In this regime the signal-to-noise ratio rises by $\sqrt4=2$. Binning coarsens the pixel scale, so it stops being useful once about two binned pixels span the seeing disc, the sampling limit of Section~\ref{sec:sampling}.

\paragraph{Long exposures.} At a fixed total time, fewer and longer exposures give fewer readings. The useful length is limited mainly by cosmic rays, which pile up in a long exposure. For CCDs on the ground the usual limit is about $30$ to $45$ minutes.

Figure~\ref{fig:nexp_comparison} shows both cases at a fixed total time.

\section{From Signal-to-Noise to Magnitudes and Detections}
\label{cc:sec:detection}

The signal-to-noise equation tells us how well the electrons of a star are counted.

Astronomers want two other things. They want the error of a magnitude, the unit in which brightness is reported. And for a faint smudge in an image they want to know whether it is a star at all, or just the noise happening to be high in one place.

Toss a coin ten times and get eight heads. It is odd, but a fair coin does it once in about $18$ tries. Toss it a hundred times and get eighty heads, and the coin is surely bent. A fair coin does that about once in two billion tries. The fraction of heads is the same. What changed is how far the result lies from the mean, measured in standard deviations.

A smudge in an image is a count of electrons above the mean sky. If the excess is a few times the noise, chance can produce it. If it is many times the noise, chance almost never does. The idea is that significance is the excess in units of the standard deviation, and the Gaussian shape of the noise turns it into a probability.

In numbers, ten tosses have a mean of $5$ heads and a standard deviation of $\sqrt{10\times\tfrac14}=1.58$, so eight heads is $1.9$ standard deviations high, with a probability of $5.5$ per cent. A hundred tosses have a mean of $50$ and a standard deviation of $5$, so eighty heads is $6$ standard deviations high, with a probability of $5.6\times10^{-10}$.

Picture the bell curve of the noise. A detection threshold is a line drawn far out on its right side, and the false alarms are the thin sliver of the curve beyond the line.

The same reasoning sets the confidence of a medical test, the ``five sigma'' that particle physicists demand before announcing a new particle, and quality control in a factory. Here it gives the error of a magnitude and the threshold for believing a detection.

\subsection{The Error of a Magnitude}

A magnitude is $m=-2.5\log_{10}F+\text{constant}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:apparent_mag_single}). With the same telescope, filter and exposure, the signal $S$ is proportional to the flux $F$, so $m=-2.5\log_{10}S+\text{constant}$. A signal that is high by one standard deviation makes the star look brighter by
\begin{align}
	\delta m
	&\equiv m(S)-m(S+\sigma_{\rm tot}) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{1} 2.5\log_{10}\frac{S+\sigma_{\rm tot}}{S} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} 2.5\log_{10}\!\left(1+\frac{1}{\SNR}\right).
	\label{eq:exact_mag_error}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The constant cancels, and $-\log_{10}a+\log_{10}b=\log_{10}(b/a)$.
	\item Eq.~\eqref{eq:snr_def}.
\end{explain}
For a large signal-to-noise ratio the logarithm is nearly linear,
\begin{align}
	\delta m
	&\eqby{1} \frac{2.5}{\ln10}\,\ln\!\left(1+\frac{1}{\SNR}\right) \nonumber\\
	&\relby{2}{\approx} \frac{2.5}{\ln10}\cdot\frac{1}{\SNR},
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $\log_{10}y=\ln y/\ln10$.
	\item $\ln(1+y)\approx y$ for $y\ll1$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\delta m\approx\frac{1.0857}{\SNR}.}
	\label{eq:linear_mag_error}
\end{equation}
The factor $2.5/\ln10=1.0857$ is the one that turned an optical depth into magnitudes of extinction in Chapter~\ref{ch:photometry} (Eq.~\eqref{ph:eq:extinction_tau}). Both times a natural logarithm is turned into magnitudes. A signal-to-noise ratio of $10$ gives an error of about $0.1$ magnitude, and $100$ gives $0.01$.

The error is not symmetric. A signal low by one standard deviation makes the star look fainter by $-2.5\log_{10}(1-1/\SNR)$, which is larger. At $\SNR=3$ the two shifts are $0.31$ and $0.44$ magnitude, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:linear_mag_error} gives $0.36$, between them. At $\SNR=10$ they are $0.103$ and $0.114$, and the linear value $0.109$ differs from each by about $5$ per cent. Figure~\ref{fig:dmag_comparison} compares the brighter-side shift with the linear form. Below $\SNR\approx5$ the linear form is a poor guide, and both sides should be given. Table~\ref{tab:snr_benchmarks} lists what typical signal-to-noise ratios mean in practice.

\subsection{Detection and the False-Alarm Probability}
\label{sec:detection_significance}

At a signal-to-noise ratio of $2$ to $5$ the question is whether there is a source at all.

\paragraph{The noise is Gaussian.} A large Poisson count has the Gaussian shape of Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:gauss_limit}, and the readout noise is Gaussian from the start. The total noise is a sum of these independent parts. A sum of independent Gaussian quantities is again Gaussian, and by the central limit theorem a sum of many independent parts of any kind is close to Gaussian. So the measured excess, when no source is present, scatters about zero with a Gaussian distribution of standard deviation $\sigma_{\rm tot}$. Measuring the excess in units of $\sigma_{\rm tot}$, as $k\sigma_{\rm tot}$, turns every case into the same standard Gaussian.

\paragraph{Probability of a false alarm.} With no source present, the probability that the noise alone rises by at least $k\sigma_{\rm tot}$ is the area of the standard Gaussian beyond $k$,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{P_{\rm tail}(k) = \int_{k}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-x^2/2}\,dx.}
	\label{eq:false_alarm_single}
\end{equation}
The integral has no closed form, but for large $k$ it is easy to estimate.
\begin{align}
	\int_k^\infty e^{-x^2/2}\,dx
	&\eqby{1} \int_k^\infty \frac1x\;d\!\left(-e^{-x^2/2}\right) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \frac{e^{-k^2/2}}{k}-\int_k^\infty\frac{e^{-x^2/2}}{x^2}\,dx \nonumber\\
	&\relby{3}{\approx} \frac{e^{-k^2/2}}{k}.
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $x\,e^{-x^2/2}\,dx=d\big(-e^{-x^2/2}\big)$.
	\item Integration by parts. The boundary term at infinity is zero.
	\item The remaining integral is smaller than the first term by a factor of order $1/k^2$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	P_{\rm tail}(k)\approx\frac{e^{-k^2/2}}{k\sqrt{2\pi}} \qquad (k\gg1).
	\label{cc:eq:tail_approx}
\end{equation}
At $k=5$ this gives $2.97\times10^{-7}$, against the exact $2.87\times10^{-7}$. The factor $e^{-k^2/2}$ is why the probability falls so steeply. Going from $5\sigma$ to $6\sigma$ multiplies it by about $e^{-5.5}\times5/6$, or $1/294$, against the exact $1/290$. Table~\ref{cc:tab:false_alarm} gives exact values, and Figure~\ref{fig:gaussian_tail_prob} plots them.

\subsection{Searching a Whole Image}

The probability $P_{\rm tail}(k)$ applies to one place chosen in advance, such as the position of a known star. A search for new objects, such as supernovae or asteroids, tests every place in the image at once. Let the image hold $n_{\rm trial}$ independent places, each of which could hold a source. The probability that at least one of them shows a false excess of $k\sigma$ is
\begin{align}
	P_{\rm any}(k)
	&\eqby{1} 1-\big[1-P_{\rm tail}(k)\big]^{n_{\rm trial}} \nonumber\\
	&\relby{2}{\approx} n_{\rm trial}\,P_{\rm tail}(k).
	\label{eq:union_bound}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The places are independent, so the probability that none of them exceeds $k\sigma$ is the product of $n_{\rm trial}$ equal factors.
	\item $(1-y)^{n}\approx1-ny$ when $ny\ll1$.
\end{explain}
The product $n_{\rm trial}P_{\rm tail}(k)$ is also exactly the mean number of false alarms in the image, whether or not it is small. When it is small it is the chance of even one.

Take a $4000\times4000$ CCD and count every pixel as a place, $n_{\rm trial}=1.6\times10^7$. Neighbouring pixels share the light of one star, so the true number of independent places is smaller, and this count gives an upper estimate.
\begin{itemize}
	\item At $k=3$ the mean number of false alarms is $1.6\times10^7\times1.35\times10^{-3}\approx2.2\times10^4$. The real objects would be lost among them.
	\item At $k=5$ it is $1.6\times10^7\times2.87\times10^{-7}\approx4.6$ in the whole image.
	\item At $k=5.5$ it is about $0.3$.
\end{itemize}
One false alarm per image is expected at $k=5.3$. This is why large surveys require $5\sigma$ or more before they claim a new object.

\section{Noise Budgets Beyond the CCD}
\label{cc:sec:pulsar}

The variance sum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:variance_addition} used nothing particular to a CCD. Any measurement made of independent parts has a budget of the same kind, and the part that dominates decides what to improve. Timing the radio pulses of a pulsar is a second example. The measured quantity is the arrival time of a pulse instead of a number of electrons, and its total error is again a sum of independent variances.
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Radiometer noise.} The noise of the receiver and its electronics, like the read and dark noise of a CCD.
	\item \textbf{Sky noise.} Radio emission from everything else in the beam, the radio form of $\sigma_{\rm sky}$.
	\item \textbf{Source noise.} Changes in the pulsar itself, with no CCD counterpart. Single pulses differ in shape (\emph{pulse jitter}), and the spin of the neutron star wanders slightly (\emph{spin noise}).
	\item \textbf{Dispersion noise.} Free electrons in the ionised gas between the stars (Chapter~\ref{ch:ism}) delay a pulse by an amount that depends on the radio frequency. The column of electrons along the line of sight changes as the pulsar and the gas move, so the delay changes too. A CCD image has nothing like it, because the atmosphere and the dust only dim and scatter its light (Section~\ref{sec:windows}).
	\item \textbf{Radio-frequency interference.} Man-made radio signals from television, mobile phones, radar and power lines, which must be found and cut out, much as cosmic-ray hits are removed from a CCD frame.
\end{itemize}
As for a CCD, the term that dominates a given observation decides how it should be planned and reduced.

\subsection{Problem 1: Normalisation, Moments, and the Gaussian Limit of the Poisson Distribution}
\label{cc:prob:poisson}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: A count $n$ has the Poisson distribution $P(n)=\bar n^{\,n}e^{-\bar n}/n!$ for $n=0,1,2,\dots$
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item Prove that it is normalised, $\sum_{n=0}^\infty P(n)=1$.
	\item Prove that its mean and its variance are both equal to $\bar n$.
	\item Plot the distribution for $\bar n=0.1$, $1$, $10$, $100$ and $1000$, and show that it approaches a Gaussian when $\bar n$ is large.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The exponential series, $\sum_{n=0}^\infty \bar n^{\,n}/n!=e^{\bar n}$, does every sum.
	\item The mean and variance are derived in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:poisson_mean} and~\eqref{eq:poisson_var}. A second route differentiates the exponential series with respect to $\bar n$.
	\item For $\bar n\gg1$, Stirling's formula turns $P(n)$ into a Gaussian of mean $\bar n$ and variance $\bar n$, Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:gauss_limit}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Normalisation}.
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{n=0}^\infty P(n)
		&\eqby{1} e^{-\bar n}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{\bar n^{\,n}}{n!} \\
		&\eqby{2} e^{-\bar n}\,e^{\bar n}
	\end{align*}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $e^{-\bar n}$ does not depend on $n$.
		\item The exponential series.
	\end{explain}
	\[
	\boxed{\sum_{n=0}^\infty P(n) = 1.}
	\]

	\item \textbf{Mean and variance}. The direct sums are Eqs.~\eqref{eq:poisson_mean} and~\eqref{eq:poisson_var}. As a second route, write $\bar n\,\dv{}{\bar n}$ for the operator that multiplies the $n$-th term of the series by $n$, since $\bar n\,\dv{}{\bar n}\bar n^{\,n}=n\,\bar n^{\,n}$. Applying it once and twice to the exponential series,
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{n=0}^\infty n\,\frac{\bar n^{\,n}}{n!}
		&\eqby{1} \bar n\,\dv{}{\bar n}\,e^{\bar n} = \bar n\,e^{\bar n}, \\
		\sum_{n=0}^\infty n^2\,\frac{\bar n^{\,n}}{n!}
		&\eqby{2} \bar n\,\dv{}{\bar n}\left(\bar n\,e^{\bar n}\right) = \bar n\,(1+\bar n)\,e^{\bar n}.
	\end{align*}
	\begin{explain}
		\item Differentiate the series term by term and multiply by $\bar n$.
		\item Do it again to the first line.
	\end{explain}
	Multiplying by $e^{-\bar n}$ gives $\avg n=\bar n$ and $\avg{n^2}=\bar n+\bar n^2$. Then
	\[
	\boxed{\avg n=\bar n,\qquad \sigma^2=\avg{n^2}-\avg n^2=\bar n.}
	\]

	\item \textbf{Plot and the Gaussian limit}. The distribution can be drawn directly from its definition.
	\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import poisson, norm

lambdas = [0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000]
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, len(lambdas), figsize=(18, 3.2))

for ax, lam in zip(axes, lambdas):
    k_max = int(lam + 6*np.sqrt(lam) + 5)
    k = np.arange(0, k_max + 1)
    ax.bar(k, poisson.pmf(k, lam), color="C0", width=1.0, label="Poisson")
    if lam >= 10:
        x = np.linspace(0, k_max, 400)
        ax.plot(x, norm.pdf(x, loc=lam, scale=np.sqrt(lam)), "C3--", label="Gaussian")
    ax.set_title(rf"$\lambda={lam}$")
    ax.set_xlabel("$k$")
axes[0].set_ylabel("$P[X=k]$")
axes[-1].legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
	\end{lstlisting}
	(In the code, \texttt{lam} is $\bar n$ and \texttt{k} is $n$.) Figure~\ref{cc:fig:poisson_gauss} shows the five distributions with the Gaussian of the same mean and variance, Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:gauss_limit}.

	\begin{figure}[htbp]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/fig_ch7_poisson_gauss.pdf}
		\caption{Poisson probabilities $P(n)$ for $\bar n=0.1$, $1$, $10$, $100$ and $1000$ (bars, or dots at every second and every sixth $n$ in the last two panels), with the Gaussian of mean $\bar n$ and variance $\bar n$ (dashed) for $\bar n\ge1$.}
		\label{cc:fig:poisson_gauss}
	\end{figure}

	For $\bar n=0.1$ the count is $0$ with probability $e^{-0.1}=0.905$ and $1$ with probability $0.1\,e^{-0.1}=0.090$, and larger counts almost never happen. No bell curve fits this. For $\bar n=1$ the counts $0$ and $1$ are equally likely, and the Gaussian puts probability at negative $n$, which cannot occur. For $\bar n=10$ the shape is close to a bell but lopsided, with a longer tail to the right. For $\bar n=100$ and $1000$ the points lie on the Gaussian. The peak height checks this, since Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:gauss_limit} gives $1/\sqrt{2\pi\bar n}=0.012616$ at $\bar n=1000$, against the Poisson value $0.012615$. The lopsidedness comes from the dropped term of order $(n-\bar n)^3/\bar n^2$, which is $1/\sqrt{\bar n}$ at one standard deviation, so
	\[
	\boxed{P(n)\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\bar n}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(n-\bar n)^2}{2\bar n}\right]\quad\text{for }\bar n\gg1.}
	\]
	This is why the noise of large counts may be treated as Gaussian, as in Section~\ref{sec:detection_significance}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 2: Signal-to-Noise with Exposure Time and Adding Images}
\label{cc:prob:coadd}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Two images $I_1$ and $I_2$ of the same bright object are taken with the same instrument. Both are dominated by the shot noise of the source. $I_1$ has an exposure time of $1\,\mathrm{min}$ and $\SNR=100$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item What is the $\SNR$ of $I_2$ if its exposure time is $5\,\mathrm{min}$?
	\item If the two images are added pixel by pixel, what is the $\SNR$ of the sum?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item With the source's own shot noise dominant, $\SNR=\sqrt{R_{\rm src}t}\propto\sqrt t$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:regime_bright}.
	\item Variances of independent images add, Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:var_add}, and the variance of a Poisson count equals its mean, Eq.~\eqref{eq:poisson_var}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Longer exposure}. Only the time differs between the two images.
	\begin{align*}
		\SNR_2
		&\eqby{1} \sqrt{R_{\rm src}t_2} \\
		&\eqby{2} \SNR_1\sqrt{\frac{t_2}{t_1}} = 100\sqrt5
	\end{align*}
	\begin{explain}
		\item Eq.~\eqref{eq:regime_bright}.
		\item Divide by $\SNR_1=\sqrt{R_{\rm src}t_1}$.
	\end{explain}
	\[
	\boxed{\SNR_2 = 223.6.}
	\]

	\item \textbf{Sum of the two images}. The signals add, $S_{1+2}=S_1+S_2=R_{\rm src}(t_1+t_2)$. The two images are independent, so their variances add, and each variance equals the signal.
	\begin{align*}
		\SNR_{1+2}
		&\eqby{1} \frac{S_1+S_2}{\sqrt{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}} \\
		&\eqby{2} \frac{S_1+S_2}{\sqrt{S_1+S_2}} = \sqrt{R_{\rm src}(t_1+t_2)} \\
		&\eqby{3} \SNR_1\sqrt{1+\frac{t_2}{t_1}} = 100\sqrt6
	\end{align*}
	\begin{explain}
		\item Eq.~\eqref{eq:snr_def}, with the variance of the sum from Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:var_add}.
		\item $\sigma_i^2=S_i$ for a Poisson count, Eq.~\eqref{eq:poisson_var}.
		\item Divide by $\SNR_1=\sqrt{R_{\rm src}t_1}$.
	\end{explain}
	\[
	\boxed{\SNR_{1+2} = 244.9.}
	\]
	As a check by counting electrons, $\SNR_1=100$ means $S_1=100^2=10^4$ electrons, so $S_2=5\times10^4$ and the sum has $6\times10^4$ electrons, with $\sqrt{6\times10^4}=244.9$. Adding the images gives the same result as one exposure of $6\,\mathrm{min}$. That stops being exactly true when readout noise matters, because the sum has been read twice and the single exposure only once.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 3: A Faint Star Twice as Far Away}
\label{cc:prob:distance}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: The image of a faint star has $\SNR=5$, and its noise is dominated by instrumental noise.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item What would the $\SNR$ be if the star were twice as far away, with everything else unchanged?
	\item What would it be if the noise had instead been dominated by the star's own shot noise?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Instrumental noise, the dark and readout terms, does not depend on the star, so $\SNR\propto R_{\rm src}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:regime_read}.
	\item With the star's own shot noise dominant, $\SNR\propto\sqrt{R_{\rm src}}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:regime_bright}.
	\item The flux, and so $R_{\rm src}$, falls as $1/d^2$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:inverse_square_flux}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Instrumental noise}. The variance $n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark}t+n_{\rm pix}N_{\rm exp}\sigma_{\rm RON}^2$ contains no light from the star, so it is the same at either distance. Only the signal changes.
	\begin{align*}
		\frac{\SNR(2d)}{\SNR(d)}
		&\eqby{1} \frac{R_{\rm src}(2d)}{R_{\rm src}(d)} \\
		&\eqby{2} \left(\frac{d}{2d}\right)^2 = \frac14
	\end{align*}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\SNR=R_{\rm src}t/\sigma_{\rm tot}$ with $t$ and $\sigma_{\rm tot}$ unchanged.
		\item $R_{\rm src}\propto F\propto1/d^2$.
	\end{explain}
	\[
	\boxed{\SNR(2d)=\frac54=1.25.}
	\]
	The star is no longer detected. An excess of $1.25\sigma$ is produced by noise alone about once in ten trials.

	\item \textbf{Shot-noise limit}. Now $\SNR=\sqrt{R_{\rm src}t}\propto\sqrt{R_{\rm src}}\propto1/d$, so
	\[
	\boxed{\SNR(2d)=\frac52=2.5.}
	\]
	A star limited by its own light loses less when it is moved away, because its noise falls with its signal. The same light loss costs a faint star on a bright sky or on a noisy detector twice as much.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 4: Dark Current and Readout Noise from Two Dark Frames}
\label{cc:prob:dark}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Two dark frames are taken with the same telescope and CCD. The first has an exposure time of $1\,\mathrm{min}$ and a noise variance $a$. The second has an exposure time of $3\,\mathrm{min}$ and a noise variance $2.6a$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item Find the noise variance of the dark current per unit time.
	\item Find the noise variance of the readout noise.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item A dark frame has no star and no sky. Its variance is $\sigma^2(t)=n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark}t+n_{\rm pix}\sigma_{\rm RON}^2$, a straight line in $t$. For a single pixel, $n_{\rm pix}=1$.
	\item Two frames of different lengths give two equations for the two unknowns, solved in Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:dark_solution}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}: The two frames give
\[
\begin{pmatrix} 1\,\mathrm{min} & 1\\ 3\,\mathrm{min} & 1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark}\\ n_{\rm pix}\sigma_{\rm RON}^2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix} a\\ 2.6a\end{pmatrix}.
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Dark variance per unit time}. By Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:dark_solution},
	\[
	\boxed{n_{\rm pix}R_{\rm dark}=\frac{\sigma_2^2-\sigma_1^2}{t_2-t_1}=\frac{2.6a-a}{3\,\mathrm{min}-1\,\mathrm{min}}=0.8a\ \text{per minute}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Read variance}. By Eq.~\eqref{cc:eq:dark_solution},
	\[
	\boxed{n_{\rm pix}\sigma_{\rm RON}^2=\frac{t_2\sigma_1^2-t_1\sigma_2^2}{t_2-t_1}=\frac{3a-2.6a}{2}=0.2a.}
	\]
	As a check, the first frame gives $0.8a\times1+0.2a=a$. In the $1$-minute frame the dark current gives $80$ per cent of the variance, and in the $3$-minute frame $92$ per cent. The read variance is the same $0.2a$ in every frame, whatever its length. A $5$-minute frame should show $0.8a\times5+0.2a=4.2a$, and taking one is how the straight line of Figure~\ref{cc:fig:dark_frames} would be tested.
\end{enumerate}
