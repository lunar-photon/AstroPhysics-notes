\chapter{Self-Gravitating Gas}
\label{ch:selfgrav}

A cold cloud of molecular gas in the Galaxy is tens of parsecs across and about $10\,\mathrm K$ (Table~\ref{tab:ism_cloud_types}). Some such clouds sit unchanged for millions of years. Others break up into dense cores that shrink, heat up and become stars. Three things decide which. Gravity pulls every part of the cloud toward every other part. Pressure pushes back. Rotation, small at first, resists being squeezed sideways.

Pressure is not one thing. In a star it is a sum,
\[
P=P_{\rm gas}+P_{\rm light}+P_{\rm degenerate}+\cdots,
\]
and each term is one case of counting states (P3, Section~\ref{pr:sec:p3}). Gas pressure holds up the Sun over most of its interior. Light carries a growing share in more massive stars (Problem~\ref{pr:prob:prad}). Degenerate electrons hold up white dwarfs. In a planet the pressure comes instead from compressed liquid and solid, each with its own equation of state.

Two questions run through what follows. When does gravity win, and why does a body held together by gravity get hotter when it loses energy?

\section{Hydrostatic Equilibrium}
\label{sg:sec:hse}

Chapter~\ref{ch:principles} gave the Euler equation for any moving gas (P1, Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:euler}) and the pull of a sphere on its own parts (P2, Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:shell_theorem}). A star that shines for billions of years is not speeding up or slowing down anywhere inside. The full Euler equation carries terms for motion that such a star does not need.

Think of a pile of books. The bottom book carries the weight of every book above it. The one in the middle carries half the pile. A diver feels the same thing in water. Every ten metres of depth adds the weight of ten metres of water above each square metre, about one atmosphere. Inside a star each layer holds up all the layers above it, so the pressure must grow toward the centre. At every radius the pressure difference across a thin layer balances the weight of that layer. This is momentum conservation (P1) for gas at rest, with the gravity of a sphere (P2).

The diver in numbers. Water has $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $g=9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, so the pressure grows by $\rho g=9800\,\mathrm{Pa}$ per metre, and one atmosphere, $1.01\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$, every $10.3\,\mathrm m$. The picture is a thin slab of gas with more pressure pushing up on its bottom face than down on its top face. The difference is exactly its weight. The same balance sets how fast the air thins with height, how thick a disk of gas around a star is, and how much mass a galaxy cluster must have to hold its hot gas (Problem~\ref{sg:prob:cluster}). It gives the pressure gradient at every radius of a star.

\paragraph{The equation.} Set $\vec v=0$ in the Euler equation \eqref{pr:eq:euler} and use the gravity of a sphere \eqref{pr:eq:shell_theorem}. Only the radial component survives,
\begin{align}
	0
	&\eqby{1} -\nabla P+\rho\,\vec g \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} -\dv{P}{r}\,\rhat-\rho\,\frac{G\,m(r)}{r^2}\,\rhat .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:euler} with $\vec v=0$ everywhere and at all times.
	\item Spherical symmetry, so $P$ depends only on $r$, and $\vec g$ from Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:shell_theorem}.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\dv{P}{r}=-\frac{G\,m(r)\,\rho}{r^2}.}
	\label{sg:eq:hse}
\end{equation}
This is \textbf{hydrostatic equilibrium}. It holds for any star at rest, whatever its equation of state and whatever carries its energy. It does not say what $P$ is made of.

\paragraph{How large is the central pressure?} Replace the derivative by the drop of the pressure from the centre to the surface, $-P_c/R$, and put the whole mass at a typical radius $R$. Then $P_c\sim GM^2/R^4$. For the Sun this is $1.1\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{Pa}$. The detailed models of Chapter~\ref{ch:structure} give $2.5\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{Pa}$, larger because the Sun's mass is concentrated toward its centre. The estimate gets the size right to within a factor of a few without any knowledge of what the Sun is made of.

\paragraph{An atmosphere.} Near the surface of a planet, $m(r)$ and $r$ hardly change, so $Gm/r^2$ is the constant surface gravity $g$. If the air is an ideal gas at one temperature $T$, with mean mass $\mu m_{\rm H}$ per particle, Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:ideal_gas} gives $\rho=P\,\mu m_{\rm H}/(k_BT)$. With height $z$ in place of $r$,
\begin{align}
	\dv{P}{z}
	&\eqby{1} -\rho g \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} -\frac{\mu m_{\rm H}\,g}{k_BT}\,P ,
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:hse} with $Gm/r^2=g$ held constant.
	\item The ideal-gas law solved for $\rho$.
\end{explain}
so the pressure falls exponentially,
\begin{equation}
	P(z)=P(0)\,e^{-z/H},\qquad H=\frac{k_BT}{\mu m_{\rm H}\,g}.
	\label{sg:eq:scale_height}
\end{equation}
The \textbf{scale height} $H$ is the height over which the pressure falls by a factor $e$. For the Earth's air ($T=288\,\mathrm K$, mean molecular mass $28.97$ atomic mass units) it is $8.4\,\mathrm{km}$. A hotter or lighter gas reaches higher.

\paragraph{Planets, and why their interiors are layered.} A planet obeys Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:hse} just as a star does, but its pressure comes from rock, ice and liquid rather than from hot gas and light. Gravity does not change these materials directly. It compresses them. Going down, the pressure grows as Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:hse} demands, and usually the temperature grows with it, so each depth sits at a different point $(P,T)$ of the material's phase diagram. Where that point crosses a phase boundary, the stable form of the material changes. Rock turns into denser mineral structures, and in the giant planets hydrogen is squeezed into a dense, metallic state. A layered interior is hydrostatic equilibrium plus a phase diagram.

\section{The Virial Theorem}
\label{sg:sec:virial}

Hydrostatic equilibrium gives the pressure at every radius, but only once the density at every radius is known. That needs the equation of state and the way energy flows, which is the work of Chapter~\ref{ch:structure}. Often the profile is not wanted at all. The questions are simpler. How hot is a star, on average? How much energy does it hold? What happens to it when it loses some?

Think of a satellite in a circular orbit. The lower its orbit, the faster it moves. A satellite that skims the air feels a little drag, loses energy, and sinks to a lower orbit, where it moves faster. Losing energy has sped it up. The station at $400\,\mathrm{km}$ moves at $7.67\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Dropped to $300\,\mathrm{km}$ it would move at $7.73\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The particles of a star are like satellites in the star's own gravity. Averaged over the whole star, their kinetic energy is tied to the depth of the gravitational well in the same way. Since the kinetic energy of a gas measures its temperature, the depth of the well sets the temperature. This is the virial theorem. It is hydrostatic equilibrium (P1 and P2) added up over the whole star, read with P3.

The satellite in symbols. Gravity supplies the centripetal force, $m v^2/r=GMm/r^2$. So the kinetic energy is $K=\tfrac12mv^2=GMm/(2r)$ and the potential energy is $U=-GMm/r$. Then $2K+U=0$, and the total energy is $E=K+U=-K$, the result $E=-GM\mu/2a$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:visviva_E} for a circular orbit. Lowering $E$ raises $K$. The picture is a bowl. The deeper the bowl, the faster everything in it has to move to stay in it. The same theorem is Clausius's virial of a gas. Zwicky applied it to the galaxies of the Coma cluster and found that they move far too fast for the mass that shines, the first evidence of dark matter. It turns the speeds of stars into the mass of a galaxy, and the temperature of the hot gas in a cluster into the cluster's mass. Here it gives the theorem by three routes, for a gas sphere at rest, for a moving gas and for any set of point masses, then the temperature of a star, and how firmly a star is bound.

\paragraph{Route A, from hydrostatic equilibrium.} Multiply Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:hse} by $4\pi r^3$ and integrate from the centre to the surface $R$. On the left,
\begin{align}
	\int_0^R4\pi r^3\,\dv{P}{r}\,dr
	&\eqby{1} \Big[4\pi r^3P\Big]_0^R-3\int_0^R4\pi r^2P\,dr \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} -3\int P\,dV .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Integration by parts.
	\item At the centre $r^3=0$, and at the surface $P=0$. The volume element is $dV=4\pi r^2dr$.
\end{explain}
On the right,
\begin{align}
	\int_0^R4\pi r^3\left(-\frac{Gm\rho}{r^2}\right)dr
	&\eqby{1} -\int_0^M\frac{Gm}{r}\,dm \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} U .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $4\pi r^2\rho\,dr=dm$, Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:mass_inside}.
	\item The gravitational energy, Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:U_def}.
\end{explain}
Setting the two equal,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{3\int P\,dV+U=0.}
	\label{sg:eq:virial_P}
\end{equation}
Only gravity and the pressure enter, so this holds for any equation of state. It is hydrostatic equilibrium weighted by $r$ and summed over the star. The detail at each radius is lost, and the overall balance is kept.

\paragraph{Route B, for a gas that moves.} Route A assumed the gas is at rest. To see what happens when it is not, follow the second moment of the mass, $I\equiv\int r^2\,dm$, where each $dm$ moves with the gas. Differentiating twice,
\begin{align}
	\frac12\dv[2]{I}{t}
	&\eqby{1} \int v^2\,dm+\int\vecr\cdot\dv{\vec v}{t}\,dm \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} 2K_{\rm bulk}+\int\vecr\cdot\big(-\nabla P+\rho\,\vec g\big)\,dV \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} 2K_{\rm bulk}+3\int P\,dV+U .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $\dv*{(r^2)}{t}=2\vecr\cdot\vec v$, then once more. The mass elements do not change (P1).
	\item $K_{\rm bulk}=\tfrac12\int v^2\,dm$ is the kinetic energy of bulk motion. The Euler equation \eqref{pr:eq:euler} gives $\rho\,\dv*{\vec v}{t}$.
	\item $-\int\vecr\cdot\nabla P\,dV=3\int P\,dV$ by integration by parts, as in Route A. For a sphere, $\vecr\cdot\vec g=-Gm/r$ by Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:shell_theorem}, so the gravity term is $U$ as in Route A. (For any shape the same result follows by writing $U$ as a sum over pairs, Route C.)
\end{explain}
For a gas of slow particles, $\gamma=\tfrac53$ in Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:P_u}, so $\int P\,dV=\tfrac23K_{\rm th}$, where $K_{\rm th}$ is the kinetic energy of the random thermal motion. With the total kinetic energy $K=K_{\rm bulk}+K_{\rm th}$,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\frac12\dv[2]{I}{t}=2K+U.}
	\label{sg:eq:virial_dynamic}
\end{equation}
A cloud that neither grows nor shrinks on average has $\avg{\ddot I}=0$, and $2K+U=0$, which is Route A again. If $2K<|U|$, then $\ddot I<0$. The cloud's size accelerates inward and it collapses. If $2K>|U|$, it disperses.

\paragraph{Route C, for point masses over a long time.} The stars of a cluster and the galaxies of a group are separate bodies, not a fluid, and their system need not be round or steady. Take $N$ point masses $m_i$ at $\vecr_i$ with velocities $\vec v_i$. Let $\vecf_{ij}$ be the force on body $i$ from body $j$. Forces come in equal and opposite pairs (P1), $\vecf_{ji}=-\vecf_{ij}$, and for gravity
\[
\vecf_{ij}=-\frac{Gm_im_j\,(\vecr_i-\vecr_j)}{|\vecr_i-\vecr_j|^3}.
\]
The second moment of the mass is now the sum $I=\sum_im_ir_i^2$. Differentiating it twice,
\begin{align}
	\frac12\dv{I}{t}
	&\eqby{1} \sum_im_i\,\vecr_i\cdot\vec v_i, \nonumber\\
	\frac12\dv[2]{I}{t}
	&\eqby{2} \sum_im_iv_i^2+\sum_i\vecr_i\cdot m_i\dv{\vec v_i}{t} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} 2K+\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}\vecr_i\cdot\vecf_{ij} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{4} 2K+\frac12\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}(\vecr_i-\vecr_j)\cdot\vecf_{ij} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{5} 2K-\frac12\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{Gm_im_j}{|\vecr_i-\vecr_j|}
	\eqby{6} 2K+U .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $\dv*{(r_i^2)}{t}=2\vecr_i\cdot\vec v_i$.
	\item Differentiate the product once more.
	\item $K=\tfrac12\sum_im_iv_i^2$ is the kinetic energy of all the motion. Newton's second law, $m_i\,\dv*{\vec v_i}{t}=\sum_{j\ne i}\vecf_{ij}$.
	\item Renaming $i\leftrightarrow j$ does not change the double sum, and with $\vecf_{ji}=-\vecf_{ij}$ it turns $\vecr_i\cdot\vecf_{ij}$ into $-\vecr_j\cdot\vecf_{ij}$. The sum is the average of its two forms.
	\item The gravitational force above, with $(\vecr_i-\vecr_j)\cdot(\vecr_i-\vecr_j)=|\vecr_i-\vecr_j|^2$.
	\item The double sum counts each pair twice, and the $\tfrac12$ counts it once. The energy of point masses is $U=-\sum_{\rm pairs}Gm_im_j/|\vecr_i-\vecr_j|$, the sum form of Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:U_def}.
\end{explain}
This is Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:virial_dynamic} again, now for any shape. Instead of asking for balance at every moment, average it over a long time $t$,
\begin{align}
	\avg{\frac12\dv[2]{I}{t}}
	&\eqby{1} \frac1t\int_0^t\frac12\dv[2]{I}{{t'}}\,dt'
	\eqby{2} \frac1t\left[\sum_im_i\,\vecr_i\cdot\vec v_i\right]_0^t
	\relby{3}{\longrightarrow} 0 .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The time average.
	\item The integral of a derivative, with the first line of the chain above.
	\item In a bound system the bodies stay in a finite region with finite speeds, so the bracket stays finite. Divided by $t$ it goes to zero as $t\to\infty$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{2\avg{K}+\avg{U}=0.}
	\label{sg:eq:virial_avg}
\end{equation}
No fluid, no sphere and no balance at any instant was assumed. The bodies may swing in and out, as long as they stay bound, and their averages still obey the balance of a star at rest. This is the form used to weigh star clusters and clusters of galaxies (Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxies}).

\paragraph{Reading it, gravity sets the temperature.} Let the gas be ideal with mean mass $\mu m_{\rm H}$ per particle, where $\mu$ is the \textbf{mean molecular weight}, so that the number density is $n=\rho/(\mu m_{\rm H})$. Then
\[
\int P\,dV=\int nk_BT\,dV=\frac{k_B}{\mu m_{\rm H}}\int T\,dm\equiv\frac{Mk_B\bar T}{\mu m_{\rm H}},
\]
which defines $\bar T$, the temperature averaged over the mass. With Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:virial_P},
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{k_B\bar T=\frac{\mu m_{\rm H}\,|U|}{3M}.}
	\label{sg:eq:virial_T}
\end{equation}
The average temperature of a star is set by the depth of its gravitational well per unit mass, and by nothing else. The source of its energy does not appear.

\paragraph{A uniform sphere.} To put numbers in, take a sphere of uniform density. The mass inside $r$ is $m=M(r/R)^3$, so $dm=3Mr^2dr/R^3$, and Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:U_def} gives
\begin{align}
	U
	&\eqby{1} -\int_0^R\frac{G\,M(r/R)^3}{r}\,\frac{3Mr^2}{R^3}\,dr \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} -\frac{3GM^2}{R^6}\int_0^Rr^4\,dr \nonumber\\
	&= -\frac35\,\frac{GM^2}{R} .
	\label{sg:eq:U_uniform}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:U_def} with $m=M(r/R)^3$ and $dm=3Mr^2dr/R^3$.
	\item Collect the powers of $r$ and $R$.
\end{explain}
Then Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:virial_T} becomes $k_B\bar T=G M\mu m_{\rm H}/(5R)$. For the Sun, with $\mu=0.6$ for its ionised gas, this is $\bar T=2.8\times10^6\,\mathrm K$. The real Sun is denser toward the middle, so its well is deeper and its gas hotter. Its centre is at $1.57\times10^7\,\mathrm K$. Without knowing anything about nuclear fusion, the virial theorem says that the inside of the Sun is at millions of kelvin. That is hot enough for hydrogen nuclei to fuse (Section~\ref{st:sec:eos}), which is why stars shine by fusion at all.

\paragraph{How firmly a star is bound.} Let the gas have $P=(\gamma-1)u$, Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:P_u}. Its internal energy is $\int u\,dV=\int P\,dV/(\gamma-1)$, and the total energy is
\begin{align}
	E
	&\eqby{1} \frac{1}{\gamma-1}\int P\,dV+U \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} -\frac{U}{3(\gamma-1)}+U \nonumber\\
	&= \frac{3\gamma-4}{3(\gamma-1)}\,U .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Internal energy plus gravitational energy, for a star at rest.
	\item The virial theorem \eqref{sg:eq:virial_P}, $\int P\,dV=-U/3$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{E=\frac{3\gamma-4}{3(\gamma-1)}\,U.}
	\label{sg:eq:E_gamma}
\end{equation}
For slow particles, $\gamma=\tfrac53$ and $E=U/2<0$. The star is bound, and taking it apart costs half its gravitational energy. As $\gamma\to\tfrac43$, $E\to0$. The star is barely bound, and a small push can make it collapse or fly apart. For $\gamma<\tfrac43$ no static balance exists at all. This single line explains three later results. Stars held up mainly by light are fragile (Section~\ref{st:sec:polytropes}). White dwarfs whose electrons move near the speed of light have a maximum mass (Section~\ref{cs:sec:wd}). And a collapsing protostar collapses a second time while its molecules are being broken apart (Section~\ref{sg:sec:rotation}).

\section{Negative Heat Capacity}
\label{sg:sec:negheat}

By the virial theorem a star of slow particles has $E=-K$, Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:E_gamma} with $\gamma=\tfrac53$. Everyday thermodynamics says that something that loses heat gets colder. Its heat capacity, the energy needed to raise its temperature by one kelvin, is positive. A star seems to break this rule.

Think of a cup of tea left in a room. It cools until it has the temperature of the room, and then its energy wobbles a little around a fixed value as the room's air gives and takes small amounts of heat. The room decides the temperature. The rule that heat capacity is positive is a statement about objects that sit in a room like this, with a large surrounding body that sets the temperature. A star has no room around it. It is isolated in empty space, held together by its own gravity, and it loses energy only by radiating into space, which never sends heat back. Nothing sets its temperature from outside. Its temperature follows from its energy through the virial theorem, and that relation runs backwards. The proof that heat capacity is positive does not apply to it.

The Sun in numbers. With $\mu=0.6$ the Sun holds $N=M/(\mu m_{\rm H})=2.0\times10^{57}$ particles, and $E=-K=-\tfrac32Nk_B\bar T$. If it shone at its present luminosity for a million years with no fusion, it would lose $\Delta E=-1.2\times10^{40}\,\mathrm J$, and its mean temperature would rise by $3\times10^5\,\mathrm K$. The picture is a curve of temperature against energy that slopes the wrong way. Moving left along it, toward lower energy, the temperature climbs. The same behaviour appears in the core collapse of globular star clusters and in black holes, whose temperature rises as they lose mass to Hawking radiation. It gives the sign of the heat capacity of a star, the reason the usual rule fails, and what a negative heat capacity does for a star.

\paragraph{The heat capacity of a star.} Along a sequence of stars in virial balance, each with a slightly different energy, $E=-\tfrac32Nk_B\bar T$. So
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{C\equiv\dv{E}{\bar T}=-\frac32Nk_B<0.}
	\label{sg:eq:negative_C}
\end{equation}
Losing energy by radiation, $dE<0$, raises the temperature, $d\bar T=dE/C>0$. Of the gravitational energy released by contraction, half goes into heating the gas and half is radiated away, since $\Delta K=-\Delta U/2$ and $\Delta E=\Delta U/2$.

\paragraph{From one equilibrium to the next.} A ball of gas in balance has its energy fixed by its size. For a uniform sphere of slow particles, Eqs.~\eqref{sg:eq:E_gamma}, \eqref{sg:eq:U_uniform} and~\eqref{sg:eq:virial_T} give
\[
E=\frac{U}{2}=-\frac{3GM^2}{10R},\qquad k_B\bar T=\frac{\mu m_{\rm H}\,GM}{5R}.
\]
Both depend on $R$ alone. A smaller ball in balance has a lower energy and a higher temperature. So the equilibria of a given mass form a ladder. Each rung is a ball in balance, and each lower rung is smaller, hotter and more tightly bound. To step down from one rung to the next, from $R_1$ to a smaller $R_2$, the gas must change its energy by
\begin{align}
	\Delta E
	&\eqby{1} \tfrac12\,\Delta U<0, \nonumber\\
	\Delta K
	&\eqby{2} \Delta E-\Delta U
	\eqby{3} -\tfrac12\,\Delta U=-\Delta E>0.
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $E=U/2$ on both rungs, and $U$ is lower on the smaller one.
	\item $E=K+U$ on both rungs.
	\item The first line.
\end{explain}
The energy $-\Delta E=\tfrac12|\Delta U|$ has to leave the cloud. Nothing can take it away except light, since the cloud is isolated. Exactly as much stays behind as heat. So the light that leaves and the rise in temperature are two sides of one step. The light pays for the step, and because the heat capacity is negative, paying raises the temperature. A star that shines on gravity alone, $\dv*{E}{t}=-L$, heats at the rate
\[
\dv{\bar T}{t}=-\frac{L}{C}=\frac{2L}{3Nk_B}>0,
\]
the $3\times10^5\,\mathrm K$ per million years of the Sun above.

The physical way down the ladder is contraction, and a real cloud takes it as follows.
\begin{itemize}
	\item A molecular cloud starts near a rung, close to balance, held up by its thermal pressure, by turbulence, whose random gas motions push like an extra pressure, and by magnetic fields threaded through the gas.
	\item When gravity wins, by the Jeans criterion of Section~\ref{sg:sec:jeans}, the cloud leaves its rung and contracts, and its gravitational energy falls, $\Delta U<0$.
	\item The released energy goes first into the infall itself, and as the gas is squeezed, into heat. The warm gas radiates in the lines of its molecules and atoms and from its dust. In a thin cloud this light escapes.
	\item If the cooling keeps up with the compression, the heat leaves as fast as it is made. The pressure never builds up, the temperature stays near $10\,\mathrm K$, and the cloud does not stop on any rung. It falls freely (Section~\ref{sg:sec:freefall}) and breaks into pieces (Section~\ref{sg:sec:jeans}). During the fall it is not in balance at all.
	\item The fall ends when the centre becomes opaque to its own cooling light. The heat now stays in, the pressure rises, and the gas settles on a new rung, the first hydrostatic core of Section~\ref{sg:sec:rotation}. It has a lower energy and a higher temperature than the cloud it came from, and the difference has left as light.
	\item From then on the steps are slow. The core, and later the protostar, is in balance at every moment and moves down the ladder on the Kelvin–Helmholtz time (Section~\ref{sg:sec:freefall}), radiating and heating, until its centre is hot enough to burn hydrogen (Section~\ref{st:sec:eos}).
\end{itemize}
The collapse is the way a cloud moves from one equilibrium to another of lower energy. Radiation carries off the energy of each step, and the negative heat capacity turns that loss into a rise of temperature.

\paragraph{Why the usual rule says $C\ge0$.} The usual rule belongs to the canonical ensemble, a system in contact with a heat bath at temperature $T$. With $\beta=1/k_BT$ its partition function is $Z(\beta)=\sum_se^{-\beta E_s}$, summed over its states $s$. The mean energy and its spread follow from $Z$,
\begin{align}
	\avg{E}
	&\eqby{1} -\pdv{\ln Z}{\beta}, \nonumber\\
	\avg{(\Delta E)^2}=\avg{E^2}-\avg{E}^2
	&\eqby{2} \pdv[2]{\ln Z}{\beta}
	\eqby{3} -\pdv{\avg{E}}{\beta}
	\eqby{4} k_BT^2\,\pdv{\avg{E}}{T}.
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Differentiate $\ln Z$ once. Each state enters with weight $e^{-\beta E_s}/Z$.
	\item Differentiate once more. The second derivative of $\ln Z$ is the mean of $E^2$ minus the square of the mean of $E$.
	\item The first line.
	\item $\beta=1/k_BT$, so $\partial/\partial\beta=-k_BT^2\,\partial/\partial T$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{C_{\rm can}=\frac{\avg{(\Delta E)^2}}{k_BT^2}\ge0.}
	\label{sg:eq:canonical_C}
\end{equation}
A spread in energy cannot be negative, so in contact with a bath the heat capacity cannot be negative either. This is a statement about the canonical ensemble only.

\paragraph{The isolated system.} An isolated star has a fixed energy and belongs to the microcanonical ensemble, where the temperature is defined from the entropy $S(E)$ by $1/T=\dv*{S}{E}$. Comparing neighbouring equilibria with slightly different $E$,
\begin{align}
	\dv{E}\left(\frac1T\right)
	&\eqby{1} \dv[2]{S}{E} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad -\frac{1}{T^2}\,\dv{T}{E}
	&= \dv[2]{S}{E} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad C_{\rm micro}=\dv{E}{T}
	&\eqby{2} -\frac{1}{T^2\,\dv*[2]{S}{E}} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Differentiate the definition of $T$ once more.
	\item Invert $\dv*{T}{E}$.
\end{explain}
Nothing here forbids $C<0$. It only requires $\dv*[2]{S}{E}>0$, an entropy that curves upward. For short-range forces, a large system can be split into parts whose energies add, and then the two ensembles agree and $S(E)$ curves downward. Gravity is long-range. The energy of a star is not the sum of the energies of its halves, because the halves attract each other. So the two ensembles need not agree, and a self-gravitating body can have $C<0$ as an isolated system even though no system with $C<0$ can sit in steady contact with a bath.

That last point is itself physical. Put a star in a bath slightly hotter than itself. Heat flows in, and with $C<0$ the star cools, so even more heat flows in. Put it in a slightly cooler bath and it heats as it loses heat. Either way it runs away from the bath's temperature. A self-gravitating body cannot come into steady equilibrium with a heat bath.

\paragraph{What it does for a star.} Suppose the nuclear burning in a core runs a little too fast. The extra energy expands the core, and by Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:negative_C} the core cools, which slows the burning. If the burning runs too slow, the core contracts and heats until the burning catches up. Negative heat capacity makes a star its own thermostat (P4 on the energy, Section~\ref{pr:sec:p4}). When fuel runs out in the core, the same rule makes the core contract and heat until the next fuel ignites. Where the pressure stops depending on temperature, in degenerate matter, the thermostat breaks, and burning can run away (Section~\ref{st:sec:eos}).

\section{The Jeans Criterion}
\label{sg:sec:jeans}

The virial theorem says when a whole cloud is in balance, $2K+U=0$, and that it collapses when $2K<|U|$. It does not say which parts of a large cloud collapse, or how big the pieces are.

Think of traffic on a busy road. Now and then cars bunch up a little. Drivers in a bunch slow down, the cars behind catch up, and the bunch grows. Drivers also react and spread out again. A short bunch is smoothed out before it can grow. A long one grows faster than drivers can spread it, and becomes a jam. Only bunches longer than some length grow. A cloud behaves the same way. A region that is slightly denser than its surroundings pulls in more gas. Its higher pressure pushes back, and that push travels at the speed of sound. A small region is smoothed out by sound before gravity can act. A large region collapses before sound can cross it. The dividing size, where the sound-crossing time equals the free-fall time, is the Jeans length. This is a comparison of two scales (P6), made exact with conservation (P1) and the gravity of the gas (P2).

A dense core in numbers. Molecular gas at $10\,\mathrm K$ with $n=10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ ($10^4\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$) and $\mu=2.33$ has the sound speed $c_s=\sqrt{k_BT/(\mu m_{\rm H})}=188\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and the free-fall time $t_{\rm ff}=3.4\times10^5$ years (Section~\ref{sg:sec:freefall}). In that time sound covers $c_st_{\rm ff}=0.065\,\mathrm{pc}$. Regions much larger than this cannot be held up. The picture is Figure~\ref{sg:fig:jeans}. Short waves oscillate as ordinary sound. Long waves do not oscillate at all. They grow.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch10_jeans_dispersion.pdf}
	\caption{The squared frequency $\omega^2$ of a small wave in a uniform self-gravitating gas against its wavenumber $k$, in units of $4\pi G\rho_0$ and of $k_J$. Below $k=k_J$ (shaded) $\omega^2<0$.}
	\label{sg:fig:jeans}
\end{figure}

The same instability, with the expansion of the universe added, grows the galaxies and clusters out of the nearly smooth early universe. In a rotating disk it becomes the Toomre criterion for spiral arms and clumps. It gives the critical mass and length, by energy and by waves.

\paragraph{By energy.} Take a uniform sphere of mass $M$, radius $R$ and temperature $T$. Its thermal energy is $K=\tfrac32Nk_BT=\tfrac32Mk_BT/(\mu m_{\rm H})$ (Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:ideal_gas}), and its gravitational energy is Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:U_uniform}. The marginal case of Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:virial_dynamic}, $2K=|U|$, gives
\begin{align}
	3\,\frac{Mk_BT}{\mu m_{\rm H}}
	&= \frac35\,\frac{GM^2}{R} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad R
	&= \frac{G M\mu m_{\rm H}}{5k_BT} .
\end{align}
Writing $M=\tfrac43\pi R^3\rho$ and eliminating $R$ or $M$ gives the \textbf{Jeans mass} and \textbf{Jeans length},
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{M_J=\left(\frac{5k_BT}{G\mu m_{\rm H}}\right)^{3/2}\left(\frac{3}{4\pi\rho}\right)^{1/2},\qquad
	R_J=\left(\frac{15\,k_BT}{4\pi G\mu m_{\rm H}\rho}\right)^{1/2}.}
	\label{sg:eq:jeans_mass}
\end{equation}
A cloud with $M>M_J$ cannot be held up by its thermal pressure. For the dense core above, $M_J=5.4\,M_\odot$ and $R_J=0.13\,\mathrm{pc}$. Hot, thin gas is stable. Cold, dense gas is not, which is why stars form in cold molecular clouds rather than in the warm diffuse gas.

\paragraph{By waves.} Take an infinite gas at rest with uniform density $\rho_0$ and one temperature, so that a small change of density $\rho_1$ changes the pressure by $c_s^2\rho_1$. Disturb it slightly, $\rho=\rho_0+\rho_1$, $\vec v=\vec v_1$, $\Phi=\Phi_0+\Phi_1$. To first order in the small quantities, continuity \eqref{pr:eq:continuity}, Euler \eqref{pr:eq:euler} and Poisson \eqref{pr:eq:poisson} read
\begin{equation}
	\pdv{\rho_1}{t}+\rho_0\nabla\cdot\vec v_1=0,\qquad
	\rho_0\pdv{\vec v_1}{t}=-c_s^2\nabla\rho_1-\rho_0\nabla\Phi_1,\qquad
	\nabla^2\Phi_1=4\pi G\rho_1 .
\end{equation}
(Strictly, a uniform $\rho_0$ cannot be at rest, because Poisson's equation then needs a $\Phi_0$ that grows without bound. Jeans set this aside, and the result is still right for regions much smaller than the cloud.) For a wave $\propto e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ along $x$, with $\vec v_1=v_1\hat x$, each derivative becomes a factor, $\partial_t\to-i\omega$ and $\partial_x\to ik$. The three equations become one matrix equation for the three amplitudes,
\begin{equation}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		-i\omega & i\rho_0k & 0\\
		ic_s^2k & -i\omega\rho_0 & i\rho_0k\\
		4\pi G & 0 & k^2
	\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{pmatrix}\rho_1\\ v_1\\ \Phi_1\end{pmatrix}=0 .
	\label{sg:eq:jeans_matrix}
\end{equation}
A wave exists only if the determinant vanishes,
\begin{align}
	0
	&\eqby{1} -i\omega\big(-i\omega\rho_0k^2\big)-i\rho_0k\big(ic_s^2k^3-i\rho_0k\cdot4\pi G\big) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \rho_0k^2\big(c_s^2k^2-4\pi G\rho_0-\omega^2\big).
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Expand along the first row.
	\item Multiply out, using $i^2=-1$, and collect.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\omega^2=c_s^2k^2-4\pi G\rho_0.}
	\label{sg:eq:jeans_dispersion}
\end{equation}
Short waves, with large $k$, have $\omega^2>0$ and oscillate as sound. Long waves with $k$ below $k_J=\sqrt{4\pi G\rho_0}/c_s$ have $\omega^2<0$, so $\omega$ is imaginary and the disturbance grows exponentially instead of oscillating. The critical wavelength is
\begin{equation}
	\lambda_J=\frac{2\pi}{k_J}=c_s\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{G\rho_0}} .
	\label{sg:eq:jeans_wavelength}
\end{equation}
It scales with $T$ and $\rho$ exactly as $R_J$ does, and differs only in the number in front, because one route treats a finite sphere and the other an infinite medium. Both say $\lambda_J\approx c_s\,t_{\rm ff}$. With $t_{\rm ff}$ from Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:tff} the ratio is $\sqrt{32/3}=3.3$.

\paragraph{Why stars form in groups.} A giant molecular cloud holds $10^5$ to $10^6\,M_\odot$ (Table~\ref{tab:ism_cloud_types}), many thousands of Jeans masses. It does not collapse into one star. While it collapses it stays thin enough to radiate away the heat of compression, so its temperature stays near $10\,\mathrm K$. By Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:jeans_mass}, $M_J\propto T^{3/2}\rho^{-1/2}$ then falls as the density rises, so parts that were stable become unstable on their own. The cloud breaks into clumps, the clumps into cores, and each core into stars. This is why most stars are born in clusters and in pairs, although many clusters later drift apart.

\section{Free Fall and the Three Clocks}
\label{sg:sec:freefall}

The Jeans criterion says when a region collapses. It does not say how fast.

Imagine a hole drilled straight through a uniform Earth. A ball dropped into it reaches the centre after $21$ minutes, whatever the hole's length. The time depends only on the Earth's density, as the units of $G\rho$ demand (Section~\ref{pr:sec:p6}). A cloud whose pressure can no longer resist gravity falls in on itself in the same way. Each shell falls toward the centre pulled by the mass inside it. The time to reach the centre depends only on the density, so every shell of a uniform cloud arrives at the same moment. This is conservation of energy (P1) for each shell, with the gravity of a sphere (P2).

A uniform cloud with the Earth's mean density would collapse in $15$ minutes. The Sun's mean density gives $30$ minutes. A dense core of a molecular cloud takes $3.4\times10^5$ years. The picture is Figure~\ref{sg:fig:freefall}. The radius falls slowly at first and then plunges to zero.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch10_freefall.pdf}
	\caption{The radius of a shell in a uniform, pressure-free sphere that starts at rest, against time in units of the free-fall time.}
	\label{sg:fig:freefall}
\end{figure}

The same collapse is a Kepler orbit with no angular momentum (Chapter~\ref{ch:twobody}). In cosmology it is the collapse of a uniform overdense sphere into a galaxy halo, and in a massive star it sets how fast the core falls in at the start of a supernova. It gives the free-fall time and the three clocks of a star.

\paragraph{The fall of one shell.} Take a uniform cloud of density $\rho_0$ at rest, with no pressure. Follow a shell that starts at radius $R_0$, with mass $M=\tfrac43\pi R_0^3\rho_0$ inside it. While the shells do not cross, $M$ stays the same, and by Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:shell_theorem}
\begin{equation}
	\dv[2]{R}{t}=-\frac{GM}{R^2}.
\end{equation}
Multiplying by $\dv*{R}{t}$ and integrating from rest at $R_0$ gives the energy of the shell per unit mass,
\begin{align}
	\frac12\left(\dv{R}{t}\right)^2-\frac{GM}{R}
	&= -\frac{GM}{R_0} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \left(\dv{R}{t}\right)^2
	&= \frac{2GM}{R_0}\left(\frac{R_0}{R}-1\right).
\end{align}
The substitution $R=R_0\cos^2\xi$, with $\xi$ running from $0$ at the start to $\pi/2$ at the centre, turns the bracket into $\tan^2\xi$,
\begin{align}
	\Big(-2R_0\cos\xi\sin\xi\,\dv{\xi}{t}\Big)^2
	&\eqby{1} \frac{2GM}{R_0}\,\frac{\sin^2\xi}{\cos^2\xi} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \cos^2\xi\,\dv{\xi}{t}
	&\eqby{2} \sqrt{\frac{GM}{2R_0^3}} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \xi+\sin\xi\cos\xi
	&\eqby{3} \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R_0^3}}\;t .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $\dv*{R}{t}=-2R_0\cos\xi\sin\xi\,\dv*{\xi}{t}$, and $R_0/R-1=1/\cos^2\xi-1=\tan^2\xi$.
	\item Divide both sides by $4R_0^2\sin^2\xi$ and take the positive root, since $\xi$ grows.
	\item $\int_0^\xi2\cos^2\xi'\,d\xi'=\xi+\sin\xi\cos\xi$, with $\xi=0$ at $t=0$.
\end{explain}
The shell reaches the centre at $\xi=\pi/2$. With $M=\tfrac43\pi R_0^3\rho_0$, $\sqrt{2GM/R_0^3}=\sqrt{8\pi G\rho_0/3}$, and
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{t_{\rm ff}=\frac{\pi/2}{\sqrt{8\pi G\rho_0/3}}=\sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32\,G\rho_0}}.}
	\label{sg:eq:tff}
\end{equation}
$R_0$ has dropped out. Every shell of a uniform cloud reaches the centre at the same time, so shells never cross, which is what the calculation assumed. The time is $(G\rho_0)^{-1/2}$ times a number, as P6 said it must be.

\paragraph{Homologous collapse.} More is true. The last line of the chain ties $\xi$ to $t$ through $\sqrt{8\pi G\rho_0/3}$ alone, the same for every shell. So $R/R_0=\cos^2\xi$ is the same function of time for every shell. A shell that started at half the cloud's radius stays at half its radius at every moment. The cloud keeps its shape and only shrinks in scale. This is called \textbf{homologous collapse}, the same word as the homology of stars in Section~\ref{st:sec:scaling}, one shape rescaled. Two things follow. Every shell keeps its mass while every volume shrinks by $\cos^6\xi$, so the density stays uniform, $\rho=\rho_0/\cos^6\xi$. And the speed of a shell, $\dv*{R}{t}=R_0\,\dv*{(\cos^2\xi)}{t}$, is proportional to its radius at each moment, as in a uniform shrinking. A real core is denser at its centre. With $t_{\rm ff}\propto\rho^{-1/2}$ its inner gas arrives first and its outer gas later, so its collapse is not homologous. A small dense centre forms while most of the gas is still falling in. That centre becomes the first core and then the protostar (Section~\ref{sg:sec:rotation}), and the rest of the gas reaches it later (Section~\ref{sg:sec:protostar}).

\paragraph{The same result as a Kepler orbit.} A shell falling from rest is a body on a Kepler orbit of zero angular momentum. As the angular momentum shrinks to zero at fixed energy, the ellipse of Chapter~\ref{ch:twobody} flattens into a straight segment from $R_0$ to the centre, with semi-major axis $R_0/2$. The fall from one end to the other is half an orbital period. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:kepler3},
\[
t_{\rm ff}=\frac12\cdot2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(R_0/2)^3}{GM}}=\pi\sqrt{\frac{R_0^3}{8GM}}=\sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32\,G\rho_0}},
\]
the same as Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:tff}.

\paragraph{The three clocks of a star.} A star changes on three very different times.
\begin{itemize}
	\item The \textbf{free-fall} or dynamical time, Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:tff}. It is how fast the star restores hydrostatic equilibrium after a disturbance. For the Sun's mean density, $1410\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, it is $30$ minutes.
	\item The \textbf{Kelvin–Helmholtz} time, $t_{\rm KH}=|U|/L$. It is how long the star could shine on its gravitational energy alone, by contracting (Section~\ref{sg:sec:negheat}). With $|U|\sim GM^2/R$, the Sun gives $t_{\rm KH}\approx3\times10^7$ years.
	\item The \textbf{nuclear} time. Fusing hydrogen into helium releases $0.7\%$ of the rest mass, and about a tenth of the Sun's hydrogen is burned on the main sequence, so $t_{\rm nuc}\approx0.007\times0.1\times Mc^2/L\approx10^{10}$ years.
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{sg:fig:clocks} places them on one axis. Each is separated from the next by many orders of magnitude.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch10_clocks.pdf}
	\caption{The free-fall, Kelvin–Helmholtz and nuclear times of the Sun on a logarithmic axis, with a day, a year and a million years for comparison.}
	\label{sg:fig:clocks}
\end{figure}

This ordering is a P6 statement, and it is why a star can be described as a sequence of hydrostatic models. A star restores its balance within an hour, while its heat content changes over tens of millions of years and its composition over billions. At every moment it is in hydrostatic equilibrium, to great accuracy, and it moves slowly from one equilibrium to the next as it loses energy or burns fuel. A collapsing cloud is different. During free fall, $t_{\rm ff}$ is the only clock, the gas is not in balance at any moment, and no equilibrium description holds.

\section{Rotation and the End of Collapse}
\label{sg:sec:rotation}

Free fall assumed a cloud with no pressure, no rotation, and gas that radiates away every bit of heat. None of these lasts. Real clouds turn a little, and as they become denser they become opaque to their own cooling radiation.

Think of a figure skater spinning with arms held out. When the skater pulls the arms in, the spin becomes much faster. Nothing pushed the skater. The angular momentum stayed the same while the size shrank. A collapsing cloud does the same (P1 for angular momentum, Section~\ref{pr:sec:p1}). Its spin grows as it shrinks, and the outward pull of rotation grows faster than gravity. Along the axis of rotation nothing resists, so the cloud keeps falling there. Across the axis the spin stops it. The cloud flattens into a disk.

In numbers, the ratio of the rotational pull to gravity grows as $1/R$ (below). A core that shrinks from $0.1\,\mathrm{pc}$ to the size of the Sun raises that ratio by $4\times10^6$. A rotation far too slow to notice at the start becomes the main obstacle at the end. The picture is a sphere that flattens into a spinning disk with a dense centre. The same spin-up gives the disks in which planets form, the accretion disks around black holes, the disks of spiral galaxies and the fast spin of young neutron stars. It gives the rotation ratio, the end of the collapse, and the three meanings of equilibrium.

\paragraph{The rotation ratio.} Let a parcel at radius $R$ circle the axis with speed $v_{\rm rot}$, so its angular momentum per unit mass is $Rv_{\rm rot}$, which is conserved. The ratio of the rotational pull to gravity is
\begin{align}
	\frac{v_{\rm rot}^2/R}{GM/R^2}
	&\eqby{1} \frac{v_{\rm rot}^2R}{GM} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \frac{(Rv_{\rm rot})^2}{GM}\,\frac1R .
	\label{sg:eq:rotation_ratio}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Rotational acceleration $v_{\rm rot}^2/R$ divided by the gravity of the mass $M$ inside.
	\item Group the conserved angular momentum per unit mass.
\end{explain}
With $Rv_{\rm rot}$ and $M$ fixed, the ratio grows as $1/R$. Shrinking $R$ by some factor raises the ratio by the same factor.

\paragraph{Where the spin comes from.} A cloud as a whole may hardly turn at all. Its gas is not still, though. Molecular clouds are turbulent. The widths of their lines show random motions of a few kilometres per second, far faster than the speed of sound in gas at $10\,\mathrm K$, about $0.2\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Turbulence makes an irregular field of velocities, with eddies and shears on every scale. Take any small region and measure the motion of its gas about the region's own centre of mass. Some of the gas goes round one way more than the other, so the region has a nonzero angular momentum, even where the cloud as a whole has none. When the cloud breaks into cores (Section~\ref{sg:sec:jeans}), each core keeps the angular momentum of the gas it gathered (P1). By the ratio above, its spin matters more and more as it shrinks, so each core flattens into its own disk, turning about its own axis. This is why young stars are so often found with disks around them, even in clouds that do not rotate as a whole.

\paragraph{The end of the isothermal collapse.} While the gas can radiate away the heat of compression, its temperature stays nearly constant and the collapse goes on. Once the centre becomes dense enough to be opaque to its own cooling radiation, the heat stays in. The pressure now rises faster than the density, close to an adiabat with $\gamma=\tfrac75$ for molecular hydrogen, above $\tfrac43$. By Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:E_gamma} a static balance is again possible. The centre stops falling and forms the \textbf{first hydrostatic core}, a few hundredths of a solar mass, which grows by catching the gas still falling in.

The core heats as it grows. Near $2000\,\mathrm K$ the hydrogen molecules start to break apart. Breaking them takes energy that would otherwise raise the pressure, so during this phase the gas behaves as if $\gamma<\tfrac43$. By Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:E_gamma} no static balance exists, and the core collapses a second time, until the molecules are gone and the gas, now atomic and soon ionised, can stop it again. That second core is the protostar.

\paragraph{Three kinds of equilibrium.} The word equilibrium has been used in three senses, and they differ.
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Hydrostatic equilibrium} is mechanical. The forces on each parcel balance, Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:hse}. A star is in it to great accuracy.
	\item \textbf{Global thermodynamic equilibrium} means no net flow of heat anywhere, and in Newtonian physics a single temperature throughout. A star is never in it. Its temperature falls from the centre outward, and that is why energy flows out. (In general relativity the condition is a little different, Section~\ref{cs:sec:relativistic}.)
	\item \textbf{Local thermodynamic equilibrium} means that each small parcel is close to equilibrium inside itself, with one temperature for its gas and its light, even though the temperature changes from parcel to parcel. This holds deep inside stars, and it is what lets the gas pressure and the light of a parcel be written with the formulas of P3. Chapters~\ref{ch:radiation} and~\ref{ch:structure} rely on it.
\end{itemize}
A star can be in excellent hydrostatic equilibrium while far from global thermodynamic equilibrium, and for most of its interior local thermodynamic equilibrium sits between the two.

\section{From Protostar to Star}
\label{sg:sec:protostar}

The collapse has left a protostar at the centre of a flattened, spinning core (Section~\ref{sg:sec:rotation}). The ladder of Section~\ref{sg:sec:negheat} says how a ball in balance then shrinks and heats toward hydrogen burning. At its birth the protostar holds only a small part of the core's mass. The rest is still outside, and it spins, so it cannot fall straight onto the star. The new star is also buried in the dust of its birth cloud. And something had to start the collapse in the first place.

Stir the water in a bath and pull the plug. The water does not drop straight into the hole. It circles round it, faster near the middle, and it drains only as fast as it loses its swirl, by rubbing against the bottom and against the slower water further out. Gas falling onto a protostar does the same. It settles into a disk in which each ring circles the star, and it reaches the star only by handing its angular momentum to gas further out or by throwing it away in a jet (P1). A star is built through a disk, and what is left of the disk becomes planets.

In numbers, gas that starts $0.05\,\mathrm{pc}$ from the centre of a core of one solar mass, moving sideways at $0.05\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, settles into a ring $300\,\mathrm{AU}$ from the star (below). To land on a protostar of three solar radii it must give up $99.3$ per cent of its angular momentum. The picture is Figure~\ref{sg:fig:protostar}. A small star sits in a flat disk inside a dusty envelope, and two jets leave along the axis through cavities they have cleared.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch10_protostar.pdf}
	\caption{Schematic side view of an embedded protostar, not to scale. The envelope is grey, the outflow cavities are light, the disk is dark, and the jet runs along the axis to a bow shock at each end. The curved arrows mark gas falling onto the disk.}
	\label{sg:fig:protostar}
\end{figure}

The same kind of disk feeds white dwarfs and black holes in binaries and the nuclei of active galaxies (Section~\ref{fp:sec:accretion}), and similar jets leave them. Building a star this way uses P1 for angular momentum and energy, P2 for the pull of the star, and P6 to compare the size of dust grains with the wavelength of light. It gives what starts a collapse, how fast a protostar grows and how it shines, where the infalling gas lands and how it gets to the star, the jets, how a young star clears its birth cloud, and how planets form in what is left.

\paragraph{What starts a collapse.} A cloud close to balance sits on a rung of the ladder. It collapses on its own once its mass exceeds the Jeans mass. A cloud just below the limit needs a push. At a fixed temperature $M_J\propto\rho^{-1/2}$ (Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:jeans_mass}), so squeezing the gas to a higher density lowers the Jeans mass. A cloud of a third of its Jeans mass becomes unstable once it is squeezed nine times denser. The squeeze comes from outside.
\begin{itemize}
	\item The blast wave of a supernova sweeps up the gas around it.
	\item A cloud that enters a spiral arm of the Galaxy (Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxies}) is squeezed as gas piles up in the arm.
	\item Two clouds collide.
	\item A hot, massive star ionises the gas around it (Chapter~\ref{ch:ism}). The ionised gas at $10^4\,\mathrm K$ is a thousand times hotter than the $10\,\mathrm K$ molecular gas next to it, so at a similar density it pushes thousands of times harder. The edge of the ionised gas, the ionisation front, pushes a shock ahead of it into the cold gas.
\end{itemize}
Each push is a shock, a sudden jump in density that moves through the gas.

\paragraph{How hard a shock squeezes.} In the frame that moves with the shock, gas of density $\rho_a$ flows in at speed $v_a$ and flows out, squeezed, with density $\rho_b$ and speed $v_b$. Cold molecular gas radiates the heat of the shock almost at once, so it leaves at its old temperature, and on both sides the pressure is $c_s^2\rho$. The mass and the momentum flowing in each second equal those flowing out (P1),
\[
\rho_av_a=\rho_bv_b,\qquad \rho_av_a^2+c_s^2\rho_a=\rho_bv_b^2+c_s^2\rho_b .
\]
Eliminating $v_b$,
\begin{align}
	c_s^2(\rho_b-\rho_a)
	&\eqby{1} \rho_av_a^2-\rho_bv_b^2 \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \rho_av_a\,(v_a-v_b) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \rho_av_a^2\,\frac{\rho_b-\rho_a}{\rho_b} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The momentum balance, rearranged.
	\item $\rho_bv_b=\rho_av_a$, from the mass balance.
	\item $v_b=v_a\rho_a/\rho_b$, from the mass balance again.
\end{explain}
A shock has $\rho_b\ne\rho_a$, so dividing by $\rho_b-\rho_a$ leaves $c_s^2\rho_b=\rho_av_a^2$,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\frac{\rho_b}{\rho_a}=\left(\frac{v_a}{c_s}\right)^2.}
	\label{sg:eq:shock_squeeze}
\end{equation}
The squeeze grows as the square of the shock's speed in units of the sound speed. In gas at $10\,\mathrm K$, with $c_s=0.19\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, a shock of $0.56\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ already squeezes the gas nine times, enough to tip the cloud above. A shock of $10\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ would squeeze it $2800$ times if nothing else resisted. Magnetic fields in the gas resist and lower this, but even weak shocks are strong triggers. Not every collapse needs one. A cloud that cools and condenses on its own crosses the Jeans limit without help.

\paragraph{How fast a protostar grows.} A core of about one Jeans mass falls in about one free-fall time, so mass reaches the centre at a rate $\dot M$ of about $M_J/t_{\rm ff}$. With $c_s^2=k_BT/(\mu m_{\rm H})$, Eqs.~\eqref{sg:eq:jeans_mass} and~\eqref{sg:eq:tff} give
\begin{align}
	\dot M\approx\frac{M_J}{t_{\rm ff}}
	&\eqby{1} \left(\frac{5c_s^2}{G}\right)^{3/2}\left(\frac{3}{4\pi\rho}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{32\,G\rho}{3\pi}\right)^{1/2} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} 5^{3/2}\,\frac{\sqrt8}{\pi}\,\frac{c_s^3}{G}
	= 10.1\,\frac{c_s^3}{G} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $M_J$ from Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:jeans_mass} with $k_BT/(\mu m_{\rm H})=c_s^2$, and $1/t_{\rm ff}$ from Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:tff}.
	\item The density cancels, $G^{-3/2}G^{1/2}=G^{-1}$, and $\tfrac{3}{4\pi}\cdot\tfrac{32}{3\pi}=\tfrac{8}{\pi^2}$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\dot M\approx10\,\frac{c_s^3}{G}.}
	\label{sg:eq:mdot}
\end{equation}
The density has dropped out. How fast a star grows depends only on the temperature of the gas it is made from. Gas at $10\,\mathrm K$ gives $1.6\times10^{-5}\,M_\odot$ per year. The number in front is rough. The outer gas starts less dense and arrives later than the mean free-fall time says, and detailed models of a collapsing core give a rate near $c_s^3/G=1.6\times10^{-6}\,M_\odot$ per year. At that rate a star of half a solar mass is assembled in $3\times10^5$ years, about one free-fall time of the core.

\paragraph{The light of a protostar.} Gas that falls from far away onto the surface of the star releases $GM/R$ per kilogram, so a protostar shines with the accretion luminosity $L=GM\dot M/R$ of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:accretion_L}. For $M=0.5\,M_\odot$, $R=3\,R_\odot$ and $\dot M=1.6\times10^{-6}\,M_\odot$ per year, $L=8\,L_\odot$. Not all of this light leaves from the star. Half of the energy of the falling gas is released in the disk, as orbital energy lost on the way in, and the other half where the gas meets the surface (Section~\ref{fp:sec:accretion}). To this the star adds the light of its own slow contraction down the ladder.

Its inside is set by the virial theorem. For ionised gas with $\mu=0.6$, Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:virial_T} with the uniform sphere gives
\[
\bar T=\frac{GM\mu m_{\rm H}}{5k_BR}=2.8\times10^6\,\mathrm K\,\frac{M/M_\odot}{R/R_\odot}.
\]
A young protostar of $0.1\,M_\odot$ and $5\,R_\odot$ is at $5.5\times10^4\,\mathrm K$ inside, and one of $1\,M_\odot$ and $2\,R_\odot$ at $1.4\times10^6\,\mathrm K$. The interior is at tens of thousands to about a million kelvin, below the several million kelvin at which hydrogen burns fast enough to stop the contraction (Section~\ref{st:sec:scaling}). For a while the burning of its small store of deuterium slows the contraction (Chapter~\ref{ch:structure}). The star itself has a surface at $2000$ to $4000\,\mathrm K$. By Wien's law \eqref{eq:wien_law}, a surface at $3000\,\mathrm K$ shines most at $0.97\,\mu\mathrm m$, in the near infrared, with some visible light.

\paragraph{Seen through dust.} The protostar sits inside its envelope, behind tens of magnitudes of visual extinction. Dust blocks the near infrared about ten times less than the visible (Section~\ref{is:sec:dust}). Behind $A_V=50$ the visible light is dimmed by $10^{-20}$ and is lost. At $2.2\,\mu\mathrm m$ the extinction is about $5$ magnitudes, and one per cent of the light gets through. The light the dust absorbs warms it to tens of kelvin, and the dust shines in turn. At $30\,\mathrm K$ its light peaks near $100\,\mu\mathrm m$, by the same Wien's law. So a protostar is seen in the infrared, by its own near-infrared light through the thinnest parts of the envelope and by the far-infrared glow of the warmed dust. The near-infrared camera of the James Webb Space Telescope imaged the protostar in the dark cloud L1527 in Taurus this way. Its light escapes through two cavities, above and below a disk seen edge on.

\paragraph{Where the infalling gas lands.} A parcel of gas keeps its angular momentum per unit mass $Rv_{\rm rot}$ as it falls. By Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:rotation_ratio} its rotational pull grows against gravity as $1/R$, and the fall across the axis stops where the two are equal. There the parcel circles the star. Setting the ratio to one at a radius $r_{\rm disk}$,
\begin{equation}
	\frac{(Rv_{\rm rot})^2}{GM}\,\frac{1}{r_{\rm disk}}=1
	\quad\implies\quad
	\boxed{r_{\rm disk}=\frac{(Rv_{\rm rot})^2}{GM}.}
	\label{sg:eq:r_disk}
\end{equation}
The landing radius grows as the square of the angular momentum the parcel carries, and it depends on nothing else but the mass it circles. For $R=0.05\,\mathrm{pc}$, $v_{\rm rot}=0.05\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and $M=M_\odot$, $r_{\rm disk}=300\,\mathrm{AU}$, three per cent of the starting radius. Gas from further in carries less angular momentum and lands closer, so the disk fills from the inside out. Disks around young stars are observed at tens to hundreds of AU.

\paragraph{A Keplerian disk.} When the disk holds much less mass than the star, each ring circles the star as a planet would. By the third law \eqref{eq:kepler3}, with the radius $r$ of the ring in place of $a$, the ring turns at the angular rate $\sqrt{GM/r^3}$, moves at $\sqrt{GM/r}$ and carries the angular momentum $\sqrt{GMr}$ per unit mass. A disk whose rings obey this is called \textbf{Keplerian}, and the disks of protostars nearly are. The angular momentum per unit mass grows outward as $\sqrt r$. Gas at $300\,\mathrm{AU}$ that ends on the surface of a star of $3\,R_\odot$ keeps the fraction $\sqrt{3\,R_\odot/300\,\mathrm{AU}}=0.007$ of its angular momentum. The other $99.3$ per cent must go somewhere.

\paragraph{Why rings move in.} Take two rings of the disk, of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, at radii $r_1<r_2$. Friction between them trades angular momentum, but the total stays the same (P1). The friction turns orbital energy into heat, which is radiated, so the orbital energy can only fall. Each ring has the orbital energy $-GMm_1/(2r_1)$ or $-GMm_2/(2r_2)$ of a circular orbit, Eq.~\eqref{eq:visviva_E} with $a=r$. For small changes $dr_1$ and $dr_2$,
\begin{align}
	0=d\Big(m_1\sqrt{GMr_1}+m_2\sqrt{GMr_2}\Big)
	&\eqby{1} \sqrt{GM}\left(\frac{m_1\,dr_1}{2\sqrt{r_1}}+\frac{m_2\,dr_2}{2\sqrt{r_2}}\right)
	\quad\implies\quad m_2\,dr_2=-m_1\,dr_1\sqrt{\frac{r_2}{r_1}}, \nonumber\\
	dE=d\left(-\frac{GMm_1}{2r_1}-\frac{GMm_2}{2r_2}\right)
	&\eqby{2} \frac{GM}{2}\left(\frac{m_1\,dr_1}{r_1^2}+\frac{m_2\,dr_2}{r_2^2}\right) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \frac{GMm_1\,dr_1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{r_1^2}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_1}\,r_2^{3/2}}\right) \nonumber\\
	&= \frac{GMm_1\,dr_1}{2\sqrt{r_1}}\left(\frac{1}{r_1^{3/2}}-\frac{1}{r_2^{3/2}}\right).
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The total angular momentum is fixed. Differentiate each $\sqrt{r}$.
	\item Differentiate each $1/r$.
	\item Replace $m_2\,dr_2$ by the first line.
\end{explain}
The last bracket is positive, since $r_1<r_2$. So $dE<0$ needs $dr_1<0$, and then $dr_2>0$,
\[
\boxed{dE<0\quad\iff\quad\text{the inner ring moves in and the outer ring moves out.}}
\]
Friction lowers the energy by moving mass in and angular momentum out. A disk spreads. Most of its mass drains onto the star, and a small part moves out carrying the angular momentum. The bookkeeping for a steady disk, with the torque that one ring exerts on the next, is Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:torque}.

\paragraph{What does the rubbing.} The ordinary viscosity of the gas is far too weak. It would take longer than the age of the universe to drain a disk. Three things act much more strongly.
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Turbulence.} Eddies carry gas, and its momentum, between rings far further than single molecules do. Magnetic fields wound up by the shear of the disk keep the gas stirred.
	\item \textbf{Magnetic torques.} Field lines that join one ring to another, or the disk to the star or the envelope, pull like stretched bands and pass angular momentum along.
	\item \textbf{Tidal torques.} Spiral arms in a massive disk, a companion star, or planets forming in the disk pull on the gas and take angular momentum from it.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Jets and outflows.} Gas can also leave, and take angular momentum with it. Magnetic field lines anchored in the disk turn with it. Gas lifted off the disk slides out along them like a bead on a spinning wire, and it is made to turn with the disk until it is well away from it. Gas from a ring at $r$ that turns at the ring's rate $\sqrt{GM/r^3}$ out to a distance $r_{\rm lever}$ from the axis carries, per kilogram,
\[
\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r^3}}\;r_{\rm lever}^2=\sqrt{GMr}\left(\frac{r_{\rm lever}}{r}\right)^2,
\]
which is $(r_{\rm lever}/r)^2$ times the angular momentum it had in the ring. For $r_{\rm lever}=3r$ the factor is nine. So a jet that carries away a tenth of the infalling mass can carry away all the angular momentum that the rest must lose. This is why a disk that feeds a star also drives a jet. The gas leaves along the axis, where the disk and the envelope do not block it, in two opposite jets. Launched near the star, it leaves at about the escape speed from there, $\sqrt{2GM/R}=250\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ for $0.5\,M_\odot$ and $3\,R_\odot$. Slower, wider outflows of swept-up gas surround the jets. The infall is uneven, so the outflow comes in bursts, and the knots along a jet record them. Some young stars brighten a hundredfold within a year as their disk dumps gas onto them, and stay bright for decades. FU Orionis, in 1937, was the first seen.

\paragraph{Herbig--Haro objects.} Where a jet runs into the gas of the cloud, it drives a shock. The shock turns the energy of bulk motion into heat, $\tfrac32k_BT\approx\tfrac12\mu m_{\rm H}v^2$, so a shock of $50\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ in gas with $\mu=0.6$ heats it to about $6\times10^4\,\mathrm K$. The hot gas cools by shining in lines of hydrogen, oxygen and sulphur, and makes the glowing patches called \textbf{Herbig--Haro objects}. In HH~30, in the Taurus cloud $450$ light years away, the disk is seen edge on as a dark lane, with a jet leaving it on both sides.

\paragraph{Clearing the birth cloud.} The envelope goes in three ways. Accretion uses it up. Outflows sweep out the cavities along the axis and push gas away. Radiation pushes on the dust. Dust is far more opaque than free electrons. From the dust column of Section~\ref{is:sec:dust}, one magnitude of $A_V$ per $1.9\times10^{25}$ hydrogen atoms per square metre, with hydrogen $70$ per cent of the mass,
\[
\kappa\approx\frac{1}{1.086\times1.9\times10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-2}}\times m_{\rm H}/0.70}\approx20\,\mathrm{m^2\,kg^{-1}}
\]
in visible light, about $500$ times the opacity of free electrons. By Eq.~\eqref{rad:eq:eddington} the Eddington luminosity for dusty gas is then about $60\,L_\odot$ per solar mass. A young star of ten solar masses shines with thousands of $L_\odot$, mostly in visible and ultraviolet light, and its light blows the dusty envelope away. A young star of one solar mass shines with a few $L_\odot$, and its outflows do most of the clearing.

The disk survives this for three reasons. It is close to the star and tightly bound. A kilogram at $30\,\mathrm{AU}$ is bound by $GM/r$, about $340$ times more firmly than a kilogram of the envelope at $0.05\,\mathrm{pc}$. The outflows leave along the axis, away from the plane of the disk. And the disk is thin, so it catches little of the starlight, and it is opaque, so only its surface feels the push.

\paragraph{The names of young stars.} Strictly, a \textbf{protostar} is a young star still hidden in its envelope. Once the envelope is gone, the star is seen in visible light, but it is still contracting slowly down the ladder on the Kelvin--Helmholtz time (Section~\ref{sg:sec:freefall}). Such stars of low mass, up to about two solar masses, are \textbf{T Tauri stars}. Those of intermediate mass, about two to eight solar masses, are \textbf{Herbig Ae/Be stars}. Protostars and these stars together are \textbf{young stellar objects}. They vary in brightness. The accretion changes from week to week, spots on the surface turn with the star, clumps of dust in the disk pass in front of it and change its extinction, and some of them pulsate.

\paragraph{Planets form in what is left.} In the disk the dust grains collide and stick. They grow into pebbles, rocks and bodies kilometres across, and these gather into planets. In a massive disk a dense region can also collapse under its own gravity, the disk form of the Jeans criterion (Section~\ref{sg:sec:jeans}). A growing planet pulls on the gas near its orbit by its tide and pushes it aside, opening a gap. The ALMA image of the young star HL Tau, taken in the millimetre light of its dust, shows bright rings separated by dark gaps of this kind. The gas of a disk lasts a few million years, much less than the tens of millions of years a Sun-like star takes to contract to the main sequence. Giant planets must gather their gas in that time, so they form while the star is still contracting. The rocky planets grow in the same disk more slowly. The Earth took a few tens of millions of years to form, about as long as the Sun took to reach the main sequence. As the star nears the main sequence, its light and wind blow away the gas and fine dust that remain, and only the planets and the larger solid bodies are left.

\section{Problem Set: Self-Gravitating Gas}

\subsection{Problem 1: Scale Heights of Planetary Atmospheres}
\label{sg:prob:scale}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Find the scale height of (a) the Earth's atmosphere ($T=288\,\mathrm K$, mean molecular mass $28.97\,\mathrm u$, $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$) and (b) the atmosphere of Mars ($T=210\,\mathrm K$, mostly $\mathrm{CO_2}$ with mean molecular mass $43.3\,\mathrm u$, $g=3.71\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$). (c) Why is the scale height of Mars larger, although its air is heavier and colder?

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Hydrostatic equilibrium with an ideal gas at one temperature gives $H=k_BT/(\mu m_{\rm H}g)$, Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:scale_height}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Earth}. $H=(1.381\times10^{-23}\times288)/(28.97\times1.661\times10^{-27}\times9.81)\,\mathrm m$.
	\[
	\boxed{H_\oplus\simeq8.4\,\mathrm{km}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Mars}.
	\[
	\boxed{H_{\rm Mars}\simeq10.9\,\mathrm{km}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Comparison}. $H\propto T/(\mu g)$. The heavier, colder air of Mars lowers $H$ by a factor $(210/288)(28.97/43.3)=0.49$, but its weaker gravity raises it by $9.81/3.71=2.6$, which wins.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 2: The Temperature of a Galaxy Cluster}
\label{sg:prob:cluster}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: The galaxies of a rich cluster move with a line-of-sight velocity dispersion $\sigma=1000\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The cluster also holds hot ionised gas ($\mu=0.6$) in hydrostatic equilibrium in the same gravitational well. Estimate the temperature of that gas. In which band of light does it shine?

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The galaxies and the gas feel the same well, so by the virial theorem their particles have the same kinetic energy per unit mass on average.
	\item For galaxies, the kinetic energy per unit mass is $\tfrac32\sigma^2$ (three directions). For gas, it is $\tfrac32k_BT/(\mu m_{\rm H})$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
Setting the two equal, $k_BT=\mu m_{\rm H}\sigma^2$, so
\[
\boxed{T=\frac{0.6\times1.673\times10^{-27}\times(10^6)^2}{1.381\times10^{-23}}\,\mathrm K\simeq7\times10^7\,\mathrm K.}
\]
Gas at this temperature has $k_BT\approx6\,\mathrm{keV}$ and shines in X-rays, as clusters are observed to. Measuring this temperature and the density profile of the gas, and using Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:hse}, gives the mass of the cluster, which comes out far larger than the mass in stars and gas. This is the X-ray version of Zwicky's argument for dark matter.

\subsection{Problem 3: Jeans Mass of a Dense Core}
\label{sg:prob:jeans}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: For a molecular core with $\mu=2.33$, find the Jeans mass (a) at $10\,\mathrm K$ and $n=10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, (b) at $20\,\mathrm K$ and the same density, and (c) at $10\,\mathrm K$ and $n=10^{12}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. What does (c) say about a core that collapses while staying at $10\,\mathrm K$?

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:jeans_mass}, with $M_J\propto T^{3/2}\rho^{-1/2}$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Reference core}. $\rho=n\mu m_{\rm H}=3.9\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ in Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:jeans_mass}.
	\[
	\boxed{M_J\simeq5.4\,M_\odot.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Twice as warm}. $M_J$ grows by $2^{3/2}=2.8$.
	\[
	\boxed{M_J\simeq15\,M_\odot.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{A hundred times denser}. $M_J$ falls by $\sqrt{100}=10$.
	\[
	\boxed{M_J\simeq0.54\,M_\odot.}
	\]
	A core that collapses while staying cold has a Jeans mass that keeps falling, so its parts become unstable on their own and it fragments.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 4: The Age of the Sun Before Fusion Was Known}
\label{sg:prob:kh}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: In the nineteenth century, Kelvin and Helmholtz assumed the Sun shines by slowly contracting. Estimate how long it could have shone at its present luminosity $L_\odot=3.83\times10^{26}\,\mathrm W$. Compare with the age of the Earth from geology and radioactivity, about $4.5\times10^9$ years.

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $t_{\rm KH}=|U|/L$ with $|U|\sim GM^2/R$.
	\item By the virial theorem only half of the released energy is radiated, which changes only the factor in front.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\[
\boxed{t_{\rm KH}\sim\frac{GM_\odot^2}{R_\odot L_\odot}=\frac{6.674\times10^{-11}\times(1.989\times10^{30})^2}{6.957\times10^8\times3.83\times10^{26}}\,\mathrm s\simeq3\times10^7\ \text{years}.}
\]
This is about a hundred times shorter than the age of the Earth. Gravity alone cannot have powered the Sun for that long, which pointed to a much larger store of energy, later found in the nucleus.

\subsection{Problem 5: A Star That Heats as It Cools}
\label{sg:prob:negheat}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: A young star of one solar mass, made of ionised gas with $\mu=0.6$, contracts toward the main sequence. It shines with $L=L_\odot$ for $10^6$ years without any fusion. By how much does its mean temperature change? Does it rise or fall?

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The heat capacity of a star in virial balance is $C=-\tfrac32Nk_B$, Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:negative_C}.
	\item The energy lost is $L\times$ time.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
$N=M/(\mu m_{\rm H})=2.0\times10^{57}$, so $C=-4.1\times10^{34}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. The energy lost is $\Delta E=-3.83\times10^{26}\times3.16\times10^{13}\,\mathrm J=-1.2\times10^{40}\,\mathrm J$.
\[
\boxed{\Delta\bar T=\frac{\Delta E}{C}\simeq+3\times10^5\,\mathrm K.}
\]
The temperature rises. The star loses energy and gets hotter, and it keeps contracting and heating until its centre is hot enough for hydrogen to fuse.

\subsection{Problem 6: How a Protostar Grows}
\label{sg:prob:protostar}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: A core of molecular gas with $\mu=2.33$ collapses onto a protostar. (a) Estimate the accretion rate $c_s^3/G$ for gas at $10\,\mathrm K$ and at $20\,\mathrm K$. (b) A protostar of $0.3\,M_\odot$ and radius $2.5\,R_\odot$ grows at the rate for $20\,\mathrm K$. Find its accretion luminosity. (c) Gas starts $0.02\,\mathrm{pc}$ from the axis and moves sideways at $0.05\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Where does it settle around the $0.3\,M_\odot$ star?

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\dot M\propto c_s^3/G$, Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:mdot}, with $c_s=\sqrt{k_BT/(\mu m_{\rm H})}\propto T^{1/2}$.
	\item $L=GM\dot M/R$, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:accretion_L}.
	\item $r_{\rm disk}=(Rv_{\rm rot})^2/(GM)$, Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:r_disk}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Accretion rate}. At $10\,\mathrm K$, $c_s=188\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $c_s^3/G=1.0\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$. Twice the temperature gives $2^{3/2}=2.8$ times the rate.
	\[
	\boxed{\dot M\simeq1.6\times10^{-6}\ \text{and}\ 4.5\times10^{-6}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Luminosity}. $\dot M=2.8\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$, so $L=6.674\times10^{-11}\times0.3\times1.989\times10^{30}\times2.8\times10^{17}/(2.5\times6.957\times10^8)\,\mathrm W$.
	\[
	\boxed{L\simeq6.5\times10^{27}\,\mathrm W\simeq17\,L_\odot.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Landing radius}. $Rv_{\rm rot}=6.2\times10^{14}\,\mathrm m\times50\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}=3.1\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $GM=4.0\times10^{19}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$.
	\[
	\boxed{r_{\rm disk}\simeq2.4\times10^{13}\,\mathrm m\simeq160\,\mathrm{AU}.}
	\]
	The gas lands in a disk the size of the outer Solar System and must lose almost all of its angular momentum before it reaches the star.
\end{enumerate}
