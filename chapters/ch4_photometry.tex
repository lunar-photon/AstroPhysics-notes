\chapter{Stellar Photometry: Flux, Magnitudes, and Radii}

Chapter~3 used photometric distance indicators (spectroscopic parallax, standard candles) without yet justifying the underlying flux measurements themselves. Photometry is the quantitative measurement of electromagnetic flux received from celestial objects. From the measured radiant flux, astronomers deduce fundamental astrophysical properties of stars, including luminosity ($L$), effective surface temperature ($T_{\rm eff}$), radius ($R_*$), and distance ($d$). In this chapter, we develop the inverse-square law, Pogson's logarithmic magnitude system, blackbody radiation thermodynamics, and the determination of stellar physical dimensions.

\section{Radiant Energy, Flux, and the Inverse-Square Law}

\begin{definition}[Luminosity and Flux]
	The \emph{luminosity} $L$ of a star is the total energy radiated into space per unit time (power in Watts, $\mathrm{W}$) across all wavelengths. The \emph{bolometric radiant flux} $F$ (the radiant flux integrated across all wavelengths) is the total energy received per unit time per unit collecting area perpendicular to the line of sight:
	\begin{keyresult}
		\begin{equation}
			F = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2}\quad \left[\mathrm{W\cdot m^{-2}}\right],
			\label{eq:inverse_square_flux}
		\end{equation}
	\end{keyresult}
	where $d$ is the distance from the source to the observer.
\end{definition}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:inverse_square_flux} is the geometric \emph{inverse-square law of radiation}, arising from energy conservation as radiant power spreads uniformly over a concentric spherical surface of area $4\pi d^2$ in an isotropic, non-absorbing medium.

\begin{example}[Solar Constant]
	The Sun has luminosity $L_\odot = 3.828 \times 10^{26}\,\mathrm{W}$. At the mean Earth-Sun distance ($1\,\mathrm{AU} = 1.4959787 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}$), the total solar irradiance (the \emph{solar constant}) at the top of Earth's atmosphere is:
	\[
	F_\odot = \frac{3.828 \times 10^{26}}{4\pi (1.4959787 \times 10^{11})^2} \approx 1361\,\mathrm{W\cdot m^{-2}}.
	\]
\end{example}

\section{The Astronomical Magnitude System}

Human vision responds logarithmically to perceived optical brightness (the Weber-Fechner law). In the 2nd century BCE, Hipparchus cataloged stars into six discrete brightness classes ($1^{\rm st}$ magnitude for the brightest down to $6^{\rm th}$ magnitude for the faintest naked-eye stars).

\subsection{Apparent Magnitude and Pogson's Formula}

In 1856, Norman Pogson formalized this ancient scale mathematically after noticing that a difference of 5 magnitudes corresponds to a flux ratio of exactly $100:1$. Thus, a single magnitude step corresponds to a flux ratio of:
\begin{equation}
	\sqrt[5]{100} = 100^{1/5} = 10^{0.4} \approx 2.511886.
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Pogson's Formula for Apparent Magnitude]
	The difference in \emph{apparent magnitude} $m_1 - m_2$ between two sources with measured fluxes $F_1$ and $F_2$ is:
	\begin{keyresult}
		\begin{equation}
			m_1 - m_2 = -2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{F_1}{F_2}\right).
			\label{eq:pogson_difference}
		\end{equation}
	\end{keyresult}
	Inverting Pogson's relation:
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{F_1}{F_2} = 10^{-0.4(m_1 - m_2)}.
		\label{eq:flux_ratio_from_mag}
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}

On an absolute scale, apparent magnitude is defined relative to a standard zero-point flux $F_0$:
\begin{equation}
	m = -2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{F}{F_0}\right) = -2.5\log_{10}F + C_0.
	\label{eq:apparent_mag_single}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Absolute Magnitude and Distance Modulus}

Because apparent magnitude $m$ depends on both intrinsic luminosity $L$ and distance $d$, it cannot directly measure a star's true physical power.

\begin{definition}[Absolute Magnitude]
	The \emph{absolute magnitude} $M$ is defined as the apparent magnitude a star would possess if it were located at a standard fiducial distance of exactly $d_0 = 10\,\mathrm{pc}$.
\end{definition}

Applying Pogson's formula between a star's actual distance $d$ and the fiducial distance $10\,\mathrm{pc}$:
\[
m - M = -2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{F(d)}{F(10\,\mathrm{pc})}\right) = -2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{L/(4\pi d^2)}{L/(4\pi (10)^2)}\right) = -2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{10}{d}\right)^2 = 5\log_{10}\left(\frac{d}{10}\right).
\]
\begin{keyresult}
	\begin{equation}
		\mu \equiv m - M = 5\log_{10}\left(\frac{d}{10\,\mathrm{pc}}\right) = 5\log_{10}d\,[\mathrm{pc}] - 5.
		\label{eq:distance_modulus}
	\end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
The quantity $\mu = m - M$ is known as the \emph{distance modulus}. In terms of trigonometric parallax $p$ (in arcseconds):
\begin{equation}
	m - M = -5\log_{10}p - 5.
	\label{eq:distance_modulus_parallax}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Photometric Passbands, Color Indices, and Interstellar Extinction}

Astronomical detectors measure flux through calibrated filters (e.g., the Johnson-Cousins $UBVRI$ system): $U$ ($365\,\mathrm{nm}$), $B$ ($440\,\mathrm{nm}$), $V$ ($550\,\mathrm{nm}$), $R$ ($640\,\mathrm{nm}$), and $I$ ($790\,\mathrm{nm}$).

\begin{definition}[Color Index]
	A \emph{color index} is the magnitude difference between two passbands (such as $B - V$). Since flux appears in the denominator of Pogson's logarithmic ratio:
	\begin{equation}
		B - V = -2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{F_B}{F_V}\right) + C_{BV}.
		\label{eq:color_index_def}
	\end{equation}
	Hotter stars emit more blue flux ($F_B > F_V$), yielding smaller or negative color indices ($B - V < 0$), whereas cooler stars yield larger positive color indices ($B - V > 0$). Color index serves as an observational proxy for surface temperature $T_{\rm eff}$.
\end{definition}

\subsubsection{Interstellar Reddening and Extinction: The Mie Scattering Mechanism}

When starlight traverses the interstellar medium, dust grains scatter and absorb photons. While light scattering in physics spans multiple elastic and inelastic mechanisms (detailed in Section~\ref{sec:scattering_classification}), interstellar extinction is governed by \textbf{Mie scattering} off sub-micron dust grains ($a \sim 0.1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$).

In this regime, the scattering cross-section falls with wavelength ($\sigma_{\rm ext} \propto \lambda^{-1}$). Because blue light has a shorter wavelength than visual light ($\lambda_B \approx 440\,\mathrm{nm} < \lambda_V \approx 550\,\mathrm{nm}$), dust attenuates blue photons more heavily ($A_B > A_V$). This preferential blue loss shifts the observed color index toward redder values:
\begin{equation}
	(B - V)_{\rm obs} = (B - V)_0 + E_{B - V},
	\label{eq:color_index_observed_split}
\end{equation}
where $E_{B - V} \equiv A_B - A_V > 0$ is the \emph{color excess}. To determine the star's true temperature and intrinsic spectral type, astronomers subtract this excess via the \textbf{Color Index Correction}:
\begin{keyresult}
	\begin{equation}
		(B - V)_0 = (B - V)_{\rm obs} - E_{B-V}.
		\label{eq:color_index_correction}
	\end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

The total visual extinction is parameterized by $A_V = R_V E_{B-V}$, where $R_V \approx 3.1$ in the diffuse Milky Way. Removing $A_V$ from the apparent distance modulus yields the true dereddened distance modulus $\mu_0$:
\begin{keyresult}
	\begin{equation}
		\mu_0 \equiv (m_V - M_V)_{\rm obs} - R_V\,E_{B-V} = 5\log_{10}d\,[\mathrm{pc}] - 5.
		\label{eq:dereddened_distance_modulus}
	\end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

\begin{tcolorbox}[notebox, title=DERIVATION: COLOR INDEX CORRECTION AND EXTINCTION FROM MIE SCATTERING]
	Consider unextinguished starlight with flux $F_{\lambda,0}$ traversing dust with column density $N_d = \int n_d\,ds$. The transmitted flux is $F_\lambda = F_{\lambda,0}e^{-\tau_\lambda}$ with optical depth $\tau_\lambda = N_d \sigma_{\rm ext}(\lambda)$. In Pogson magnitudes, the extinction is
	\begin{equation}
		A_\lambda = -2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{F_\lambda}{F_{\lambda,0}}\right) = \frac{2.5}{\ln 10}\tau_\lambda \approx 1.086\,N_d\,\sigma_{\rm ext}(\lambda).
	\end{equation}
	For interstellar grains ($a \sim 0.1\,\mu\mathrm{m} \sim \lambda$), Mie scattering yields $\sigma_{\rm ext}(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{-1}$. Evaluating across the $B$ and $V$ passbands gives
	\begin{equation}
		A_B - A_V = 1.086\,N_d\left[\sigma_{\rm ext}(\lambda_B) - \sigma_{\rm ext}(\lambda_V)\right] > 0,
	\end{equation}
	since $\lambda_B < \lambda_V \implies \sigma_{\rm ext}(\lambda_B) > \sigma_{\rm ext}(\lambda_V)$. The observed color index is $(B - V)_{\rm obs} = m_B - m_V = (B - V)_0 + (A_B - A_V) = (B - V)_0 + E_{B-V}$. Dereddening isolates the intrinsic color $(B - V)_0 = (B - V)_{\rm obs} - E_{B-V}$, and subtracting $A_V = R_V E(B-V)$ yields the true distance modulus:
	\begin{equation}
		5\log_{10}d - 5 = (m_V - M_V)_{\rm obs} - R_V\,E_{B-V}.
	\end{equation}
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Thermal Radiation and the Stefan-Boltzmann Law}

To relate radiant emission to stellar physical properties, we model stellar photospheres as thermodynamic blackbodies.

\subsection{Planck's Law of Blackbody Radiation}

A blackbody in thermodynamic equilibrium at temperature $T$ radiates a specific intensity $B_\lambda(T)$ (energy per unit time, area, solid angle, and wavelength):
\begin{keyresult}
	\begin{equation}
		B_\lambda(T) = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5} \frac{1}{\exp\!\left(\dfrac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}\right) - 1}\quad \left[\mathrm{W\cdot m^{-2}\cdot sr^{-1}\cdot m^{-1}}\right],
		\label{eq:planck_wavelength}
	\end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where $h = 6.62607 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}$ is Planck's constant, $c = 2.99792 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}$ is the speed of light, and $k_B = 1.38065 \times 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J/K}$ is Boltzmann's constant.

In frequency space:
\begin{equation}
	B_\nu(T) = \frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2} \frac{1}{\exp\!\left(\dfrac{h\nu}{k_B T}\right) - 1}\quad \left[\mathrm{W\cdot m^{-2}\cdot sr^{-1}\cdot Hz^{-1}}\right].
	\label{eq:planck_frequency}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Wien's Displacement Law}

To find the wavelength of peak emission, write $B_\lambda \propto \lambda^{-5}(e^x-1)^{-1}$ with $x \equiv hc/(\lambda k_BT)$, and note that since $x \propto 1/\lambda$, the chain rule gives $dx/d\lambda = -x/\lambda$. Differentiating with respect to $\lambda$ (product and chain rule) and setting the result to zero:
\begin{equation}
	\dv{B_\lambda}{\lambda} \propto -5\lambda^{-6}(e^x-1)^{-1} + \lambda^{-5}(e^x-1)^{-2}e^x\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right) = 0.
\end{equation}
Multiplying through by $-\lambda^{6}(e^x-1)$ gives the transcendental condition on $x$:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{x e^x}{e^x - 1} = 5, \quad \text{where } x \equiv \frac{hc}{\lambda_{\max} k_B T} \approx 4.965114 \quad \text{(solved numerically).}
\end{equation}
This establishes \emph{Wien's Displacement Law}:
\begin{keyresult}
	\begin{equation}
		\lambda_{\max} T = \frac{hc}{4.965114\,k_B} \approx 2.89777 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\cdot K} = 2898\,\mathrm{\mu m\cdot K}.
		\label{eq:wien_law}
	\end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{The Stefan-Boltzmann Law and Effective Temperature}

Integrating Planck's specific intensity over all wavelengths and across the outward hemisphere ($\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \pi$, the projected solid angle of a hemisphere) gives the total emergent surface flux $F_{\rm surface} = \pi\int_0^\infty B_\lambda(T)\,d\lambda$. Substituting $x = hc/(\lambda k_BT)$ (so $\lambda = hc/(xk_BT)$ and $d\lambda = -hc\,dx/(xk_BT)^2$... more directly, in frequency form with $x=h\nu/k_BT$) converts the integral into a pure number times $T^4$:
\begin{equation}
	\int_0^\infty B_\nu(T)\,d\nu = \frac{2k_B^4T^4}{h^3c^2}\int_0^\infty \frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx = \frac{2k_B^4T^4}{h^3c^2}\cdot\frac{\pi^4}{15},
\end{equation}
using the standard result $\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx = \pi^4/15$ (a tabulated integral, obtained by expanding $1/(e^x-1)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-nx}$ and summing the resulting Gamma-function integrals, $\sum_n \Gamma(4)/n^4 = 6\zeta(4) = 6\cdot\pi^4/90 = \pi^4/15$). Multiplying by $\pi$ gives
\begin{equation}
	F_{\rm surface} = \sigma_{\rm SB} T^4,
	\label{eq:surface_flux_sb}
\end{equation}
where the \emph{Stefan-Boltzmann constant} collects all the remaining factors:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{\rm SB} = \frac{2\pi^5 k_B^4}{15 c^2 h^3} \approx 5.670374 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\cdot m^{-2}\cdot K^{-4}}.
	\label{eq:stefan_boltzmann_constant}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Effective Temperature]
	The \emph{effective surface temperature} $T_{\rm eff}$ of a star with total luminosity $L$ and radius $R_*$ is the temperature of an ideal blackbody having the identical surface area and total bolometric power (integrated across all wavelengths):
	\begin{keyresult}
		\begin{equation}
			L = 4\pi R_*^2\,\sigma_{\rm SB} T_{\rm eff}^4.
			\label{eq:stefan_boltzmann_luminosity}
		\end{equation}
	\end{keyresult}
\end{definition}

\section{Determination of Stellar Physical Radii}

\subsection{Radius from Bolometric Luminosity and Temperature}

Normalizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:stefan_boltzmann_luminosity} relative to solar values ($L_\odot = 3.828 \times 10^{26}\,\mathrm{W}$, $R_\odot = 6.957 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m}$, $T_{\rm eff,\odot} = 5772\,\mathrm{K}$):
\begin{keyresult}
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{R_*}{R_\odot} = \sqrt{\frac{L_*}{L_\odot}}\left(\frac{T_{\rm eff,\odot}}{T_{\rm eff}}\right)^2.
		\label{eq:stellar_radius_solar_units}
	\end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
In terms of absolute bolometric magnitude $M_{\rm bol}$ (the magnitude integrated across all wavelengths):
\begin{equation}
	\log_{10}\left(\frac{R_*}{R_\odot}\right) = -0.2(M_{\rm bol,*} - M_{\rm bol,\odot}) - 2\log_{10}\left(\frac{T_{\rm eff}}{T_{\rm eff,\odot}}\right),
	\label{eq:radius_from_mbol}
\end{equation}
where $M_{\rm bol,\odot} = +4.74\,\mathrm{mag}$.

\subsection{Angular Diameter ($\theta$)}

A spherical star of physical radius $R_*$ located at distance $d$ subtends an angular diameter $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
	\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{R_*}{d} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta \approx \frac{2 R_*}{d}\quad (\text{radians}).
	\label{eq:angular_diameter_rad}
\end{equation}
Converting $\theta$ to arcseconds ($1\,\mathrm{rad} = 206{,}264.8''$):
\begin{keyresult}
	\begin{equation}
		\theta\,[\mathrm{mas}] = 206{,}264{,}800 \times \frac{2 R_*}{d} = 9.300 \times 10^{-3} \left(\frac{R_*}{R_\odot}\right)\left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{pc}}{d}\right).
		\label{eq:angular_diameter_mas}
	\end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

\begin{example}[Angular Diameter of Betelgeuse]
	Betelgeuse ($\alpha$ Orionis) is a red supergiant with $R_* \approx 764\,R_\odot$ at a distance $d \approx 168\,\mathrm{pc}$ ($p \approx 5.95\,\mathrm{mas}$). Its expected angular diameter is:
	\[
	\theta = 9.300 \times 10^{-3} \times \frac{764}{168} \approx 42.3\,\mathrm{mas}.
	\]
	This matches direct optical interferometric measurements ($42\text{--}44\,\mathrm{mas}$), demonstrating that giant stars can be directly resolved with modern optical/IR interferometers (such as the VLTI).
\end{example}

Resolving an angular diameter of tens of milliarcseconds, as in the Betelgeuse example above, is only possible because of the interferometric and diffraction-limited resolution machinery developed next in Chapter~5.

\section{Problem Set: Stellar Photometry, Color Indices, and Extinction}

\subsection{Problem 1: Distance and Extinction from Observed Color Indices}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: An $\mathrm{A0\,V}$ star with intrinsic color index $(B - V)_0 = 0.00\,\mathrm{mag}$ and absolute magnitude $M_V = +0.60\,\mathrm{mag}$ is observed behind an interstellar cloud with apparent magnitudes $m_B = 14.55\,\mathrm{mag}$ and $m_V = 13.80\,\mathrm{mag}$. For standard dust ($R_V = 3.1$), calculate the color excess $E_{B-V}$, visual extinction $A_V$, and true distance $d$.

\textbf{Solution}: The observed color index is $(B - V)_{\rm obs} = m_B - m_V = 14.55 - 13.80 = +0.75\,\mathrm{mag}$. The color excess is $E_{B-V} = (B - V)_{\rm obs} - (B - V)_0 = 0.75 - 0.00 = +0.75\,\mathrm{mag}$, which gives visual extinction $A_V = R_V\,E_{B-V} = 3.1 \times 0.75 = 2.325\,\mathrm{mag}$. The true dereddened distance modulus is $\mu_0 = m_V - M_V - A_V = 13.80 - 0.60 - 2.325 = 10.875\,\mathrm{mag}$, yielding a true distance of $d = 10^{(\mu_0 + 5)/5} = 10^{3.175} \approx 1496\,\mathrm{pc} \approx 1.50\,\mathrm{kpc}$. Neglecting dust extinction would give an apparent distance of $10^{(13.80 - 0.60 + 5)/5} \approx 4365\,\mathrm{pc}$, overestimating the distance by a factor of nearly $3$.

\subsection{Problem 2: Photometric Parameters and Physical Radius of Aldebaran}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: The star Aldebaran ($\alpha$ Tauri) has a luminosity of $L = 439\,L_\odot$, an effective surface temperature $T_{\rm eff} = 3900\,\mathrm{K}$, and a trigonometric parallax of $\varpi = 47\,\text{mas}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item What is the distance to Aldebaran in parsecs?
	\item What is the bolometric flux $F$ received at Earth (in $\mathrm{W\cdot m^{-2}}$)?
	\item Given the Sun's absolute bolometric magnitude $M_{\rm bol,\odot} = +4.74\,\mathrm{mag}$ and solar constant $F_\odot = 1361\,\mathrm{W\cdot m^{-2}}$, compute the apparent bolometric magnitude $m_{\rm bol}$ and absolute bolometric magnitude $M_{\rm bol}$ of Aldebaran.
	\item What is the physical radius $R_*$ of Aldebaran in solar radii ($R_\odot$)?
	\item What is the angular diameter $\Theta$ subtended by Aldebaran in milliarcseconds (mas)?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Inverse-Square Law for Flux}: The total bolometric flux received at distance $d$ is:
	\begin{equation}
		F = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2}.
	\end{equation}
	\item \textbf{Bolometric Magnitudes}: Apparent and absolute bolometric magnitudes follow from Pogson's relation relative to the Sun:
	\begin{equation}
		m_{\rm bol} - m_{\rm bol,\odot} = -2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{F}{F_\odot}\right), \qquad M_{\rm bol} - M_{\rm bol,\odot} = -2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{L}{L_\odot}\right),
	\end{equation}
	and distance modulus $\mu = m_{\rm bol} - M_{\rm bol} = 5\log_{10}d - 5$.
	\item \textbf{Stefan-Boltzmann Radius}: The stellar radius is determined by total luminosity and surface temperature:
	\begin{equation}
		L = 4\pi R_*^2 \sigma_{\rm SB} T_{\rm eff}^4 \implies \frac{R_*}{R_\odot} = \sqrt{\frac{L}{L_\odot}}\left(\frac{T_{\rm eff,\odot}}{T_{\rm eff}}\right)^2,
	\end{equation}
	where $T_{\rm eff,\odot} = 5772\,\mathrm{K}$ and $R_\odot = 6.957 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m}$.
	\item \textbf{Angular Diameter}: The angular diameter subtended at distance $d$ is:
	\begin{equation}
		\Theta \approx \frac{2 R_*}{d} \quad [\text{radians}] = 206{,}265{,}000 \times \frac{2 R_*}{d} \quad [\text{mas}].
	\end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Distance}:
	From the parallax $\varpi = 47\,\text{mas} = 0.047''$:
	\[
	\boxed{d = \frac{1}{\varpi} = \frac{1}{0.047''} \simeq 21.28\,\mathrm{pc} = 6.566 \times 10^{17}\,\mathrm{m}}.
	\]

	\item \textbf{Bolometric Flux at Earth}:
	With $L = 439\,L_\odot = 439 \times (3.828 \times 10^{26}\,\mathrm{W}) \simeq 1.680 \times 10^{29}\,\mathrm{W}$:
	\[
	\boxed{F = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2} = \frac{1.680 \times 10^{29}\,\mathrm{W}}{4\pi (6.566 \times 10^{17}\,\mathrm{m})^2} \simeq 3.10 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\cdot m^{-2}}}.
	\]

	\item \textbf{Absolute and Apparent Bolometric Magnitudes}:
	The absolute bolometric magnitude is:
	\[
	M_{\rm bol} = M_{\rm bol,\odot} - 2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{L}{L_\odot}\right) = +4.74 - 2.5\log_{10}(439) = 4.74 - 2.5(2.6425) = \boxed{-1.87\,\mathrm{mag}}.
	\]
	The apparent bolometric magnitude follows from the distance modulus $\mu = 5\log_{10}(21.28) - 5 \simeq 1.64\,\mathrm{mag}$:
	\[
	\boxed{m_{\rm bol} = M_{\rm bol} + 5\log_{10}d - 5 = -1.87 + 1.64 = -0.23\,\mathrm{mag}}.
	\]
	(Equivalently, using flux ratio with $m_{\rm bol,\odot} = -26.74$: $m_{\rm bol} = -26.74 - 2.5\log_{10}(3.10 \times 10^{-8} / 1361) \simeq -0.23\,\mathrm{mag}$).

	\item \textbf{Physical Radius}:
	Using Stefan--Boltzmann scaling with solar effective temperature $T_{\rm eff,\odot} = 5772\,\mathrm{K}$:
	\[
	\frac{R_*}{R_\odot} = \sqrt{\frac{L}{L_\odot}}\left(\frac{T_{\rm eff,\odot}}{T_{\rm eff}}\right)^2 = \sqrt{439}\left(\frac{5772\,\mathrm{K}}{3900\,\mathrm{K}}\right)^2 = (20.952) \times (1.480)^2 \simeq \boxed{45.9\,R_\odot}.
	\]
	In SI units, $R_* \simeq 45.9 \times (6.957 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m}) \simeq 3.19 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 0.213\,\mathrm{AU}$.

	\item \textbf{Angular Diameter}:
	Subtended angular diameter is:
	\[
	\Theta = \frac{2 R_*}{d} = \frac{2 \times (3.193 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{m})}{6.566 \times 10^{17}\,\mathrm{m}} \simeq 9.726 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{rad}.
	\]
	Converting to milliarcseconds:
	\[
	\boxed{\Theta = (9.726 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{rad}) \times 206{,}264{,}800\,\text{mas/rad} \simeq 20.0\,\mathrm{mas}}.
	\]
	This angular diameter is readily measurable with optical interferometers (e.g., CHARA, VLTI).
\end{enumerate}
