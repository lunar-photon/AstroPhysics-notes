\chapter{The Two-Body Problem}
\label{ch:twobody}
	
\section{Central Force Problem and Conservation Laws}
\label{tb:sec:central}

We know how to solve Newton's equations for one body in a given force. A stone thrown across a field, or a mass on a spring, moves in a force that does not care where anything else is.

Two stars are different. Each one pulls the other, so each moves the source of the other's force. Newton's laws then give six coupled second-order equations, and neither star can be followed alone.

Think of two skaters on ice holding the ends of a rope and pulling on it. Seen from the side, both move in complicated loops. But the rope pulls them with equal and opposite forces, so the balance point between them, nearer the heavier skater, never speeds up. And the rope only pulls along itself, so it cannot change how fast the pair turns about that point. Only the length of the rope and the direction it points are left to work out.

A binary star is the pair of skaters, with gravity as the rope. The idea is to split off the motion of the balance point and then use the conserved energy and angular momentum, so that the two-body problem becomes one body moving in a fixed central force.

The skaters in numbers. Let them weigh $60\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $40\,\mathrm{kg}$ and stand $5\,\mathrm m$ apart. The balance point is $5\times40/100=2\,\mathrm m$ from the heavier skater and $3\,\mathrm m$ from the lighter one. Their separation moves like one body of mass $60\times40/100=24\,\mathrm{kg}$ on the end of the rope.

The picture is a dumbbell whose centre drifts along a straight line while the two weights swing round it.

The same split gives the reduced mass of the electron in the hydrogen atom. It also makes the Earth wobble round a point $4700\,\mathrm{km}$ from its centre each month, as the Earth and the Moon turn about their common centre of mass. Conservation of energy and angular momentum is principle P1 (Chapter~\ref{ch:principles}). Treating each star as a point is P2, since outside a sphere its pull is that of a point at the centre. The split and the conserved quantities together fix all twelve constants of the motion.

\subsection{Central force problem}
	A central force acts along the line joining two particles and depends only on their separation $r=|\vecx_1-\vecx_2|$. Such a force comes from a scalar potential $V(r)$. We write $\nabla$ for the gradient with respect to $\vecr=\vecx_1-\vecx_2$, so that $\nabla V=V'(r)\,\rhat$ with $V'\equiv dV/dr$. The force on particle 1 is $-\nabla V$, and by Newton's third law the force on particle 2 is $+\nabla V$. Newton's equations for the pair are
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\begin{bmatrix}m_1 & 0\\0&m_2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\vecx_1\\\vecx_2\end{bmatrix}
		=\begin{bmatrix}-\nabla V(r)\\ \nabla V(r)\end{bmatrix}. \label{eq:eom2}
	\end{equation}
	These are six second-order equations, so the general solution has 12 constants of integration.

	\subsection{Separating centre-of-mass motion}
	\label{sec:com_separation}
	With $M=m_1+m_2$ and the \textbf{reduced mass} $\mu = m_1m_2/M$, we change to the relative and centre-of-mass coordinates,
	\[
	\begin{bmatrix}\vecr\\ \vec X\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\ m_1/M& m_2/M\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\vecx_1\\\vecx_2\end{bmatrix},
	\qquad
	\begin{bmatrix}\vecx_1\\\vecx_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}m_2/M&1\\-m_1/M&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\vecr\\\vec X\end{bmatrix}.
	\]
	We substitute the second form into \eqref{eq:eom2} and multiply both sides from the left by the matrix
	\[
	\begin{bmatrix}m_2/M&-m_1/M\\1&1\end{bmatrix},
	\]
	chosen so that it turns the mass matrix diagonal,
	\begin{align}
		\begin{bmatrix}m_2/M&-m_1/M\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}m_1 & 0\\0&m_2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}m_2/M&1\\-m_1/M&1\end{bmatrix}\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\begin{bmatrix}\vecr\\\vec X\end{bmatrix}
		&= \begin{bmatrix}m_2/M&-m_1/M\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-\nabla V\\ \nabla V\end{bmatrix} \nonumber\\
		\begin{bmatrix}m_2/M&-m_1/M\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mu&m_1\\-\mu&m_2\end{bmatrix}\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\begin{bmatrix}\vecr\\\vec X\end{bmatrix}
		&\eqby{1} \begin{bmatrix}-\nabla V\\ 0\end{bmatrix} \nonumber\\
		\begin{bmatrix}\mu&0\\0&M\end{bmatrix}\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\begin{bmatrix}\vecr\\\vec X\end{bmatrix}
		&\eqby{2} \begin{bmatrix}-\nabla V\\ 0\end{bmatrix}.
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item On the left, the mass matrix times the inverse transformation has the entries $m_1m_2/M=\mu$, $m_1$, $-\mu$ and $m_2$. On the right, the first row is $-(m_2+m_1)\nabla V/M=-\nabla V$ and the second is $-\nabla V+\nabla V=0$.
		\item The first row of the product is $(\mu m_2+\mu m_1)/M=\mu$ and $(m_1m_2-m_2m_1)/M=0$. The second row is $\mu-\mu=0$ and $m_1+m_2=M$.
	\end{explain}
	The mass matrix is now diagonal, and the two rows decouple,
	\begin{align}
		\mu\,\ddot{\vecr} &= -\nabla V(r), \label{eq:relEOM}\\
		M\,\ddot{\vec X} &= 0. \label{eq:comEOM}
	\end{align}
	Equation \eqref{eq:comEOM} integrates at once to $\vec X(t) = \vec V_{\rm CM}\,t+\vec X_0$. The six constants $\vec V_{\rm CM},\vec X_0$ fix an inertial frame in which the centre of mass is at rest at the origin. All the dynamics is in the \emph{relative} coordinate $\vecr$, governed by \eqref{eq:relEOM}. It is a one-body central-force problem with the reduced mass $\mu$.

	\paragraph{Back to the two bodies.} Once $\vec X(t)$ and $\vecr(t)$ are known, the inverse matrix above gives each body's path. Its rows read $\vecx_1=\vec X+(m_2/M)\,\vecr$ and $\vecx_2=\vec X-(m_1/M)\,\vecr$, and $m_1/M=1-m_2/M$, so
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{\vecx_1(t)=\vec X(t)+\frac{m_2}{M}\,\vecr(t),\qquad \vecx_2(t)=\vec X(t)-\left(1-\frac{m_2}{M}\right)\vecr(t).}
		\label{tb:eq:body_paths}
	\end{equation}
	Each body follows the centre of mass and adds a copy of the relative orbit, shrunk by a mass fraction. Body 1 takes the share $m_2/M$ of $\vecr$, and body 2 takes the rest, $1-m_2/M$, pointing the other way. The heavier body takes the smaller share. For the Earth and the Moon $M/m_{\rm Moon}=82.3$, so the Earth's centre moves on a copy of the Moon's orbit $82.3$ times smaller, of radius $384\,400/82.3\approx4700\,\mathrm{km}$. That is the monthly wobble of the Earth.

	The relative orbit and the total mass are not enough to find the two paths. Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:body_paths} needs one more number, the mass fraction $m_2/M$. The relative orbit cannot supply it. For gravity, $V=-Gm_1m_2/r$ and $\mu=m_1m_2/M$, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:relEOM} reads $\ddot{\vecr}=-GM\rhat/r^2$. The masses enter only through their sum. Move some mass from one body to the other at fixed $M$ and $\vecr(t)$ does not change at all. So no measurement of the relative orbit, however precise, can tell how $M$ is split. The mass fraction has to be measured some other way. It is the place of the centre of mass on the line between the bodies, $m_2/M=|\vecx_1-\vec X|/r$, a purely geometric ratio. We can see it by tracking each body against the far background stars (Problem~\ref{tb:prob:astrometric}), or by comparing the two Doppler swings of a spectroscopic binary (Problem~\ref{prob:sb2}). When one body is far lighter than the other, the centre of mass sits too close to the heavy body to measure, and the split must come from outside the orbit (Problem~\ref{prob:halo_mass}).

	\subsection{Conserved quantities}

	\paragraph{Angular momentum.} Define $\vecL=\vecr\times\vecp=\mu\,\vecr\times\dot{\vecr}$, with $\vecp=\mu\dot{\vecr}$. Then
	\begin{align}
		\dot{\vecL}
		&\eqby{1} \mu\big[\dot{\vecr}\times\dot{\vecr}+\vecr\times\ddot{\vecr}\big] \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{2} -\vecr\times\nabla V(r) \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{3} -V'(r)\,r\,\rhat\times\rhat = 0 .
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item The product rule.
		\item $\dot{\vecr}\times\dot{\vecr}=0$, and $\mu\ddot{\vecr}=-\nabla V$ from \eqref{eq:relEOM}.
		\item $\vecr=r\rhat$ and $\nabla V=V'(r)\rhat$. The force is central.
	\end{explain}
	$\vecL$ supplies three constants of motion. They are its magnitude $L$ and two angles fixing its direction, the \textbf{inclination} $\iota$ and the \textbf{longitude of the ascending node} $\Omega$ (Section~\ref{sec:coord_orbital_elements}). Since $\vecr\cdot\vecL=\mu\,\vecr\cdot(\vecr\times\dot{\vecr})=0$, the body stays in the plane through the origin perpendicular to $\vecL$.

	\paragraph{Energy.} $E=K+V$ with $K=\tfrac12\mu\dot{\vecr}^2$. The kinetic energy of the centre of mass is a separate constant and drops out. Writing $\dot r=d|\vecr|/dt$, we have $\rhat\cdot\dot{\vecr}=\dot r$, because $d(\vecr\cdot\vecr)/dt=2r\dot r=2\vecr\cdot\dot{\vecr}$. Then
	\begin{align}
		\dot E
		&\eqby{1} \mu\dot{\vecr}\cdot\ddot{\vecr}+V'(r)\,\dot r \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{2} \mu\dot{\vecr}\cdot\ddot{\vecr}+\nabla V\cdot\dot{\vecr} \nonumber\\
		&= \big(\mu\ddot{\vecr}+\nabla V\big)\cdot\dot{\vecr}\eqby{3}0 .
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item The product rule, and the chain rule $dV/dt=V'(r)\,\dot r$.
		\item $\nabla V\cdot\dot{\vecr}=V'(r)\,\rhat\cdot\dot{\vecr}=V'(r)\,\dot r$.
		\item The equation of motion \eqref{eq:relEOM}.
	\end{explain}
	So $E$ is conserved for \emph{any} central potential $V(r)$, not just gravity. The \textbf{total energy and angular momentum} give four constants of integration. There is one more conserved quantity, the Runge--Lenz vector of Section~\ref{tb:sec:runge_lenz}. It points towards the periapsis and fixes the orientation of the orbit within its plane. The last constant is the time $t_0$ of a passage through periapsis, which fixes where on the orbit the body is at a given time. The count is
	\[
	\underbrace{\vec X_0,\vec V_{\rm CM}}_{6}
	+
	\underbrace{a,e,\iota,\Omega,\omega,t_0}_{6}
	=12.
	\]
	
	\section{Coordinate Systems and Orbital Elements}
	\label{sec:coord_orbital_elements}

	The angular momentum fixes a plane in space, and the body stays in it.

	A telescope does not see that plane face on. It sees the orbit squashed onto the sky, and to compare the two we must know how the orbital plane sits relative to the line of sight.

	Take a hoop and tilt it in front of a wall. Seen from the front, the round hoop looks like an ellipse. To say how it is tilted, we name the line on the wall about which it was turned, like a hinge, and the angle of the turn. To say where a mark on the hoop is, we give one more angle measured round the hoop from the hinge.

	An orbit is the hoop and the plane of the sky is the wall. The idea is that the orbit's own frame is the sky frame turned twice, first by $\Omega$ about the line of sight and then by $\iota$ about the hinge, the line of nodes. One more angle, $\omega$, places the ellipse within its plane.

	The hoop in numbers. A circle of radius $1$ tilted by $\iota=60^\circ$ about a line through its centre appears as an ellipse with semi-axes $1$ and $\cos60^\circ=0.5$. Lengths along the hinge stay the same and lengths across it shrink by $\cos\iota$.

	The picture is two planes, the orbit and the sky, joined at a hinge along the line of nodes.

	The same two turns build the ecliptic and Galactic frames of Chapter~\ref{ch:coords}, and in rigid-body mechanics they are the Euler angles. Only geometry enters here, so none of the six principles is used. The two turns give the matrix from the sky frame to the orbital frame, the orbit as projected on the sky, and the six orbital elements.

	\paragraph{The sky frame.} Fix an equatorial (reference) frame with the vernal equinox $\hat\gamma$ along $X$ and celestial north $\hat N$ along $Z$, and let $\hat Y$ complete the right-handed triad. For a source at right ascension $\alpha$ and declination $\delta$, the line of sight from the observer to the source is (Chapter~\ref{ch:coords}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:unit_vector_eq})
	\[
	\hat R = \cos\delta\cos\alpha\,\hat\gamma+\cos\delta\sin\alpha\,\hat Y+\sin\delta\,\hat N .
	\]
	The local axes in the plane of the sky point along increasing $\alpha$ and $\delta$. They are the normalised derivatives of $\hat R$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:astrometry}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:equatorial_basis_vectors}),
	\[
	\hat\alpha=\frac{\pdv{\hat R}{\alpha}}{\abs{\pdv{\hat R}{\alpha}}}=-\sin\alpha\,\hat\gamma+\cos\alpha\,\hat Y,\qquad
	\hat\delta=\frac{\pdv{\hat R}{\delta}}{\abs{\pdv{\hat R}{\delta}}}=-\sin\delta\cos\alpha\,\hat\gamma-\sin\delta\sin\alpha\,\hat Y+\cos\delta\,\hat N .
	\]
	The triad $(\hat\alpha,\hat\delta,\hat R)$ is right-handed, since $\hat\alpha\times\hat\delta=\hat R$.

	\paragraph{The orbital frame.} The orbital plane has its own frame. Its origin is the centre of mass, its $X$ axis lies along the \textbf{line of nodes}, where the orbital plane cuts the sky plane, and its $Z$ axis lies along $\vecL$. We write $\hatn$ for the unit vector towards the \textbf{ascending node}, where the body crosses the sky plane moving away from us. The frame is reached by two turns. A turn by $\Omega$ about $\hat R$ brings the first axis from $\hat\alpha$ to $\hatn$, and a turn by $\iota$ about $\hatn$ brings the third axis from $\hat R$ to $\hat L$. Each turn is a matrix whose rows are the new axes (Chapter~\ref{ch:coords}, Eqs.~\eqref{co:eq:rows_rule}, \eqref{eq:z_axis_rotation_matrix} and~\eqref{co:eq:Rx}), so

	\begin{equation}
		\begin{pmatrix} \hatn\\ \hat y\\ \hat L \end{pmatrix}_{\text{Orbital Plane}} =
		\begin{pmatrix} 1&0&0\\ 0&\cos\iota&\sin\iota\\ 0&-\sin\iota&\cos\iota \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \cos\Omega&\sin\Omega&0\\ -\sin\Omega&\cos\Omega&0\\ 0&0&1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \hat\alpha\\ \hat\delta\\ \hat R \end{pmatrix}_{\text{Sky plane}}.
		\label{eq:orbital_frame_rotation}
	\end{equation}
	Multiplying out the two matrices,
	\begin{align}
		\begin{pmatrix} \hatn\\ \hat y\\ \hat L \end{pmatrix}
		&\eqby{1}
		\begin{pmatrix} \cos\Omega&\sin\Omega&0\\ -\sin\Omega\cos\iota&\cos\Omega\cos\iota&\sin\iota\\ \sin\Omega\sin\iota&-\cos\Omega\sin\iota&\cos\iota \end{pmatrix}
		\begin{pmatrix} \hat\alpha\\ \hat\delta\\ \hat R \end{pmatrix}.
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item Each row of $\mathbf R_x(\iota)$ times each column of $\mathbf R_z(\Omega)$. The first row of $\mathbf R_x$ copies the first row of $\mathbf R_z$. The second and third rows mix the second and third rows of $\mathbf R_z$ with weights $(\cos\iota,\sin\iota)$ and $(-\sin\iota,\cos\iota)$.
	\end{explain}
	Reading off the rows,
	\begin{align}
		\hatn  &= \cos\Omega\,\hat\alpha+\sin\Omega\,\hat\delta, \label{eq:node}\\
		\hat y &= -\sin\Omega\cos\iota\,\hat\alpha+\cos\Omega\cos\iota\,\hat\delta+\sin\iota\,\hat R, \label{eq:yhat}\\
		\hat L &= \sin\Omega\sin\iota\,\hat\alpha-\cos\Omega\sin\iota\,\hat\delta+\cos\iota\,\hat R. \label{eq:Lhat}
	\end{align}
	\begin{figure}[htbp]
		\centering
		\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{
			\includegraphics{fig_ch6_orbital_frame_3d.pdf}}
		\caption{An orbit (red) and the plane of the sky (blue), with the sky axes $\hat\alpha$, $\hat\delta$ and the orbital axes $\hatn$, $\hat y$, $\hat L$. $\Omega$ is the angle in the sky plane from $\hat\alpha$ to the ascending node. $\omega$ is the angle in the orbital plane from the ascending node to the periapsis. $\iota$ is the angle between the two planes at the node. $v$ is the angle in the orbital plane from the periapsis to the body $P$, and $P_{\rm sky}$ is the projection of $P$ on the sky plane.}
		\label{fig:elements}
	\end{figure}
	The third row gives $\iota=\arccos(\hat L\cdot\hat R)$, the angle between the orbital plane and the sky plane. The rows of a rotation are orthonormal, so $(\hatn,\hat y,\hat L)$ is a right-handed orthonormal triad. Its first two vectors span the orbital plane and the third is its normal. In the plane we use polar coordinates $(r,\phi)$, with $\phi$ measured from $\hatn$. The radial and angular unit vectors are $(\hatn,\hat y)$ turned by $\phi$,

	\begin{equation}
		\begin{pmatrix}\rhat\\ \phihat\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi&\sin\phi\\ -\sin\phi&\cos\phi\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hatn\\ \hat y\end{pmatrix},
		\qquad\text{that is}\qquad
		\rhat=\cos\phi\,\hatn+\sin\phi\,\hat y,\quad
		\phihat=-\sin\phi\,\hatn+\cos\phi\,\hat y. \label{eq:rhat_phihat}
	\end{equation}
	\paragraph{The projected orbit in the sky plane.} The body sits in its plane at the polar angle $\phi=\omega+v$. Here $\omega$ is the angle from the node to the periapsis and $v$, the \textbf{true anomaly}, is the angle from the periapsis to the body. Both are found in Section~\ref{tb:sec:kepler_orbits}. The body's orbital components are $(r\cos(\omega+v),\,r\sin(\omega+v),\,0)$. To get its sky components $(X,Y,Z)$ along $(\hat\alpha,\hat\delta,\hat R)$ we undo Eq.~\eqref{eq:orbital_frame_rotation}. A rotation is undone by its transpose (Chapter~\ref{ch:coords}, Eq.~\eqref{co:eq:orthogonal}), and a product is undone in reverse order, so

	\begin{align}
		\begin{pmatrix}
			X \\
			Y \\
			Z
		\end{pmatrix}_{\text{Sky Plane}}
		&=
		\begin{pmatrix}
			\cos\Omega & -\sin\Omega & 0 \\
			\sin\Omega & \cos\Omega & 0 \\
			0 & 0 & 1
		\end{pmatrix}
		\begin{pmatrix}
			1 & 0 & 0 \\
			0 & \cos\iota & -\sin\iota \\
			0 & \sin\iota & \cos\iota
		\end{pmatrix}
		\begin{pmatrix}
			r\cos(\omega+v) \\
			r\sin(\omega+v) \\
			0
		\end{pmatrix}_{\text{Orbital Plane}} \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{1}
		\begin{pmatrix}
			\cos\Omega & -\sin\Omega & 0 \\
			\sin\Omega & \cos\Omega & 0 \\
			0 & 0 & 1
		\end{pmatrix}
		\begin{pmatrix}
			r\cos(\omega+v) \\
			r\sin(\omega+v)\cos\iota \\
			r\sin(\omega+v)\sin\iota
		\end{pmatrix} \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{2}
		r\begin{pmatrix}
			\cos(\omega+v)\cos\Omega-\sin (\omega+v)\sin\Omega\cos\iota \\
			\cos(\omega+v)\sin\Omega+\sin (\omega+v)\cos\Omega\cos\iota \\
			\sin(\omega+v)\sin\iota
		\end{pmatrix}.
		\label{tb:eq:sky_xyz}
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\mathbf R_x(\iota)^T$ times the column.
		\item $\mathbf R_z(\Omega)^T$ times the column.
	\end{explain}
	$X$ and $Y$ give the position on the sky. $Z$ is the distance along the line of sight, beyond the centre of mass, and it returns in the timing of pulsars (Section~\ref{sec:romer}).
	\begin{figure}[htbp]
		\centering
		\includegraphics{fig_ch6_conic_sections_geometry.pdf}
		\caption{An orbit (black) and its projection (blue) on the plane of the sky. N, E and W mark north, east and west on the sky, and the arrow points towards the Earth. $\Omega$ is measured in the sky plane from north to the node line, and $\omega$ in the orbital plane from the node line to the periapsis. P marks a position $(r\cos(\omega+v),r\sin(\omega+v))$ on the orbit.}
		\label{tb:fig:sky_projection}
	\end{figure}

	Figure~\ref{tb:fig:sky_projection} takes north as the first sky axis and west as the second. This pair is $(\hat\alpha,\hat\delta)$ turned by $90^\circ$ about $\hat R$, so Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:sky_xyz} holds unchanged with $\Omega$ counted from north. In the same way we may choose the first sky axis along the line of nodes. That sets $\Omega=0$, and the sky position becomes
	\begin{align}
		X &= r\cos(\omega+v),\\
		Y &= r\sin (\omega+v)\cos\iota.
	\end{align}
	Lengths along the line of nodes are unchanged, and every length across it shrinks by the factor $\cos\iota$. A circular orbit therefore appears as an ellipse with axis ratio $\cos\iota$, and it appears as a circle only when $\iota=0$ (Problem~1).
	\paragraph{The six orbital elements.} Six constants appear as the orbit is solved. The angles $\Omega$ and $\iota$ give the orientation of the plane and come from $\vecL$. The semi-major axis $a$ and the eccentricity $e$ give the size and shape and come from $E$ and $L$. The \textbf{argument of periapsis} $\omega$ gives the orientation of the ellipse \emph{within} the plane. The time $t_0$ of periapsis passage fixes where on the ellipse the body is at a given time. Figure~\ref{fig:elements} shows them together.
	
	\section{Planar Motion and Kepler's Second Law}
	\label{tb:sec:planar}

	We have a conserved angular momentum, and with it an orbit that stays in one plane.

	Conservation alone does not say how fast the body moves at each point of its path.

	A ball slides on a smooth table, tied to a string that passes down through a small hole. Pull the string from below and the ball circles faster as its circle shrinks. The string pulls only towards the hole, so it never pushes the ball sideways. What stays fixed is the distance from the hole times the sideways speed.

	A planet does the same. It moves fastest where it is nearest the Sun. The idea is that the area swept by the line from the Sun to the planet grows at a fixed rate, $L/(2\mu)$.

	The Earth in numbers. It is $0.983\,\mathrm{AU}$ from the Sun at perihelion in January and $1.017\,\mathrm{AU}$ at aphelion in July. At both points the velocity is perpendicular to the radius, so the distance times the speed is the same at both. The Earth is therefore $1.017/0.983=1.035$ times faster in January, about $3.5$ per cent.

	The picture is a fan of thin triangles with a common tip at the Sun, long and narrow far away and short and wide nearby, all of the same area.

	The same conservation of angular momentum (P1) spins up a skater who pulls in her arms, and spins up a collapsing cloud until it flattens into a disk (Section~\ref{sg:sec:rotation}). It holds for any central force, not only gravity. It gives Kepler's second law and the single radial equation that the rest of the chapter solves.

	\paragraph{Areal velocity.} In a time $dt$ the radius vector moves from $\vecr$ to $\vecr+\dot{\vecr}\,dt$ and sweeps a thin triangle. Its area is half the size of the cross product of its two sides,
	\begin{equation}
		dA=\tfrac12\big|\vecr\times\dot{\vecr}\,dt\big| \eqby{1} \frac{L}{2\mu}\,dt,
		\qquad\text{so}\qquad
		\frac{dA}{dt}=\frac{r^2\dot\phi}{2} \eqby{2} \frac{L}{2\mu} = \text{const.} \label{eq:kepler2}
	\end{equation}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\vecL=\mu\,\vecr\times\dot{\vecr}$.
		\item In polar coordinates the triangle has sides $r$ and $r\,d\phi$, so $dA=\tfrac12r^2d\phi$. Then $r^2\dot\phi=L/\mu$, as Eq.~\eqref{eq:phidot} below shows.
	\end{explain}
	This is \textbf{Kepler's second law}, equal areas in equal times. It follows only from $\vecL$ being conserved, that is, from the force being central. It does not depend on the form of $V(r)$.

	\paragraph{Equations of motion in $(r,\phi)$.} With $Z\parallel\vecL$ and $X\parallel\hatn$, we have $\vecr=r\rhat$. The unit vectors \eqref{eq:rhat_phihat} turn with $\phi$. Differentiating their matrix,
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}\rhat\\ \phihat\end{pmatrix}
		=\dot\phi\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\phi&\cos\phi\\ -\cos\phi&-\sin\phi\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hatn\\ \hat y\end{pmatrix}
		=\dot\phi\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\ -1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\rhat\\ \phihat\end{pmatrix},
		\label{tb:eq:unit_vector_rates}
	\end{equation}
	so $\dot{\rhat}=\dot\phi\,\phihat$ and $\dot{\phihat}=-\dot\phi\,\rhat$. The velocity and acceleration follow by the product rule,
	\begin{align}
		\dot{\vecr}&=\dot r\,\rhat+r\dot{\rhat}\eqby{1}\dot r\,\rhat+r\dot\phi\,\phihat, \nonumber\\
		\ddot{\vecr}&=\ddot r\,\rhat+\dot r\dot\phi\,\phihat+\dot r\dot\phi\,\phihat+r\ddot\phi\,\phihat+r\dot\phi\,\dot{\phihat}
		\eqby{1}\big(\ddot r-r\dot\phi^2\big)\rhat+\big(2\dot r\dot\phi+r\ddot\phi\big)\phihat.
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\dot{\rhat}=\dot\phi\,\phihat$ and $\dot{\phihat}=-\dot\phi\,\rhat$, from Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:unit_vector_rates}.
	\end{explain}
	The same rates follow from the Christoffel symbols of polar coordinates, $\nabla_{\mu}\mathbf{e}_{\nu}=\Gamma^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}\mathbf{e}_{\alpha}$. With $\Gamma^{\phi}_{\phi r}=1/r$ and the coordinate basis vector $\mathbf e_\phi=r\phihat$, we get $\dot{\rhat}=\dot{\phi}\,\Gamma_{\phi r}^{\mu}\mathbf{e}_{\mu}=\dot{\phi}\,\mathbf{e}_{\phi}/r=\dot{\phi}\,\phihat$.
	\begin{figure}[htbp]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{3D_Spherical.png}
		\caption{Spherical polar coordinates and their unit vectors.}
		\label{fig:3D_spherical}
	\end{figure}
	Equation \eqref{eq:relEOM}, with $\nabla V=V'(r)\rhat$, splits into radial and tangential parts,
	\begin{align}
		\mu\big(\ddot r-r\dot\phi^2\big) &= -V'(r), \label{eq:radial}\\
		\mu\big(2\dot r\dot\phi+r\ddot\phi\big) &= 0\implies \dv{(r^2\dot{\phi})}{t}=r\big(2\dot r\dot\phi+r\ddot\phi\big)=0. \label{eq:tangential}
	\end{align}
	These look like two coupled equations. But Eq.~\eqref{eq:tangential} is only the conservation of angular momentum, Kepler's second law again, because
	\[
	\vecL=\mu\vecr\times\dot{\vecr}=\mu\,r\rhat\times\big(\dot r\,\rhat+r\dot\phi\,\phihat\big)=\mu r^2\dot\phi\,\hat L .
	\]
	So
	\begin{equation}
		\dot\phi = \frac{L}{\mu r^2}, \label{eq:phidot}
	\end{equation}
	and Eq.~\eqref{eq:radial} becomes, eliminating $\dot\phi$,
	\begin{equation}
		\mu\ddot r - \frac{L^2}{\mu r^3} = -V'(r). \label{eq:radial_reduced}
	\end{equation}
	This is one-dimensional motion in $r$ alone. The term $L^2/(\mu r^3)$ is the centrifugal push, and Problem~5 treats it as an effective potential.
	
	\section{The Orbit Equation}
	\label{tb:sec:orbit_equation}

	We have one radial equation, \eqref{eq:radial_reduced}, with time as the variable.

	For a closed orbit the first thing we want is its shape, the curve $r(\phi)$. Time only gets in the way.

	A hiker's track drawn on a map shows the whole route without saying how fast she walked. Each point of the route is named by where it is, not by when she was there.

	The angle $\phi$ of an orbit grows steadily, since $\dot\phi=L/(\mu r^2)>0$ whenever $L\ne0$. So $\phi$ can replace $t$ as the clock. The idea is to change the variable from $t$ to $\phi$ and to write $\rho=1/r$. For gravity the equation then becomes linear.

	The smallest example is a circle, where $\rho$ is constant and $d^2\rho/d\phi^2=0$. For gravity the orbit equation \eqref{eq:orbiteq} below then gives $\rho=GM\mu^2/L^2$, a circle of radius $(L/\mu)^2/GM$. For the Earth, $L/\mu=r\,|\dot{\vecr}|=1.496\times10^{11}\,\mathrm m\times29.78\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}=4.455\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, and with $GM_\odot=1.327\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$ the radius is $1.000\,\mathrm{AU}$, as it must be.

	The picture is the orbit drawn on paper with no clock marks along it.

	The same change of variable, Binet's, gives the bending of light and the turning of Mercury's orbit in general relativity. Here it uses only the conservation of $L$ (P1). It gives an equation for the shape of the orbit in any central potential.

	\paragraph{From time to angle.} Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:phidot}, $\dfrac{dr}{dt}=\dot\phi\dfrac{dr}{d\phi}=\dfrac{L}{\mu r^2}\dfrac{dr}{d\phi}$, and
	\begin{align}
		\frac{d^2r}{dt^2}
		&\eqby{1} \frac{L}{\mu r^2}\frac{d}{d\phi}\!\left[\frac{L}{\mu r^2}\frac{dr}{d\phi}\right] \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{2} \frac{L^2}{\mu^2 r^4}\left[\frac{d^2r}{d\phi^2}-\frac{2}{r}\left(\frac{dr}{d\phi}\right)^2\right].
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $d/dt=\dot\phi\,d/d\phi$, applied to $dr/dt$.
		\item The product rule, with $\dfrac{d}{d\phi}\dfrac{1}{r^2}=-\dfrac{2}{r^3}\dfrac{dr}{d\phi}$.
	\end{explain}
	Substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:radial_reduced} and dividing by $L^2/\mu$,
	\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{r^4}\left[\frac{d^2r}{d\phi^2}-\frac{2}{r}\left(\frac{dr}{d\phi}\right)^2\right]-\frac{1}{r^3}=-\frac{\mu}{L^2}V'(r).
	\label{tb:eq:shape_r}
	\end{equation}
	\paragraph{Binet's substitution.} We set $r=1/\rho$. Then
	\[
	\frac{dr}{d\phi}=-\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\frac{d\rho}{d\phi},\qquad
	\frac{d^2r}{d\phi^2}=-\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\frac{d^2\rho}{d\phi^2}+\frac{2}{\rho^{3}}\left(\frac{d\rho}{d\phi}\right)^2,
	\]
	so the left side of Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:shape_r} is
	\begin{align}
		\rho^4\left[-\frac{1}{\rho^2}\frac{d^2\rho}{d\phi^2}+\frac{2}{\rho^3}\left(\frac{d\rho}{d\phi}\right)^2-2\rho\cdot\frac{1}{\rho^4}\left(\frac{d\rho}{d\phi}\right)^2\right]-\rho^3
		&\eqby{1} -\rho^2\frac{d^2\rho}{d\phi^2}-\rho^3 .
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item The two $(d\rho/d\phi)^2$ terms cancel.
	\end{explain}
	Setting this equal to $-(\mu/L^2)V'(1/\rho)$ and dividing by $-\rho^2$ gives the \textbf{orbit equation},
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{\frac{d^2\rho}{d\phi^2}+\rho = \frac{\mu}{L^2}\frac{1}{\rho^2}V'\!\left(\frac1\rho\right).} \label{eq:orbiteq}
	\end{equation}
	This is an equation for the orbit's shape alone, valid for any central potential.
	
	\section{Keplerian Orbits}
	\label{tb:sec:kepler_orbits}

	The orbit equation \eqref{eq:orbiteq} holds for any central force.

	For gravity we want the actual shape, and we want to know whether the orbit closes on itself.

	Hang a weight on a spring. Gravity pulls it down with a constant force, and the weight bobs up and down exactly as it would without gravity. The only change is that it bobs about a lower rest point. A constant force on an oscillator just moves the centre of the oscillation.

	For gravity the orbit equation is that spring, with $\phi$ as the time. The idea is that $\rho=1/r$ oscillates about a constant value, once per turn of $\phi$, so the orbit closes and is a conic section.

	The spring in numbers. A mass of $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ on a spring of stiffness $100\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ hangs $9.8\,\mathrm{cm}$ lower than the unstretched spring would put it, and oscillates about that point with the same period, $2\pi\sqrt{1/100}\,\mathrm s=0.63\,\mathrm s$, as it would in free space.

	The picture is $\rho$ plotted against $\phi$, a cosine wave riding on a constant level, with one wave per turn.

	Only the $1/r$ potential and the harmonic potential give closed orbits for every bound motion (Bertrand's theorem). In the hydrogen atom the same fact shows up as the equal energies of states with the same principal quantum number. The pull of a spherical star or planet is that of a point at its centre (P2), so the result holds for real stars and planets, not only for points. It gives the conic $r(\phi)$, its axes, and Kepler's first law.

	\paragraph{The conic.} For Newtonian gravity $V(r)=-GM\mu/r$, so $V'(r)=GM\mu/r^2=GM\mu\rho^2$. Equation \eqref{eq:orbiteq} becomes
	\[
	\frac{d^2\rho}{d\phi^2}+\rho =\frac{\mu}{L^2}\frac{1}{\rho^2}\,GM\mu\rho^2=\frac{GM\mu^2}{L^2}.
	\]

	Shifting $\tilde\rho=\rho-GM\mu^2/L^2$ turns this into the simple-harmonic-oscillator equation $d^2\tilde\rho/d\phi^2+\tilde\rho=0$, with solution $\tilde\rho=C\cos(\phi-\omega)$ for constants $C\ge0$ and $\omega$. In terms of $r=1/\rho$,
	\[
	r(\phi)=\left(\frac{GM\mu^2}{L^2}+C\cos(\phi-\omega)\right)^{-1}
	=\frac{L^2/(GM\mu^2)}{1+\dfrac{L^2C}{GM\mu^2}\cos(\phi-\omega)}.
	\]
	We define the \textbf{semi-latus rectum} $\lambda \equiv L^2/(GM\mu^2)$, which is the value of $r$ at $\phi=\omega+90^{\circ}$, and the \textbf{eccentricity} $e\equiv \lambda C$. Then
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{r(\phi) = \frac{\lambda}{1+e\cos(\phi-\omega)}.} \label{eq:conic}
	\end{equation}
	This is the polar equation of a conic section with one focus at the origin, semi-latus rectum $\lambda$ and eccentricity $e$. Its major axis is turned by $\omega$, the \textbf{argument of periapsis}, and the periapsis is at $\phi=\omega$. The angle from the periapsis, $v\equiv\phi-\omega$, is the \textbf{true anomaly}.

	\paragraph{The axes.} For $e<1$ the orbit is closed. Its nearest point, the periapsis, is at $r_{\rm p}=\lambda/(1+e)$, and its farthest, the apoapsis, is at $r_{\rm a}=\lambda/(1-e)$. The major axis is their sum, so the \textbf{semi-major axis} is
	\[
	a=\frac{r_{\rm p}+r_{\rm a}}{2}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\frac{(1-e)+(1+e)}{1-e^2}=\frac{\lambda}{1-e^2}.
	\]
	The centre lies midway between the two ends, at $a-r_{\rm p}=a-a(1-e)=ae$ from the focus.
	\begin{figure}[htbp]
		\centering
		\includegraphics{fig_ch6_vis_viva_geometry.pdf}
		\caption{An ellipse with semi-axes $a$ and $b$, centre $O$ and foci $F$ and $F'$, each at distance $ae$ from $O$. The periapsis P and the apoapsis A lie at distances $a(1-e)$ and $a(1+e)$ from $F$. The dotted lines join the end of the minor axis to the two foci. The semi-latus rectum $\lambda$ is drawn at $F$, perpendicular to the major axis.}
		\label{tb:fig:ellipse}
	\end{figure}
	To see that the conic is the ellipse $x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2=1$ with \textbf{semi-minor axis} $b = a\sqrt{1-e^2}$, we put the origin of $(x,y)$ at the centre and the periapsis on the $+x$ axis (taking $\omega=0$). The focus is then at $(ae,0)$, and a point at $(r,\phi)$ about the focus has
	\begin{align}
		x &= ae+r\cos\phi,\\
		y &= r\sin\phi.
	\end{align}
	Substituting into the ellipse with $b^2=a^2(1-e^2)$,
	\begin{align}
		1&=\frac{(ae+r\cos\phi)^2}{a^2}+\frac{r^2\sin^2\phi}{a^2(1-e^2)} \nonumber\\
		0&\eqby{1} r^2(1-e^2\cos^2\phi)+2ae(1-e^2)\,r\cos\phi-a^2(1-e^2)^2 \nonumber\\
		r&\eqby{2} \frac{a(1-e^2)(1-e\cos\phi)}{(1-e\cos\phi)(1+e\cos\phi)}
		=\frac{a(1-e^2)}{1+e\cos\phi}.
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item Multiply by $a^2(1-e^2)$, expand, and use $\sin^2\phi=1-\cos^2\phi$ and $(1-e^2)a^2e^2-a^2(1-e^2)=-a^2(1-e^2)^2$.
		\item The quadratic formula. The discriminant is $4a^2(1-e^2)^2\big[e^2\cos^2\phi+1-e^2\cos^2\phi\big]=4a^2(1-e^2)^2$, and the root with $r>0$ is kept. Then $1-e^2\cos^2\phi$ is factored.
	\end{explain}
	This is the conic \eqref{eq:conic} with $\lambda=a(1-e^2)$, so the orbit is this ellipse. The point $(0,b)$ at the end of the minor axis is at distance $\sqrt{b^2+a^2e^2}=a$ from each focus (Figure~\ref{tb:fig:ellipse}). At $x=ae$ the ellipse has $y=b\sqrt{1-e^2}=b^2/a=\lambda$, which is the semi-latus rectum again.

	\begin{center}
		\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
			\toprule
			Eccentricity & Conic section\\
			\midrule
			$e=0$ & circle\\
			$0<e<1$ & ellipse ($E<0$, bound)\\
			$e=1$ & parabola ($E=0$)\\
			$e>1$ & hyperbola ($E>0$, unbound)\\
			\bottomrule
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	The sign of $E$ in each row is shown in Section~\ref{tb:sec:EL_ae}. Bound, closed elliptical orbits ($E<0$) are \textbf{Kepler's first law}. In the centre-of-mass frame, $\vec X=0$, Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:body_paths} gives the two bodies' positions,
	\[
	\vecx_1=\frac{m_2}{M}\vecr,\qquad \vecx_2=-\frac{m_1}{M}\vecr.
	\]
	
	\section{Energy and Angular Momentum in Terms of \texorpdfstring{$a,e$}{a,e}}
	\label{tb:sec:EL_ae}

	We have the shape of the orbit, with constants $\lambda$ and $e$, and two conserved numbers, $E$ and $L$.

	The shape constants came out of solving a differential equation. We do not yet know how they depend on $E$ and $L$.

	A child on a swing stops for a moment at the top of each swing. There all of the energy is height, so the height of the swing tells us the energy without any need to know the speed elsewhere.

	An orbit has two such turning points of $r$, the periapsis and the apoapsis, where $\dot r=0$. The idea is to evaluate the conserved $E$ at a turning point. The size $a$ then depends on $E$ alone, and $e$ on $E$ and $L$.

	The swing in numbers. A child of $20\,\mathrm{kg}$ who rises $0.5\,\mathrm m$ has $E=20\times9.8\times0.5=98\,\mathrm J$ more than at rest, and passes the bottom at $\sqrt{2\times9.8\times0.5}=3.1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

	The picture is the two ends of the ellipse, where the body moves straight across the radius.

	The same turning points are the roots of the effective potential in Problem~5. In the hydrogen atom the energy depends only on the principal quantum number, the quantum form of $E$ depending only on $a$. The whole calculation is P1. It gives $E$, $L$ and the speed in terms of $a$ and $e$, and the inverse.

	\subsection{Energy}
	Writing the energy in polar coordinates,
	\begin{align}
	E&=\frac12\mu\dot{\vecr}^2-\frac{GM\mu}{r} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{1} \frac12\mu\big(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2\big)-\frac{GM\mu}{r} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2}\frac12\mu\dot r^2+\frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}-\frac{GM\mu}{r}.
	\label{tb:eq:energy_polar}
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\dot{\vecr}=\dot r\,\rhat+r\dot\phi\,\phihat$ with $\rhat\perp\phihat$.
		\item $\dot\phi=L/(\mu r^2)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:phidot}.
	\end{explain}
	Since $E$ is conserved, we evaluate it at periapsis ($\phi=\omega$). There $r=\lambda/(1+e)$, and $\dot r=0$ because $r$ has a minimum,
	\begin{align}
	E &\eqby{1} \frac{L^2(1+e)^2}{2\mu\lambda^2}-\frac{GM\mu(1+e)}{\lambda} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2}\frac{GM\mu(1+e)^2}{2\lambda}-\frac{GM\mu(1+e)}{\lambda} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3}-\frac{GM\mu}{2\lambda}(1-e^2) \eqby{4} -\frac{GM\mu}{2a}.
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:energy_polar} with $\dot r=0$ and $r=\lambda/(1+e)$.
		\item $L^2=GM\mu^2\lambda$, the definition of $\lambda$.
		\item $(1+e)^2-2(1+e)=(1+e)(e-1)=-(1-e^2)$.
		\item $a=\lambda/(1-e^2)$.
	\end{explain}
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{E=-\frac{GM\mu}{2a}.} \label{eq:visviva_E}
	\end{equation}
	The energy depends on the size of the orbit only, not on its shape. Since $\lambda>0$ always, the sign of $E$ follows $e$. Bound orbits ($E<0$) have $e<1$ and $a>0$. Unbound orbits ($E\geq0$) have $e\geq1$, and hyperbolic orbits formally have $a<0$.

	\subsection{Angular momentum}
	At the apoapsis, $r=a+ae=\lambda/(1-e)$, and at the periapsis, $r=a-ae=\lambda/(1+e)$, we have $\dot{r}=0$. Equating the energy at the two points as a check,
	\begin{align}
		\frac{L^2(1+e)^2}{2\mu\lambda^2}-\frac{GM\mu(1+e)}{\lambda}
		&=\frac{L^2(1-e)^2}{2\mu\lambda^2}-\frac{GM\mu(1-e)}{\lambda} \nonumber\\
		\frac{L^2\big[(1+e)^2-(1-e)^2\big]}{2\mu\lambda^2}&\eqby{1}\frac{GM\mu\big[(1+e)-(1-e)\big]}{\lambda} \nonumber\\
		\frac{2eL^2}{\mu\lambda^2}&\eqby{2}\frac{2eGM\mu}{\lambda}.
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item Collect the $L^2$ terms on the left and the $GM\mu$ terms on the right.
		\item $(1+e)^2-(1-e)^2=4e$.
	\end{explain}
	So $L^2=GM\mu^2\lambda$, the definition of $\lambda$ again. The two turning points agree, as they must. With $\lambda=a(1-e^2)$ the angular momentum in terms of $a$ and $e$ is
	\begin{equation}
		L=\sqrt{GM\mu^2\lambda}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad L^2=GM\mu^2 a(1-e^2), \label{eq:L_of_ae}
	\end{equation}
	and, with the direction $\hat L$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:Lhat},
	\[
	\vecL = \sqrt{GM\mu^2\lambda}\,\big(\sin\Omega\sin\iota\,\hat\alpha-\cos\Omega\sin\iota\,\hat\delta+\cos\iota\,\hat R\big).
	\]

	\subsection{Instantaneous kinetic energy}
	\label{sec:vis_viva}
	From $E=K+V$ and Eq.~\eqref{eq:visviva_E},
	\begin{equation}
		K=\tfrac12\mu\dot{\vecr}^2 = E-V= GM\mu\left(\dfrac1r-\dfrac1{2a}\right),
		\qquad\text{or}\qquad
		|\dot{\vecr}|^2=GM\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right).
		\label{tb:eq:visviva_speed}
	\end{equation}
	This is the \emph{vis-viva} equation. The speed at any point depends only on the distance and on $a$. The kinetic energy is shared between the two bodies through $\vecx_1=(m_2/M)\vecr$ and $\vecx_2=-(m_1/M)\vecr$,
	\[
	K_1=\tfrac12 m_1|\dot{\vecx}_1|^2=\tfrac12 m_1\frac{m_2^2}{M^2}\dot{\vecr}^2=\frac{m_2}{M}\cdot\tfrac12\mu\dot{\vecr}^2=\frac{m_2}{M}K,
	\qquad K_2=\frac{m_1}{M}K,
	\]
	using $\mu=m_1m_2/M$. The lighter body carries the larger share.

	\paragraph{Inverting for $a,e$.} Equations \eqref{eq:visviva_E} and \eqref{eq:L_of_ae} give the orbital elements directly from the conserved energy and angular momentum. The first gives $a=-GM\mu/(2E)$. Then
	\begin{equation}
		e^2=1-\frac{L^2}{GM\mu^2a}\eqby{1}1+\frac{2EL^2}{(GM)^2\mu^3},
	\end{equation}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $1/a=-2E/(GM\mu)$.
	\end{explain}
	so
	\begin{equation}
		a=-\frac{GM\mu}{2E},\qquad e=\sqrt{1+\frac{2EL^2}{(GM)^2\mu^3}}. \label{eq:a_e_from_EL}
	\end{equation}
	
	\section{True and Eccentric Anomaly; Kepler's Equation}
	\label{tb:sec:anomalies}

	We have the shape $r(\phi)$ and the size of the orbit from its energy.

	The shape alone does not say where the body is at a given time, and that is what an observer needs to point a telescope or predict an eclipse.

	Draw a clock face on a rubber sheet and stretch the sheet in one direction. The hour marks move, and the angles measured on the stretched sheet are no longer $30^\circ$ apart. But each mark can still be named by its old angle on the unstretched face, and with that name the arithmetic stays simple.

	An ellipse is a circle of radius $a$ squashed by the factor $b/a$ across its major axis. A point on the ellipse at $(a\cos u,\,b\sin u)$ from the centre is named by the angle $u$ on that circle, the \textbf{eccentric anomaly}. The idea is that with $r=a(1-e\cos u)$ the time integral of the orbit becomes elementary.

	The ellipse in numbers. For $e=0.5$ and $u=90^\circ$, the body is at $r=a(1-0)=a$. Its true anomaly follows from Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:cosv} below, $\cos v=(0-0.5)/(1-0)=-0.5$, so $v=120^\circ$. Seen from the focus the body is $120^\circ$ round from periapsis, while on the circle it is $90^\circ$ round.

	The picture is the ellipse inside its circumscribed circle, with a line across the major axis joining the body to the circle and the angle $u$ drawn at the centre.

	The same parameter $x=a\cos u$, $y=b\sin u$ describes any ellipse in geometry, and in calculus the same substitution removes the square root of a quadratic from an integral. For a hyperbola $\cosh$ and $\sinh$ take the place of $\cos$ and $\sin$. The integrand comes from $E$ and $L$ (P1). The result is the time $t(u)$, the true anomaly $v(u)$, and the same for hyperbolic orbits.

	\paragraph{The time integral.} We now want $r(t)$, and hence $\phi(t)$, not just $r(\phi)$. Solving the energy \eqref{tb:eq:energy_polar} for $\dot r$,
	\begin{align}
	\dot r &\eqby{1} \sqrt{\frac{2}{\mu}\left(E-\frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}+\frac{GM\mu}{r}\right)} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \sqrt{-\frac{GM}{a}-\frac{GM\lambda}{r^2}+\frac{2GM}{r}}
	\eqby{3}\sqrt{GM}\,\frac1r\left(2r-\frac{r^2}{a}-\lambda\right)^{1/2}.
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:energy_polar}, on the outbound half of the orbit where $\dot r>0$.
		\item $E=-GM\mu/(2a)$ and $L^2=GM\mu^2\lambda$, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:visviva_E} and~\eqref{eq:L_of_ae}.
		\item Take $GM/r^2$ out of the square root.
	\end{explain}
	We separate the variables and integrate from the periapsis, which the body passes at the time $t_0$ with $r=a(1-e)$,
	\begin{align}
		t-t_0&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{GM}}\int_{a(1-e)}^{r}\frac{r'\,dr'}{\left(2r'-r'^2/a-\lambda\right)^{1/2}} \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{1}\sqrt{\frac{a}{GM}}\int_{a(1-e)}^{r}\frac{r'dr'}{\sqrt{a^2e^2-(r'-a)^2}}.
		\label{tb:eq:time_integral}
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item Multiply the bracket by $a$. With $\lambda=a(1-e^2)$, $2ar-r^2-a^2(1-e^2)=a^2e^2-(r-a)^2$.
	\end{explain}
	\subsection{Elliptical orbits: eccentric anomaly}
	For $a>0$ we substitute
	\begin{equation}
		r=a\big(1-e\cos u\big), \label{eq:r_of_u}
	\end{equation}
	where $u$ is the \textbf{eccentric anomaly}. This choice keeps $r>0$ throughout, since $-1\le\cos u\le1$, and it makes the square root a perfect square,
	\begin{align}
		t-t_0
		&\eqby{1}\sqrt{\frac{a}{GM}}\int_0^u \frac{a(1-e\cos u')\,ae\sin u'\,du'}{ae\sin u'} \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{2}\frac{a^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GM}}\int_0^u (1-e\cos u')\,du' = \frac{a^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GM}}\big[u-e\sin u\big].
		\label{tb:eq:t_of_u}
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $dr=ae\sin u\,du$, and $a^2e^2-(r-a)^2=a^2e^2-a^2e^2\cos^2u=a^2e^2\sin^2u$, whose root is $ae\sin u$ for $0<u<\pi$. The periapsis $r=a(1-e)$ is $u=0$.
		\item Cancel $ae\sin u'$.
	\end{explain}
	On the inbound half, $\pi<u<2\pi$, both $\dot r$ and $\sin u$ change sign, so the same formula holds for all $u$. The time $t_0$ of periapsis passage, where $u=0$, is the sixth and final constant of integration.
	
	\paragraph{Relating the true anomaly $v$ and the eccentric anomaly $u$.}
	The true anomaly $v=\phi-\omega$ gives $r=\lambda/(1+e\cos v)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:conic}. Equating this with Eq.~\eqref{eq:r_of_u},
	\begin{align}
	1+e\cos v&\eqby{1}\frac{1-e^2}{1-e\cos u} \nonumber\\
	e\cos v&=\frac{1-e^2-1+e\cos u}{1-e\cos u}=\frac{e(\cos u-e)}{1-e\cos u},
	\qquad\text{so}\qquad
	\cos v = \frac{\cos u - e}{1-e\cos u}. \label{tb:eq:cosv}
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $a(1-e\cos u)=a(1-e^2)/(1+e\cos v)$, with $\lambda=a(1-e^2)$.
	\end{explain}
	The sine follows from $\sin^2v=1-\cos^2v$,
	\begin{align}
		\sin^2v&=\frac{(1-e\cos u)^2-(\cos u-e)^2}{(1-e\cos u)^2}
		\eqby{1}\frac{(1-e^2)(1-\cos^2u)}{(1-e\cos u)^2},
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item The numerator is $1-2e\cos u+e^2\cos^2u-\cos^2u+2e\cos u-e^2=(1-e^2)-(1-e^2)\cos^2u$.
	\end{explain}
	and $v$ and $u$ lie in the same half of the orbit, so the positive root is taken where $\sin u>0$. Together,
	\begin{equation}
		\sin v = \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin u}{1-e\cos u},\qquad
		\tan v = \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin u}{\cos u - e},\qquad
		\tan\frac v2 = \sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\,\tan\frac u2. \label{eq:v_of_u}
	\end{equation}
	The half-angle form is proved in Problem~4. At $u=0$ and $u=\pi$ we get $v=u$. The periapsis and the apoapsis are the same points for the two anomalies, as they must be by symmetry. Multiplying $\cos v$ and $\sin v$ by $r=a(1-e\cos u)$ gives the position in the orbital plane, with $x$ towards the periapsis,
	\begin{equation}
		r\cos v=a(\cos u-e),\qquad r\sin v=a\sqrt{1-e^2}\,\sin u .
		\label{tb:eq:xy_of_u}
	\end{equation}
	This is the point $(a\cos u,\,b\sin u)$ of the opening, measured from the focus at $(ae,0)$.

	\subsection{Hyperbolic trajectories}
	For $a<0$ and $e>1$, the matching substitution is $r=a(1-e\cosh u)=(-a)(e\cosh u-1)$. Since $\cosh u\ge1$ and $a<0$, it keeps $r>0$. Multiplying the bracket of the time integral by $-a>0$ instead of $a$,
	\begin{align}
		t-t_0
		&\eqby{1}\sqrt{\frac{-a}{GM}}\int_{-a(e-1)}^{r}\frac{r'dr'}{\sqrt{(r'-a)^2-a^2e^2}} \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{2}\frac{(-a)^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GM}}\int_0^u(e\cosh u'-1)\,du'
		=\frac{(-a)^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GM}}\big[e\sinh u - u\big].
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $-a\big(2r-r^2/a-\lambda\big)=(r-a)^2-a^2e^2$, with $\lambda=a(1-e^2)$. The periapsis is at $r=-a(e-1)$.
		\item $dr=(-a)e\sinh u\,du$, and $(r-a)^2-a^2e^2=a^2e^2(\cosh^2u-1)=a^2e^2\sinh^2u$, whose root is $(-a)e\sinh u$ for $u>0$.
	\end{explain}
	This is the \textbf{hyperbolic} anomaly relation. The true anomaly follows as for the ellipse, with $\cosh u$ in place of $\cos u$. Equating $\lambda/(1+e\cos v)$ with $a(1-e\cosh u)$ gives $\cos v=(\cosh u-e)/(1-e\cosh u)$. Then $1-\cos^2v=(e^2-1)\sinh^2u/(e\cosh u-1)^2$, and the half-angle form follows from
	\[
	\frac{1-\cos v}{1+\cos v}=\frac{(e+1)(\cosh u-1)}{(e-1)(\cosh u+1)}=\frac{e+1}{e-1}\tanh^2\frac u2 .
	\]
	The true-anomaly relations are
	\begin{equation}
		\cos v = \frac{e-\cosh u}{e\cosh u - 1},\qquad
		\sin v = \frac{\sqrt{e^2-1}\sinh u}{e\cosh u-1},\qquad
		\tan\frac v2=\sqrt{\frac{e+1}{e-1}}\tanh\frac u2. \label{eq:v_of_u_hyp}
	\end{equation}
	
	\section{Kepler's Third Law}
	\label{tb:sec:kepler3}

	We have the time $t(u)$ along an elliptical orbit, Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:t_of_u}.

	What we usually measure first for a binary or a planet is the period, the one time scale of the orbit.

	A pendulum clock is set by the length of its pendulum alone. A longer pendulum swings more slowly, in a fixed way, whatever the size of its swing.

	An orbit is set the same way by its semi-major axis alone. The idea is that one full turn of $u$ is one period, and the eccentricity drops out.

	The Earth in numbers. With $a=1\,\mathrm{AU}$ and $GM_\odot=1.327\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$, the period $2\pi\sqrt{a^3/GM}$ below is $365.26$ days. Mars, at $a=1.524\,\mathrm{AU}$, takes $1.524^{3/2}=1.881$ years.

	The picture is two ellipses with the same major axis, one round and one thin, that are run round in the same time.

	The same law weighs the Earth from the Moon's orbit, the stars of a binary (Problem~9) and the dark halo of a galaxy (Problem~10). With zero angular momentum it gives the free-fall time of a collapsing cloud (Section~\ref{sg:sec:freefall}). It combines P1 and P2. It gives the mean motion $n$ and $n^2a^3=GM$.

	\paragraph{The period.} In one complete orbit $u$ advances by $2\pi$, while $t$ advances by the orbital period $P$. We define the \textbf{mean motion} $n\equiv2\pi/P$, the mean angular rate. From Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:t_of_u},
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{2\pi}{n} = \frac{a^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GM}}\big[2\pi - e\sin(2\pi)\big] = \frac{a^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GM}}\cdot2\pi
		\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
		\boxed{n=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a^3}},\qquad n^2a^3=GM.} \label{eq:kepler3}
	\end{equation}
	The period depends on $a$ and the total mass $M$ only. It does not depend on $e$ or on how $M$ is split between the two bodies.

	\section{Kepler's Equation}
	\label{sec:kepler_equation}

	We have $t$ as a function of $u$, and the mean motion $n$.

	An observer has the time $t$ and needs the position, so the relation must be run the other way.

	Two runners start together on a circular track. One keeps a steady pace. The other speeds up on one side of the track and slows down on the other, and both finish the lap together. The steady runner's position is easy to give at any time, and the other's position can be named by how far ahead or behind the steady one she is.

	For an orbit the steady runner is a fictitious body moving round at the mean rate $n$, like the mean Sun of Chapter~\ref{ch:time}. Its angle is the \textbf{mean anomaly} $\ell=n(t-t_0)$. The idea is that Kepler's equation ties $\ell$ to the real body's eccentric anomaly $u$.

	The runners in numbers. For $e=0.5$, a quarter of a period after periapsis the mean anomaly is $\ell=90^\circ$. Solving $u-0.5\sin u=\pi/2$ gives $u=115.8^\circ$, and Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:cosv} gives $v=140.2^\circ$. The real body is $50^\circ$ ahead of the mean one.

	The picture is the steady runner and the real one on the same track, the real one ahead in the first half of the lap and behind in the second.

	The same mean body gives the equation of time in Chapter~\ref{ch:time}. Nothing new enters beyond P1, through Kepler's second law. It gives Kepler's equation for ellipses and hyperbolas.

	\paragraph{Mean anomaly.} Defining the \textbf{mean anomaly} $\ell\equiv n(t-t_0)$,
	\begin{equation}
		\ell \eqby{1} n\,\frac{a^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GM}}\,(u-e\sin u) \eqby{2} u-e\sin u ,
	\end{equation}
	\begin{explain}
		\item Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:t_of_u}.
		\item $n=\sqrt{GM/a^3}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kepler3}.
	\end{explain}
	so that
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{\ell = u - e\sin u} \qquad\text{(elliptical Kepler's equation).} \label{eq:kepler_eq}
	\end{equation}
	This transcendental equation must be solved for $u(\ell)$, that is $u(t)$, numerically (Problem~3) or by a series (Section~\ref{sec:bessel}). The hyperbolic analogue, with $n\equiv\sqrt{GM/(-a)^3}$, is
	\begin{equation}
		\ell = e\sinh u - u. \label{eq:kepler_eq_hyp}
	\end{equation}
	
	\section{Keplerian Parametrization}
	\label{sec:parametrization}
	
	With Kepler's equation the position follows at any time. We collect the formulas, then add the rates $\dot u$, $\dot r$ and $\dot\phi$ that the perturbation theory and the timing of binaries need.

	\subsection{Elliptical orbits}
	Collecting Eqs.~\eqref{eq:r_of_u}, \eqref{eq:v_of_u} and~\eqref{eq:kepler_eq},
	\begin{align}
		r&=a(1-e\cos u), & \phi&=v+\omega,\\
		\tan\frac v2 &= \sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan\frac u2, & \ell&=u-e\sin u.
	\end{align}

	Differentiating Kepler's equation \eqref{eq:kepler_eq} in $t$ gives $n=\dot u(1-e\cos u)$, since $\dot\ell=n$. So
	\begin{equation}
		\dot u = \frac{n}{1-e\cos u}. \label{eq:udot}
	\end{equation}
	Differentiating $r=a(1-e\cos u)$,
	\begin{equation}
		\dot r = ae\sin u\,\dot u = \frac{an\,e\sin u}{1-e\cos u}. \label{eq:rdot_u}
	\end{equation}
	For $\dot\phi$ we need $dv/du$. Differentiating $\cos v$ from Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:cosv},
	\begin{align}
		-\sin v\,\frac{dv}{du}
		&\eqby{1}\frac{-\sin u\,(1-e\cos u)-(\cos u-e)\,e\sin u}{(1-e\cos u)^2}
		=-\frac{(1-e^2)\sin u}{(1-e\cos u)^2}, \nonumber\\
		\frac{dv}{du}&\eqby{2}\frac{(1-e^2)\sin u}{(1-e\cos u)^2}\cdot\frac{1-e\cos u}{\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin u}
		=\frac{\sqrt{1-e^2}}{1-e\cos u}.
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item The quotient rule.
		\item Divide by $-\sin v$, with $\sin v$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:v_of_u}.
	\end{explain}
	So
	\begin{equation}
		\dot\phi = \dot v = \frac{dv}{du}\dot u = \frac{n\sqrt{1-e^2}}{(1-e\cos u)^2}. \label{eq:phidot_u}
	\end{equation}
	As a check, Eq.~\eqref{eq:phidot} gives $\dot\phi=L/(\mu r^2)=\sqrt{GMa(1-e^2)}/\big[a^2(1-e\cos u)^2\big]$, which is the same since $\sqrt{GM/a^3}=n$.

	\subsection{Hyperbolic trajectories}
	In the same way, with $r=-a(e\cosh u - 1)$, $\phi=v+\omega$, $\tan(v/2)=\sqrt{(e+1)/(e-1)}\tanh(u/2)$ and $\ell=e\sinh u - u$,
	\begin{equation}
		\dot u = \frac{n}{e\cosh u - 1},\quad
		\dot r = -an\,\frac{e\sinh u}{e\cosh u - 1},\quad
		\dot\phi=\dot v = \frac{n\sqrt{e^2-1}}{(e\cosh u-1)^2}. \label{eq:hyp_derivs}
	\end{equation}
	The first is the derivative of $\ell=e\sinh u-u$, the second is $\dot r=(-a)e\sinh u\,\dot u$, and the third uses $dv/du=\sqrt{e^2-1}/(e\cosh u-1)$, found from $\cos v$ as for the ellipse.

	\section{Hyperbolic-Trajectory Parameters}
	\label{tb:sec:hyperbolic}

	A body that passes a planet or a star once, on an unbound orbit with $e>1$, is described by a few numbers read off the hyperbola. We find the speed far away, the angle of the asymptotes, the closest approach, the miss distance and the angle of the velocity. Problem~12 uses them for a flyby of Jupiter.

	\textbf{Hyperbolic excess speed} $v_\infty$. Far away $r\to\infty$ and $V\to0$, so the energy is all kinetic. From $\tfrac12\mu\dot{\vecr}_\infty^2 = E=-GM\mu/(2a)$, with $a<0$,
	\begin{equation}
		v_\infty\equiv|\dot{\vecr}_\infty| = \sqrt{-\frac{GM}{a}}. \label{eq:vinf}
	\end{equation}

	\textbf{Asymptote angle} $\theta_\infty$. As $u\to\infty$ the body runs off to infinity at the true anomaly $v_{\rm asy}$, with $\cos v_{\rm asy}=\lim(e-\cosh u)/(e\cosh u-1)=-1/e$. The asymptote makes the angle $\theta_\infty=\pi-v_{\rm asy}$ with the axis on the far side from the periapsis, which is half the angle between the two asymptotes,
	\begin{equation}
		\cos\theta_\infty = -\lim_{u\to\infty}\frac{e-\cosh u}{e\cosh u -1} = \frac1e. \label{eq:scattering}
	\end{equation}
	The body is turned by the \textbf{deflection angle} $\delta=\pi-2\theta_\infty$, so $\sin(\delta/2)=\cos\theta_\infty=1/e$.

	\textbf{Periapsis distance}. The smallest $r$ is at $v=0$,
	\begin{equation}
		r_{\rm p} = \min_v \frac{a(1-e^2)}{1+e\cos v} =\frac{a(1-e^2)}{1+e}= -a(e-1). \label{eq:rp}
	\end{equation}

	\textbf{Semi-minor axis and impact parameter}. The semi-minor axis $b=-a\sqrt{e^2-1}$ satisfies $b^2=a^2(e^2-1)=-a\lambda$, since $\lambda=a(1-e^2)$. The impact parameter $b'$ is the distance from the focus to the incoming asymptote, the miss distance had there been no pull. The asymptote passes through the centre of the hyperbola, which is $-a+r_{\rm p}=-ae$ from the focus, and it makes the angle $\theta_\infty$ with the axis, so
	\[
	b' = (-a+r_{\rm p})\sin\theta_\infty = -ae\sin\theta_\infty \eqby{1} -ae\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{e^2}} = -a\sqrt{e^2-1} = b,
	\]
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\cos\theta_\infty=1/e$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:scattering}.
	\end{explain}
	so the impact parameter and the semi-minor axis are the same length.

	\textbf{Flight-path angle} $\psi$, the angle between $\dot{\vecr}$ and the in-plane direction $\phihat$ perpendicular to $\rhat$,
	\begin{equation}
		\sin\psi = \frac{\dot{\vecr}\cdot\rhat}{|\dot{\vecr}|}=\frac{\dot r}{\sqrt{\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2}}. \label{eq:flightpath}
	\end{equation}
	It is zero at the periapsis, where $\dot r=0$.

	\section{Fourier Solution of Kepler's Equation}
	\label{sec:bessel}

	Kepler's equation gives $\ell$ from $u$ in one line, but going from $\ell$ to $u$ takes an iteration (Problem~3).

	For work by hand, in perturbation theory or for small $e$, we want $u$ as an explicit function of $\ell$.

	A musician who hears a steady beat that is slightly uneven can describe it as the beat plus a few overtones. Anything that repeats can be written as a sum of sines of the repeat frequency and its multiples.

	The difference $u-\ell=e\sin u$ repeats every orbit, and it changes sign with $\ell$. So it is a sum of $\sin(s\ell)$. The idea is to find the coefficients by changing the variable of the integral from $\ell$ to $u$, where Kepler's equation makes it simple.

	The series in numbers. For $e=0.1$, the first term below is $2J_1(0.1)\sin\ell=0.0999\sin\ell$, close to $e\sin\ell$. The second is $J_2(0.2)\sin2\ell=0.0050\sin2\ell$, close to $\tfrac12e^2\sin2\ell$. The terms fall off as powers of $e$.

	The picture is the straight line $u=\ell$ with a small wave on top of it, one wave per orbit.

	The same Bessel functions give the sidebands of a frequency-modulated radio signal and the diffraction pattern of a circular aperture (Chapter~\ref{ch:resolution}). Here they come from pure analysis, with $e$ as the small number (P6). The result is $u(\ell)$ as a series.

	\paragraph{The coefficients.} Kepler's equation $u-\ell=e\sin u$ is solved as a Fourier series in $\ell$,
	\[
	e\sin u = \sum_{s=1}^{\infty}c_s\sin(s\ell).
	\]
	The function $u(\ell)-\ell$ is odd and has period $2\pi$, and it vanishes at $\ell=0$ and $\ell=\pi$, where $u=\ell$. So
	\begin{align}
		c_s
		&\eqby{1} \frac2\pi\int_0^\pi (u-\ell)\sin(s\ell)\,d\ell \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{2} \frac{2}{s\pi}\Big[-(u-\ell)\cos(s\ell)\Big]_0^\pi+\frac{2}{s\pi}\int_0^\pi\cos(s\ell)\left(\frac{du}{d\ell}-1\right)d\ell \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{3} \frac{2}{s\pi}\int_0^\pi\cos(s\ell)\,du \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{4} \frac{2}{s\pi}\int_0^\pi \cos(su-se\sin u)\,du \eqby{5} \frac{2}{s}J_s(se).
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item The Fourier sine coefficient of an odd function of period $2\pi$.
		\item Integration by parts.
		\item The boundary term vanishes since $u=\ell$ at both ends. $\int_0^\pi\cos(s\ell)\,d\ell=0$ for a whole number $s\ge1$. Then $(du/d\ell)\,d\ell=du$, and $u$ runs from $0$ to $\pi$ as $\ell$ does.
		\item $\ell=u-e\sin u$, Kepler's equation.
		\item The integral form of the Bessel function of the first kind, $J_s(x)=\tfrac1\pi\int_0^\pi\cos(s\tau-x\sin\tau)\,d\tau$.
	\end{explain}
	So
	\begin{equation}
		u(\ell) = \ell + \sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{s}J_s(se)\sin(s\ell). \label{eq:kepler_series}
	\end{equation}
	This is the classical Bessel-function series solution of Kepler's equation. Since $J_s(se)\sim(se/2)^s/s!$ for small $e$, the $s$-th term is of order $e^s$.
	
	\section{The Laplace--Runge--Lenz Vector}
	\label{tb:sec:runge_lenz}
	
	The energy and the angular momentum fix four of the six elements, $a$, $e$, $\iota$ and $\Omega$.
	
	They do not say where the periapsis points within the plane. The angle $\omega$ came out of solving the orbit equation, not from anything conserved. Yet the ellipse of a $1/r$ potential does not turn, so something must hold its long axis in place.
	
	A marble rolling round the inside of a salad bowl traces a loop that slowly turns, a rosette. In a bowl where the restoring force is exactly proportional to the distance from the centre, the loop closes on itself and its long axis stays put. Energy and angular momentum are the same in both bowls, so in the second something more is conserved.
	
	The Kepler ellipse does not turn either. The idea is that a conserved vector $\vecA$ points from the focus to the periapsis, and its direction gives $\omega$.
	
	The Earth in numbers. Its orbit has $e=0.0167$. In units of $GM\mu^2$ the vector $\vecA$ is an arrow of length $0.0167$ pointing to the perihelion, which the Earth passes in early January.
	
	The picture is an arrow from the focus to the periapsis that stays fixed while the body goes round.
	
	In the hydrogen atom the same vector, made into an operator, explains why states of equal principal quantum number have equal energy. A small extra force turns $\vecA$ slowly, which is the precession of an orbit (Section~\ref{sec:perturbed_orbits}). Its conservation is P1 together with the $1/r^2$ law (P2). It gives a fifth conserved quantity, its direction and size, and a count of the new information it carries.
	
	\paragraph{Conservation.} Define $\vecA \equiv \vecp\times\vecL - GM\mu^2\rhat$, with $\vecp=\mu\dot{\vecr}$. For the pure Kepler problem,
	\begin{align}
	\dot{\vecA}
	&\eqby{1} \dot{\vecp}\times\vecL - GM\mu^2\dot{\rhat} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} -\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}L\big(\rhat\times\hat L\big) - GM\mu^2\dot\phi\,\phihat \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \frac{GM\mu L}{r^2}\,\phihat - \frac{GM\mu L}{r^2}\,\phihat=0 .
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item The product rule, with $\dot{\vecL}=0$.
		\item $\dot{\vecp}=-GM\mu\rhat/r^2$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:relEOM}, $\vecL=L\hat L$, and $\dot{\rhat}=\dot\phi\,\phihat$ from Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:unit_vector_rates}.
		\item $(\rhat,\phihat,\hat L)$ is right-handed, so $\rhat\times\hat L=-\phihat$. And $\dot\phi=L/(\mu r^2)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:phidot}.
	\end{explain}
	So $\vecA$ is conserved for the unperturbed problem. It is a constant of the Kepler problem beyond $E$ and $\vecL$, and it reflects an extra hidden symmetry of the $1/r$ potential. For bound orbits, $\vecL$ and a rescaled $\vecA$ together generate the rotation group $SO(4)$ of four dimensions.
	
	\paragraph{Direction and magnitude of $\vecA$.} The vector $\vecA$ lies in the orbital plane, since $\vecA\cdot\vecL=(\vecp\times\vecL)\cdot\vecL-GM\mu^2\rhat\cdot\vecL=0$. So we may write $\vecA=A_r\rhat+A_{\phi}\phihat$.
	
	\begin{figure}[htbp]
		\centering
		\includegraphics{fig_ch6_orbital_elements_3d.pdf}
		\caption{An orbit in its plane, with the focus $F$ at the origin and the body at periapsis $P$. The unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf r}$, $\hat{\mathbf p}$ and $\hat{\mathbf L}$ are drawn at $P$, where $\hat{\mathbf p}\times\hat{\mathbf L}=\hat{\mathbf r}$.}
		\label{tb:fig:lrl_periapsis}
	\end{figure}
	At periapsis the velocity is purely tangential, $\vecp\perp\rhat$. Then
	\[
	\rhat\times\vecA\big|_{\rm peri}=\rhat\times(\vecp\times\vecL)\big|_{\rm peri}=\big[\vecp\,(\rhat\cdot\vecL)-\vecL\,(\rhat\cdot\vecp)\big]_{\rm peri}=0,
	\]
	because both dot products vanish there. So $\vecA$ is parallel to $\rhat$ at periapsis (Figure~\ref{tb:fig:lrl_periapsis}). To find which way it points, and how long it is, we dot $\vecA$ into $\vecr$,
	\begin{align}
		\vecA\cdot\vecr &= (\vecp\times\vecL)\cdot\vecr - GM\mu^2 r \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{1}\epsilon_{kij}\,p_i L_j \,r_k-GM\mu^2 r\eqby{2}\epsilon_{jki}\,r_kp_i\,L_j-GM\mu^2 r \nonumber\\
		&= \underbrace{(\vecr\times\vecp)}_{\vecL}\cdot\vecL - GM\mu^2 r = L^2 - GM\mu^2 r .
		\label{tb:eq:A_dot_r}
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $(\vecp\times\vecL)_k=\epsilon_{kij}\,p_iL_j$, summed over repeated indices.
		\item The cyclic symmetry $\epsilon_{kij}=\epsilon_{jki}$, and $(\vecr\times\vecp)_j=\epsilon_{jki}\,r_kp_i$.
	\end{explain}
	At periapsis $r=\lambda/(1+e)$ and $L^2=GM\mu^2\lambda$, so $\vecA\cdot\vecr=GM\mu^2\lambda\,e/(1+e)>0$ for $e>0$. Hence $\vecA$ points from the focus \emph{towards the periapsis}, and the angle between $\vecA$ and $\vecr$ is the true anomaly $v$. Writing $\vecA\cdot\vecr = Ar\cos v$ and using
	\[
	r = \frac{L^2/(GM\mu^2)}{1+e\cos v}\implies GM\mu^2 r = \frac{L^2}{1+e\cos v},
	\]
	we find
	\begin{align}
		Ar\cos v&=L^2 - GM\mu^2 r \nonumber\\
		A\, \frac{L^2/(GM\mu^2)}{1+e\cos v}\,\cos v&\eqby{1}L^2\left(1-\frac{1}{1+e\cos v}\right)=\frac{L^2e\cos v}{1+e\cos v}.
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item The conic \eqref{eq:conic} for $r$ on the left, and $GM\mu^2r=L^2/(1+e\cos v)$ on the right.
	\end{explain}
	Cancelling $L^2\cos v/(1+e\cos v)$ on both sides,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{A = GM\mu^2 e.} \label{eq:A_magnitude}
	\end{equation}
	A second route runs the other way. Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:A_dot_r} with $\vecA\cdot\vecr=Ar\cos v$ is $r=L^2/(GM\mu^2+A\cos v)=\lambda/\big(1+(A/GM\mu^2)\cos v\big)$. This is the conic \eqref{eq:conic} again, found from two conserved vectors without solving any differential equation.
	
	The periapsis is at the polar angle $\omega$, so by Eq.~\eqref{eq:rhat_phihat},
	\begin{equation}
		\hat A = \cos\omega\,\hatn+\sin\omega\,\hat y. \label{eq:Ahat}
	\end{equation}
	Equivalently, the \textbf{eccentricity vector}
	\begin{equation}
		\vec e \equiv \frac{\vecA}{GM\mu^2} = e\hat A=e_x\,\hatn+e_y\,\hat y,
		\qquad e_x\equiv e\cos\omega,\quad e_y\equiv e\sin\omega,
		\label{tb:eq:ecc_vector}
	\end{equation}
	is a conserved vector that holds $(e,\omega)$ together. Since $e$, $\Omega$ and $\iota$ are already fixed by $(E,\vecL)$, the only \emph{new} information in $\vecA$ is $\omega$, the direction of the periapsis from the ascending node.
	\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable,title={How many new constants $\vecA$ gives}]
		The three components of $\vecA$ obey two conditions set by $E$ and $\vecL$. One is $\vecA\cdot\vecL=0$. The other fixes its length, since
		\[
		|\vecA|^2=G^2M^2\mu^4e^2\eqby{1}G^2M^2\mu^4\left(1+\frac{2EL^2}{G^2M^2\mu^3}\right)=G^2M^2\mu^4+2\mu EL^2,
		\]
		where (1) is Eq.~\eqref{eq:a_e_from_EL}. So $\vecA$ supplies one new constant, not three. With $E$ (one), $\vecL$ (three), $\vecA$ (one) and $t_0$ (one) the count of six for the relative orbit closes.
	\end{tcolorbox}
	
	\section{Perturbed Orbits}
	\label{sec:perturbed_orbits}
	
	The Kepler orbit is exact for two point masses alone in space.
	
	Real orbits feel other forces as well. The other planets tug on each one, a flattened planet pulls its satellites off the inverse square, gas drags on low satellites, an engine pushes a spacecraft, and general relativity adds a small extra pull. The orbit is then no longer a fixed ellipse.
	
	A navigator on a ship in a slow current plots, each hour, the course the ship would follow in still water from where it is now and how it is moving now. The current shifts that course a little from one hour to the next. The ship's true track is a chain of these still-water courses, each one touching the track for a moment.
	
	A planet tugged by the others is the ship. At each instant it moves on some Kepler ellipse that fits its present position and velocity, and the elements of that ellipse drift. The idea is to describe the true path by a Kepler ellipse whose elements change, at rates set by the extra force.
	
	Mercury in numbers. Its perihelion turns by about $575''$ per century against the stars. About $532''$ of this comes from the pull of the other planets and $43''$ from general relativity. Mercury makes about $415$ orbits in a century, so the ellipse turns by about $1.4''$ per orbit, while its size and shape barely change.
	
	The picture is an ellipse that turns slowly, so that the path traced over many orbits is a rosette.
	
	The same method is the variation of constants for a forced oscillator, and in quantum mechanics it is the interaction picture, where the constants of the free motion change slowly under a weak perturbation. The rates come from conservation (P1). That the extra force is small, and that its effects can be ordered by powers of it, is P6. It gives exact rates for $E$, $\vecL$ and $\vecA$, and the osculating orbit that turns them into rates of the elements.
	
	\paragraph{Equation of motion.} We add a force $\vec F$ to the relative equation of motion,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\mu\ddot{\vecr}
			=
			-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\rhat+\vec F .
		}
		\label{eq:perturbed_eom}
	\end{equation}
	The central Newtonian term defines the Kepler problem. The \textbf{perturbing force} $\vec F$ need not be central.
	
	The momentum is $\vecp=\mu\dot{\vecr}$. In the orbital plane, with $\dot{\vecr}=\dot r\,\rhat+r\dot\phi\,\phihat$ and $L=\mu r^2\dot\phi$,
	\begin{equation}
		\vecp
		=
		\mu\dot r\,\rhat+\frac{L}{r}\,\phihat,
		\qquad
		L=\mu r^2\dot\phi .
		\label{eq:polar_momentum}
	\end{equation}
	
	Orbital perturbation theory represents the actual trajectory by Keplerian orbital elements that vary with time, and derives their rates directly from Eq.~\eqref{eq:perturbed_eom}.
	\subsection{Osculating Kepler orbit}
	\label{sec:osculating_orbit}

	We first fix which Kepler ellipse belongs to the true path at each instant. For $\vec F=0$, the Kepler solution can be written in terms of six
	constants
	\begin{equation}
		Q=(Q_1,\ldots,Q_6)
		=
		(a,e,\iota,\Omega,\omega,t_0),
	\end{equation}
	so that
	\begin{equation}
		\vecr=\vecr(Q,t),
		\qquad
		\vecp=\vecp(Q,t).
		\label{eq:kepler_phase_solution}
	\end{equation}
	For fixed $Q$, these functions satisfy the unperturbed equations of
	motion.
	
	For the perturbed problem, let
	\begin{equation}
		Q_i=Q_i(t).
	\end{equation}
	The actual trajectory is represented in terms of a set of
	time-dependent osculating orbital elements \(Q(t)\) as
	\begin{equation}
		\vecr(t)
		=
		\vecr_{\rm osc}\bigl(Q(t),t\bigr),
		\qquad
		\vecp(t)
		=
		\vecp_{\rm osc}\bigl(Q(t),t\bigr).
		\label{eq:perturbed_parameterization}
	\end{equation}
	Here, for fixed values of \(Q\), the functions
	\(\vecr_{\rm osc}(Q,t)\) and \(\vecp_{\rm osc}(Q,t)\) describe the
	corresponding Keplerian orbit.  The time dependence of \(Q(t)\) accounts
	for the slow evolution of this osculating Keplerian orbit under the
	perturbing force.
	
	At each instant, the osculating Kepler orbit is chosen to have the same
	position and momentum as the actual trajectory.  Thus,
	\begin{equation}
		\vecr_{\rm osc}\bigl(Q(t),t\bigr)=\vecr(t),
		\qquad
		\vecp_{\rm osc}\bigl(Q(t),t\bigr)=\vecp(t).
		\label{eq:osculating_state}
	\end{equation}
	This does not make the actual trajectory Keplerian. At each instant it is represented by the Keplerian orbit that touches it at that instant, the \textbf{osculating} orbit.
	
	The corresponding accelerations are different,
	\begin{align}
		\dot{\vecp}_{\rm actual}
		&=
		-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\rhat+\vec F,
		\\
		\dot{\vecp}_{\rm osc}
		&=
		-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\rhat.
	\end{align}
	Hence
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot{\vecp}_{\rm actual}
			-
			\dot{\vecp}_{\rm osc}
			=
			\vec F .
		}
		\label{eq:perturbation_acceleration_difference}
	\end{equation}
	
	The two trajectories therefore agree in position and momentum at the
	instant at which the osculating orbit is constructed, but they have
	different subsequent evolution.
	
	\subsection{Variation of parameters}
	
	The osculating condition becomes six equations for the six rates $\dot Q_i$. Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:perturbed_parameterization} by the chain rule,
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vecr}
		=
		\left.\pdv{\vecr}{t}\right|_Q
		+
		\sum_{i=1}^{6}
		\pdv{\vecr}{Q_i}\dot Q_i .
		\label{eq:r_chain_rule}
	\end{equation}
	For fixed $Q$, the first term is the velocity of the Kepler solution.
	The osculating condition requires this Kepler velocity to coincide with
	the actual velocity,
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vecr}
		=
		\left.\pdv{\vecr}{t}\right|_Q.
	\end{equation}
	Therefore
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\sum_{i=1}^{6}
			\pdv{\vecr}{Q_i}\dot Q_i=0 .
		}
		\label{eq:osculating_r_constraint}
	\end{equation}
	
	This is a vector equation and therefore supplies three scalar
	conditions.
	
	Now differentiate $\vecp(Q(t),t)$,
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vecp}
		=
		\left.\pdv{\vecp}{t}\right|_Q
		+
		\sum_{i=1}^{6}
		\pdv{\vecp}{Q_i}\dot Q_i .
		\label{eq:p_chain_rule}
	\end{equation}
	For fixed $Q$, $\vecp(Q,t)$ obeys the Kepler equation,
	\begin{equation}
		\left.\pdv{\vecp}{t}\right|_Q
		=
		-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\rhat .
	\end{equation}
	The actual momentum obeys
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vecp}
		=
		-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\rhat+\vec F.
	\end{equation}
	Subtracting the Kepler contribution gives
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\sum_{i=1}^{6}
			\pdv{\vecp}{Q_i}\dot Q_i
			=
			\vec F .
		}
		\label{eq:osculating_p_constraint}
	\end{equation}
	
	Equations \eqref{eq:osculating_r_constraint} and
	\eqref{eq:osculating_p_constraint} form six scalar equations for the
	six quantities $\dot Q_i$.  This is the formal variation-of-parameters
	construction of the osculating elements.
	
	Six equations for six unknowns, so the count closes. The perturbing force enters only through the change it makes in the instantaneous position and momentum, and the Kepler orbit is redefined at each instant from that state.
	
	\subsection{Perturbed Kepler invariants}
	
	The Keplerian energy, angular momentum and eccentricity vector are
	defined from the instantaneous state by
	\begin{align}
		E
		&\equiv
		\frac{p^2}{2\mu}-\frac{GM\mu}{r},
		\label{eq:pert_E_def}
		\\
		\vec L
		&\equiv
		\vecr\times\vecp,
		\label{eq:pert_L_def}
		\\
		\vec A
		&\equiv\vecp\times\vec L-GM\mu^2\,\rhat .\label{eq:pert_A_def}
	\end{align}
	For the unperturbed Kepler problem these are constants.  In the
	perturbed problem they become time-dependent quantities.
	
	Their magnitudes determine the instantaneous Keplerian parameters,
	\begin{align}
		E&=-\frac{GM\mu}{2a(t)},
		\label{eq:instantaneous_E_a}
		\\
		L^2&=GM\mu^2a(t)(1-e^2(t)),
		\label{eq:instantaneous_L_ae}
		\\
		A&\equiv|\vec A|=GM\mu^2e(t) .
		\label{eq:instantaneous_A_e}
	\end{align}
	
	Thus $a$ and $e$ may be regarded as functions of the actual state even
	when the full trajectory is not Keplerian.
	
	\subsection{Exact Rate of the Energy}
	
	We differentiate Eq.~\eqref{eq:pert_E_def} along the actual motion,
	\begin{align}
		\dot E
		&\eqby{1}
		\frac{\vecp}{\mu}\cdot\dot{\vecp}
		+
		\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\dot r \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{2}
		\dot{\vecr}\cdot
		\left(
		-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\rhat+\vec F
		\right)
		+
		\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\dot r \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{3}
		-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\dot r
		+\dot{\vecr}\cdot\vec F
		+\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\dot r
		=\dot{\vecr}\cdot\vec F .
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $d(p^2)/dt=2\vecp\cdot\dot{\vecp}$ and $d(1/r)/dt=-\dot r/r^2$.
		\item $\vecp/\mu=\dot{\vecr}$, and $\dot{\vecp}$ from the equation of motion \eqref{eq:perturbed_eom}.
		\item $\dot{\vecr}\cdot\rhat=\dot r$.
	\end{explain}
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot E=\dot{\vecr}\cdot\vec F .
		}
		\label{eq:perturbed_energy_rate}
	\end{equation}
	The Kepler energy changes only by the work done by the extra force.
	
	\subsection{Exact Rate of angular momentum}
	
	From $\vec L=\vecr\times\vecp$,
	\begin{align}
		\dot{\vec L}
		&\eqby{1}
		\dot{\vecr}\times\vecp
		+
		\vecr\times\dot{\vecp}
		\eqby{2}
		\vecr\times
		\left(
		-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\rhat+\vec F
		\right)
		\eqby{3}\vecr\times\vec F .
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item The product rule.
		\item $\dot{\vecr}\times\vecp=\mu\,\dot{\vecr}\times\dot{\vecr}=0$, and $\dot{\vecp}$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:perturbed_eom}.
		\item The central term vanishes because $\vecr\parallel\rhat$.
	\end{explain}
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot{\vec L}=\vecr\times\vec F .
		}
		\label{eq:perturbed_L_rate}
	\end{equation}
	Only the part of $\vec F$ perpendicular to $\vecr$ exerts a torque.
	
	\subsection{Exact Rate of the Runge--Lenz vector}
	
	We need one kinematic fact first. Since $\rhat=\vecr/r$,
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\rhat}
		=\frac{\dot{\vecr}}{r}-\frac{\dot r\,\vecr}{r^2}
		\eqby{1}\frac{r^2\dot{\vecr}-(\vecr\cdot\dot{\vecr})\,\vecr}{r^3}
		\eqby{2}\frac{(\vecr\times\dot{\vecr})\times\vecr}{r^3}
		=\frac{\vec L\times\vecr}{\mu r^3}.
		\label{tb:eq:rhat_dot_L}
	\end{equation}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $r\dot r=\vecr\cdot\dot{\vecr}$.
		\item $(\vec a\times\vec b)\times\vec c=\vec b\,(\vec a\cdot\vec c)-\vec a\,(\vec b\cdot\vec c)$.
	\end{explain}
	It uses only the definitions, so it holds with the perturbation on. Now we differentiate Eq.~\eqref{eq:pert_A_def},
	\begin{align}
		\dot{\vec A}
		&\eqby{1}
		\dot{\vecp}\times\vec L
		+
		\vecp\times\dot{\vec L}
		-
		GM\mu^2\,\dot{\rhat} \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{2}
		-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\rhat\times\vec L
		+
		\vec F\times\vec L
		+
		\vecp\times(\vecr\times\vec F)
		-
		GM\mu^2\,\dot{\rhat} \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{3}
		-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\rhat\times\vec L
		+
		\vec F\times\vec L
		+
		\vecp\times(\vecr\times\vec F)
		+\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\,\rhat\times\vec L \nonumber\\
		&=\vec F\times\vec L
		+
		\vecp\times(\vecr\times\vec F).
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item The product rule.
		\item $\dot{\vecp}$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:perturbed_eom} and $\dot{\vec L}=\vecr\times\vec F$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:perturbed_L_rate}.
		\item $-GM\mu^2\dot{\rhat}=-(GM\mu/r^2)\,\vec L\times\rhat=(GM\mu/r^2)\,\rhat\times\vec L$, from Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:rhat_dot_L} with $\vecr=r\rhat$.
	\end{explain}
	The two central terms cancel. Therefore
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot{\vec A}
			=
			\vec F\times\vec L
			+
			\vecp\times(\vecr\times\vec F).
		}
		\label{eq:perturbed_A_rate}
	\end{equation}
	
	This equation is the starting point for the evolution of the eccentricity and of the direction of the periapsis.
	
	\section{Planar perturbations}
	\label{tb:sec:planar_pert}
	
	We have exact rates for $E$, $\vecL$ and $\vecA$ under any extra force.
	
	To follow an orbit we need the rates of the elements $a$, $e$ and $\omega$ themselves, written with the parts of the force along and across the radius.
	
	Pushing a child on a swing adds energy only if the push is along the motion. A push at the top of the swing, where the child is at rest, does no work at all. When and in which direction we push decides what changes.
	
	An orbit is the same. A push along the motion changes the energy and so the size $a$. A push along the radius at the periapsis, where $\dot r=0$, does no work, but it still changes the shape and turns the ellipse. The idea is to split $\vec F$ into its radial and angular parts and read off $\dot a$, $\dot e$ and $\dot\omega$ from the exact rates. These are the planar \textbf{Gauss equations}.
	
	A thruster in numbers. A spacecraft on a circular orbit of radius $a=2.03\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{km}$ about the Earth fires an engine giving $0.2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ along its motion (Problem~8). Eq.~\eqref{eq:gauss_planar_a} below with $e=0$ gives $\dot a=2\,(F_\phi/\mu)\sqrt{a^3/GM_\oplus}=1.8\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so the orbit starts to grow by almost $2\,\mathrm{km}$ every second.
	
	The picture is the force arrow at one point of the orbit, split into a part along the radius and a part across it.
	
	The same equations follow satellite drag, the push of sunlight on a solar sail, and the slow shrinking of a binary pulsar's orbit as it radiates gravitational waves. They combine the conservation laws (P1) with the smallness of the force (P6). They give $\dot a$, $\dot e$, $\dot\omega$ and $\dot v$ for any force in the plane.
	
	\paragraph{Force in the plane.} We take a perturbing force confined to the orbital plane,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\vec F
			=
			F_r\rhat+F_{\phi}\phihat .
		}
		\label{eq:planar_force}
	\end{equation}
	Its torque is $\vecr\times\vec F=rF_\phi\,\rhat\times\phihat=rF_\phi\,\hat L$, along $\hat L$. So $\dot{\vecL}$ is parallel to $\vec L=L\hat L$, and only the length of $\vecL$ changes. The plane itself stays fixed,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot\iota=0,
			\qquad
			\dot\Omega=0.
		}
		\label{eq:planar_plane_fixed}
	\end{equation}
	
	In the orbital plane, by Eq.~\eqref{eq:polar_momentum},
	\begin{equation}
		\vecp
		=
		\mu\dot r\,\rhat+\mu r\dot\phi\,\phihat
		=
		\mu\dot r\,\rhat+\frac{L}{r}\phihat .
		\label{eq:planar_p}
	\end{equation}
	
	The equations of motion are the $\rhat$ and $\phihat$ parts of Eq.~\eqref{eq:perturbed_eom}, with the acceleration of Section~\ref{tb:sec:planar},
	\begin{align}
		\mu(\ddot r-r\dot\phi^2)
		&=
		-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}+F_r,
		\label{eq:perturbed_radial}\\
		\mu(r\ddot\phi+2\dot r\dot\phi)
		&=
		F_{\phi}.
		\label{eq:perturbed_theta}
	\end{align}
	
	The second equation gives the rate of $L$,
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{dL}{dt}
		=
		\frac{d}{dt}(\mu r^2\dot\phi)
		=
		\mu\big(2r\dot r\dot\phi+r^2\ddot\phi\big)
		=
		r\,\mu\big(r\ddot\phi+2\dot r\dot\phi\big)
		=
		rF_{\phi},
	\end{equation}
	so
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot L=rF_{\phi}.
		}
		\label{eq:planar_Ldot}
	\end{equation}
	
	The energy equation \eqref{eq:perturbed_energy_rate} becomes
	\begin{align}
		\dot E
		&\eqby{1}
		\dot rF_r+r\dot\phi F_{\phi}
		\eqby{2}
		\dot rF_r+\frac{L}{\mu r}F_{\phi} .
		\label{eq:planar_Edot}
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\dot{\vecr}\cdot\vec F$ with $\dot{\vecr}=\dot r\,\rhat+r\dot\phi\,\phihat$.
		\item $r\dot\phi=L/(\mu r)$.
	\end{explain}
	
	\subsection{Keplerian geometry of the instantaneous ellipse}
	
	We need the osculating ellipse's $r$, $\dot r$ and $\dot\phi$ in terms of its elements. The true anomaly is
	\begin{equation}
		v\equiv\phi-\omega .
		\label{eq:true_anomaly_def}
	\end{equation}
	For the instantaneous Kepler orbit,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			r=\frac{\lambda}{1+e\cos v},
		}
		\label{eq:instantaneous_conic}
	\end{equation}
	where
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\lambda\equiv a(1-e^2)
			=\frac{L^2}{GM\mu^2}.
		}
		\label{eq:lambda_def}
	\end{equation}
	
	The instantaneous angular velocity is
	\begin{equation}
		\dot\phi
		=
		\frac{L}{\mu r^2}.
		\label{eq:theta_dot_keplerian}
	\end{equation}
	For the radial velocity we start from the general case, where the elements depend on time,
	\begin{equation}
		a(t)=a_0+\delta a(t),\qquad
		e(t)=e_0+\delta e(t),\qquad
		\omega(t)=\omega_0+\delta\omega(t),
	\end{equation}
	and the changes $\delta a$, $\delta e$ and $\delta\omega$ are produced by the perturbing force. Along the actual path, by the chain rule,
	\begin{align}
		\dot r
		=
		\left.\pdv{r}{t}\right|_Q
		+
		\sum_{i=1}^{6}\pdv{r}{Q_i}\dot Q_i .
	\end{align}
	Since $r=\rhat\cdot\vecr$ and $\rhat\cdot\partial\rhat/\partial Q_i=0$, each $\partial r/\partial Q_i$ is $\rhat\cdot\partial\vecr/\partial Q_i$. The sum is therefore the radial part of $\sum_i(\partial\vecr/\partial Q_i)\dot Q_i$, which vanishes by the osculating condition \eqref{eq:osculating_r_constraint}. So $\dot r$ is the radial velocity of the osculating ellipse with its elements held fixed. On that ellipse only the true anomaly moves, at the rate $\dot v=\dot\phi$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta_dot_keplerian}, giving
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot r
			=
			\frac{dr}{dv}\dot v
			=
			\frac{\lambda e\sin v}{(1+e\cos v)^2}
			\frac{L}{\mu r^2}
			=
			\frac{Le\sin v}{\mu\lambda}.
		}
		\label{eq:rdot_true_anomaly}
	\end{equation}
	The last step uses $1/r^2=(1+e\cos v)^2/\lambda^2$. The result is exact for the osculating elements.
	
	Since
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{\lambda}{r}=1+e\cos v,
	\end{equation}
	we also have
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\frac{\mu r\dot r}{L}
			=
			\frac{e\sin v}{1+e\cos v}.
		}
		\label{eq:xi_relation}
	\end{equation}
	We name this ratio,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\xi\equiv
			\frac{e\sin v}{1+e\cos v}.
		}
		\label{eq:xi_def}
	\end{equation}
	
	\subsection{Rate of the semi-major axis}
	
	At a given instant the osculating orbit has the same energy as the actual orbit, so its semi-major axis $a(t)$ follows the energy. Its Keplerian energy and its rate are
	\begin{equation}
		E\bigg|_{\rm osculating}
		=
		-\frac{GM\mu}{2a},
		\qquad
		\therefore\qquad
		\dot E\bigg|_{\rm osculating}
		=
		\frac{GM\mu}{2a^2}\dot a.\label{eq:osculating_energy}
	\end{equation}
	The right side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:planar_Edot} is evaluated with the osculating $r$, $\dot r$ and $L$,
	\begin{align}
		\dot{E}\bigg|_{\rm osculating}
		&=
		\dot rF_r+\frac{L}{\mu r}F_{\phi}
		\eqby{1}
		\frac{L}{\mu\lambda}e\sin v\,F_r
		+
		\frac{L}{\mu r}F_{\phi}.
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\dot r=Le\sin v/(\mu\lambda)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:rdot_true_anomaly}.
	\end{explain}
	Every quantity here is the osculating value, so this is exact. Since the perturbing force is small, $F_r,F_{\phi}=O(F)$, the rates of the elements are $O(F)$ too. To first order the quantities multiplying $F_r$ and $F_\phi$ may therefore be evaluated on the unperturbed Keplerian orbit, and this is what the orbit averages of Section~\ref{tb:sec:averaged} use. Equating with Eq.~\eqref{eq:osculating_energy},
	\begin{align}
		\dot a
		&=
		\frac{2a^2L}{GM\mu^2}
		\left[
		\frac{e\sin v}{\lambda}F_r
		+
		\frac{1}{r}F_{\phi}
		\right] \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{1}
		\frac{2a^2\sqrt{GM\lambda}}{GM\mu\lambda}
		\Big[
		e\sin v\,F_r
		+
		(1+e\cos v)F_{\phi}
		\Big]
		\eqby{2}
		\sqrt{\frac{4a^3}{GM\mu^2(1-e^2)}}
		\Big[
		e\sin v\,F_r
		+
		(1+e\cos v)F_{\phi}
		\Big].
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $L=\mu\sqrt{GM\lambda}$ and $1/r=(1+e\cos v)/\lambda$.
		\item $2a^2/(\mu\sqrt{GM\lambda})=\sqrt{4a^4/(GM\mu^2a(1-e^2))}$, with $\lambda=a(1-e^2)$.
	\end{explain}
	Here we used
	\begin{equation}
		L=\mu\sqrt{GM\lambda},
		\qquad
		\lambda=a(1-e^2).\label{eq: angular_momenta}
	\end{equation}
	So
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot a
			=
			\sqrt{
				\frac{4a^3}{GM\mu^2(1-e^2)}
			}
			\left[
			e\sin v\,F_r
			+
			(1+e\cos v)F_{\phi}
			\right].
		}
		\label{eq:gauss_adot}
	\end{equation}
	
	\subsection{Rate of the eccentricity}
	
	Since $A=GM\mu^2e$, it is enough to find $\dot A$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:perturbed_A_rate}. For the planar force, with $\vec L=L\hat L$, the two terms are
	\begin{align}
		\vec F\times\vec L
		&=
		L
		\left(
		F_r\rhat+F_{\phi}\phihat
		\right)\times\hat L
		\eqby{1}
		L
		\left(
		-F_r\phihat+F_{\phi}\rhat
		\right), \nonumber\\
		\vecp\times(\vecr\times\vec F)
		&\eqby{2}
		\left(
		\mu\dot r\rhat+\frac{L}{r}\phihat
		\right)
		\times
		\left(
		rF_{\phi}\hat L
		\right)
		\eqby{1}
		L F_{\phi}\rhat-\mu r\dot r F_{\phi}\phihat .
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\rhat\times\hat L=-\phihat$ and $\phihat\times\hat L=\rhat$, since $(\rhat,\phihat,\hat L)$ is right-handed.
		\item $\vecp$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:planar_p}, and $\vecr\times\vec F=rF_\phi\hat L$.
	\end{explain}
	Adding them,
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vec A}=
		\vec F\times\vec L
		+
		\vecp\times(\vecr\times\vec F)
		=
		2L F_{\phi}\rhat
		-
		\left(
		L F_r+\mu r\dot r F_{\phi}
		\right)\phihat .
		\label{eq:planar_Adot}
	\end{equation}
	With $\mu r\dot r=L\xi$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:xi_def},
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot{\vec A}
			=
			L
			\left[
			2F_{\phi}\rhat
			-
			(F_r+\xi F_{\phi})\phihat
			\right].
		}
		\label{eq:planar_Adot_xi}
	\end{equation}
	For a purely radial force this is $\dot{\vec{A}}=-F_rL\,\phihat$.
	
	The unit vector $\hat A$ points to the periapsis, and the true anomaly is measured from it. Let $\hat q$ be $\hat A$ turned by $90^\circ$ towards increasing $v$. Then $(\rhat,\phihat)$ is $(\hat A,\hat q)$ turned by $v$, and the inverse turn is the transpose,
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{pmatrix}\rhat\\ \phihat\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos v&\sin v\\ -\sin v&\cos v\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat A\\ \hat q\end{pmatrix}
		\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
		\begin{pmatrix}\hat A\\ \hat q\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos v&-\sin v\\ \sin v&\cos v\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\rhat\\ \phihat\end{pmatrix}.
		\label{tb:eq:periapsis_frame}
	\end{equation}
	The first row is
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\hat A
			=
			\cos v\,\rhat-\sin v\,\phihat .
		}
		\label{eq:Ahat_f}
	\end{equation}
	Writing $\vec A=A\hat A$,
	\begin{align}
		\dot{\vec A} &= \dot A\hat A+A\dot{\hat A},\\
		\dot{\vec A}\cdot\hat A &\eqby{1}\dot A ,
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\hat A\cdot\hat A=1$, so $2\hat A\cdot\dot{\hat A}=0$.
	\end{explain}
	and therefore
	\begin{align}
		\dot A&=\dot{\vec A}\cdot\hat A
		\eqby{1}L\left[2F_{\phi}\cos v+(F_r+\xi F_{\phi})\sin v\right],
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item Eq.~\eqref{eq:planar_Adot_xi} dotted into Eq.~\eqref{eq:Ahat_f}, with $\rhat\cdot\rhat=\phihat\cdot\phihat=1$ and $\rhat\cdot\phihat=0$.
	\end{explain}
	that is,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot A
			=
			L
			\left[
			F_r\sin v
			+
			F_{\phi}
			\left(
			2\cos v+\xi\sin v
			\right)
			\right].
		}
		\label{eq:Adot_magnitude_exact}
	\end{equation}
	Up to this point all relations have been exact. We now write both sides with the osculating elements, $A=GM\mu^2e$ and $L=\sqrt{GM\mu^2a(1-e^2)}$,
	\begin{align}
		\dot A\bigg\lvert_{\rm osculating}
		&=
		L
		\left[
		F_r\sin v
		+
		F_{\phi}
		\left(
		2\cos v+\xi\sin v
		\right)
		\right]\bigg\lvert_{\rm osculating} \nonumber\\
		GM\mu^2\dot e &=\sqrt{GM\mu^2 a(1-e^2)}\left[
		F_r\sin v
		+
		F_{\phi}
		\left(
		2\cos v+\xi\sin v
		\right)
		\right],
	\end{align}
	and obtain
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot e
			=
			\sqrt{
				\frac{a(1-e^2)}{GM\mu^2}
			}
			\left[
			F_r\sin v
			+
			F_{\phi}
			\left(
			2\cos v+\xi\sin v
			\right)
			\right].
		}
		\label{eq:gauss_edot}
	\end{equation}
	
	
	\subsection{Rate of periapsis}
	
	The direction of $\vec A$ gives $\omega$. The unit vector perpendicular to $\hat A$, towards increasing true anomaly, is the second row of Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:periapsis_frame},
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\hat q
			=
			\sin v\,\rhat+\cos v\,\phihat .
		}
		\label{eq:qhat_def}
	\end{equation}
	Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:Ahat_f},
	\begin{align}
		\dot{\hat A}
		&=
		-\dot v\sin v\,\rhat
		+\cos v\,\dot{\rhat}
		-\dot v\cos v\,\phihat
		-\sin v\,\dot{\phihat} \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{1}
		(\dot\phi-\dot v)
		\left(
		\sin v\,\rhat+\cos v\,\phihat
		\right)
		\eqby{2}
		\dot\omega\,\hat q .
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\dot{\rhat}=\dot\phi\,\phihat$ and $\dot{\phihat}=-\dot\phi\,\rhat$, Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:unit_vector_rates}.
		\item $v=\phi-\omega$ gives $\dot v=\dot\phi-\dot\omega$, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:qhat_def}.
	\end{explain}
	Since
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vec A}
		=
		\dot A\,\hat A+A\dot{\hat A}=\dot A\,\hat A+A\dot\omega\,\hat q,
	\end{equation}
	projection onto $\hat q$ gives
	\begin{equation}
		A\dot\omega
		=
		\dot{\vec A}\cdot\hat q.
	\end{equation}
	Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:planar_Adot_xi} and~\eqref{eq:qhat_def},
	\begin{align}
		A\dot\omega\bigg\lvert_{\rm osculating}
		&=
		L
		\left[
		2F_{\phi}\sin v
		-
		(F_r+\xi F_{\phi})\cos v
		\right]\bigg\lvert_{\rm osculating}.
	\end{align}
	With $A=GM\mu^2 e$,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot\omega
			=
			-\frac{L}{GM\mu^2e}
			\left[
			F_r\cos v
			+
			F_{\phi}
			\left(
			\xi\cos v-2\sin v
			\right)
			\right].
		}
		\label{eq:gauss_omegadot_L}
	\end{equation}
	Using
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{L}{GM\mu^2e}
		=
		\frac{1}{e}
		\sqrt{
			\frac{a(1-e^2)}{GM\mu^2}
		},
	\end{equation}
	we obtain
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot\omega
			=
			-\frac{1}{e}
			\sqrt{
				\frac{a(1-e^2)}{GM\mu^2}
			}
			\left[
			F_r\cos v
			+
			F_{\phi}
			\left(
			\xi\cos v-2\sin v
			\right)
			\right].
		}
		\label{eq:gauss_omegadot}
	\end{equation}
	
	\subsection{Rate of the true anomaly}
	
	Since $v=\phi-\omega$, we have $\dot v=\dot\phi-\dot\omega$. The instantaneous Keplerian value of $\dot\phi$ is $L/(\mu r^2)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta_dot_keplerian}. With the osculating orbit,
	\begin{align}
		\dot v&=\frac{L}{\mu r^2}-\dot\omega\bigg\lvert_{\rm osculating} \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{1}\frac{\mu\sqrt{GMa(1-e^2)}\,(1+e\cos v)^2}{\mu\,a^2(1-e^2)^2}-\dot\omega
		\eqby{2}\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a^3}}
		\frac{(1+e\cos v)^2}{(1-e^2)^{3/2}}-\dot{\omega}.\label{eq:gauss_fdot}
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $r=a(1-e^2)/(1+e\cos v)$ and $L=\mu\sqrt{GMa(1-e^2)}$.
		\item $\sqrt{a(1-e^2)}/\big(a^2(1-e^2)^2\big)=a^{-3/2}(1-e^2)^{-3/2}$.
	\end{explain}
	
	\subsection{Planar Gauss equations}
	
	Collecting the results,
	\begin{align}
		\dot a
		&=
		\sqrt{
			\frac{4a^3}{GM\mu^2(1-e^2)}
		}
		\left[
		e\sin v\,F_r
		+
		(1+e\cos v)F_{\phi}
		\right],
		\label{eq:gauss_planar_a}
		\\[6pt]
		\dot e
		&=
		\sqrt{
			\frac{a(1-e^2)}{GM\mu^2}
		}
		\left[
		F_r\sin v
		+
		F_{\phi}
		\left(
		2\cos v+\xi\sin v
		\right)
		\right],
		\label{eq:gauss_planar_e}
		\\[6pt]
		\dot\omega
		&=
		-\frac{1}{e}
		\sqrt{
			\frac{a(1-e^2)}{GM\mu^2}
		}
		\left[
		F_r\cos v
		+
		F_{\phi}
		\left(
		\xi\cos v-2\sin v
		\right)
		\right],
		\label{eq:gauss_planar_omega}
		\\[6pt]
		\dot v
		&=
		\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a^3}}
		\frac{(1+e\cos v)^2}{(1-e^2)^{3/2}}
		-\dot\omega,
		\label{eq:gauss_planar_f}
	\end{align}
	where
	\begin{equation}
		\xi=\frac{e\sin v}{1+e\cos v}.
	\end{equation}
	For a perturbation confined to the plane,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot\iota=\dot\Omega=0.
		}
	\end{equation}
	
	These are equations for the \emph{osculating} elements. The quantities $a$, $e$ and $\omega$ are therefore functions of time, even though the actual position and momentum at each instant are those of a Keplerian orbit. Only the force in the plane enters. A push along the motion ($F_\phi$ at $e=0$) changes $a$, while a radial push changes $e$ and $\omega$ but, at the apsides, not $a$.
	
	\section{Orbit-averaged evolution}
	\label{tb:sec:averaged}
	
	The Gauss equations give the rates of the elements at each instant.
	
	Over one orbit most of that change goes forward and then back again. Over many orbits only the net drift matters, and the instantaneous rates hide it.
	
	The sea rises and falls twice a day with the tides. To see the sea level creep up over decades, we average over each day and watch how the daily average changes.
	
	The elements of an orbit wobble within each orbit in the same way. The idea is to average the rates over one unperturbed Kepler orbit. To first order in the force, what is left is the slow, \textbf{secular} drift.
	
	The example below in numbers. A small extra radial force $\chi/r^3$ with $\chi\mu/L^2=0.01$ turns the periapsis by $-\pi\times0.01=-0.031\,\mathrm{rad}=-1.8^\circ$ per orbit, while $a$ and $e$ return to their starting values after each orbit.
	
	The picture is a rate that swings up and down within each orbit around a small offset. The offset is the drift.
	
	The same averaging gives the mean Sun of Chapter~\ref{ch:time}, the adiabatic invariants of mechanics and the slow precession of the Earth's axis. It works because the orbital period is short compared with the time over which the elements change (P6). It gives the averaging rule and a worked example, checked against an exact solution.
	
	\paragraph{The average.} For any quantity $X$,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\avg{\dot X}
			=
			\frac{1}{P}\int_0^P\dot X\,dt .
		}
		\label{eq:orbit_average}
	\end{equation}
	
	For an unperturbed Kepler ellipse the elements are fixed, so $\dot v=\dot\phi$ and
	\begin{equation}
		L=\mu r^2\dot v,
	\end{equation}
	and therefore
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			dt=\frac{\mu r^2}{L}\,dv.
		}
		\label{eq:dt_df}
	\end{equation}
	On the true path $\dot v=\dot\phi-\dot\omega$ differs from $\dot\phi$ by $\dot\omega=O(F)$. Inside an average that is already multiplied by $F$, the difference between $d\phi$ and $dv$ changes the result only at $O(F^2)$.
	
	The average rate can therefore be written as
	\begin{equation}
		\avg{\dot X}
		=
		\frac{1}{P}
		\int_0^{2\pi}
		\dot X
		\frac{\mu r^2}{L}\,dv .
		\label{eq:average_f}
	\end{equation}
	
	A nonzero value of $\avg{\dot X}$ is a secular change. Terms whose integral over $0\leq v\leq2\pi$ vanishes describe only oscillations within each orbit.
	\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Example: Perturbation by an Inverse-Cube Force $F_r = \chi/r^3$}{Example: Perturbation by an Inverse-Cube Force}}
	
	We take the perturbing force
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\vec F
			=
			\frac{\chi}{r^3}\rhat ,
		}
		\label{eq:worked_force}
	\end{equation}
	with a constant $\chi$ that is positive for a push outward. Thus
	\begin{equation}
		F_r=\frac{\chi}{r^3},
		\qquad
		F_{\phi}=0.
	\end{equation}
	We find the three rates directly from $E$, $L$ and $\vecA$, and then compare with the Gauss equations.
	
	%----------------------------------------------------------
	\subsubsection{Rate of the semimajor axis}
	
	From Eq.~\eqref{eq:osculating_energy},
	\begin{equation}
		\dot E
		=
		\frac{GM\mu}{2a^2}\dot a.
		\label{eq:energy_a}
	\end{equation}
	
	The exact rate of change of the energy of the actual orbit is, by Eq.~\eqref{eq:perturbed_energy_rate},
	\begin{equation}
		\dot E
		=
		\vec F\cdot\dot{\vecr}.
		\label{eq:energy_rate}
	\end{equation}
	
	The perturbation is radial and $\dot{\vecr}=\dot r\,\rhat+r\dot\phi\,\phihat$, so only the radial velocity does work,
	\begin{equation}
		\dot E
		=
		\frac{\chi}{r^3}\dot r.
		\label{eq:energy_radial}
	\end{equation}
	
	For the osculating Keplerian orbit, Eq.~\eqref{eq:rdot_true_anomaly} with $\lambda=L^2/(GM\mu^2)$ gives
	\begin{equation}
		\dot r
		=
		\frac{Le\sin v}{\mu}\,\frac{GM\mu^2}{L^2}
		=
		\frac{GM\mu}{L}e\sin v.
		\label{eq:osculating_rdot}
	\end{equation}
	
	Therefore
	\begin{equation}
		\dot E
		=
		\frac{\chi GM\mu}{Lr^3}e\sin v.
	\end{equation}
	
	Comparing this result with Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_a},
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{GM\mu}{2a^2}\dot a
		=
		\frac{\chi GM\mu}{Lr^3}e\sin v,
	\end{equation}
	so that
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot a
			=
			\frac{2\chi a^2e\sin v}{Lr^3}
		}.
		\label{eq:adot}
	\end{equation}
	
	
	%----------------------------------------------------------
	\subsubsection{Rate of the eccentricity}
	
	The Runge--Lenz vector is
	\begin{equation}
		\vec A
		=
		\vecp\times\vec L
		-
		GM\mu^2\rhat,
		\label{eq:A_definition}
	\end{equation}
	and for the osculating Keplerian orbit
	\begin{equation}
		A
		=
		GM\mu^2 e.
		\label{eq:A_e}
	\end{equation}
	
	Since the perturbing force is radial, it exerts no torque,
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vec L}
		=
		\vecr\times\vec F
		=
		\vecr\times
		\frac{\chi}{r^3}\rhat
		=
		0.
		\label{eq:Ldot_zero}
	\end{equation}
	
	Differentiating $L^2=GM\mu^2a(1-e^2)$ gives
	\begin{equation}
		2L\dot L
		=
		GM\mu^2
		\left[
		(1-e^2)\dot a
		-
		2ae\dot e
		\right],
	\end{equation}
	and the left side is zero. Therefore
	\begin{equation}
		\dot e
		=
		\frac{1-e^2}{2ae}\dot a .
		\label{eq:edot_adot}
	\end{equation}
	
	Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:adot},
	\begin{equation}
		\dot e
		=
		\frac{1-e^2}{2ae}\,
		\frac{2\chi a^2e\sin v}{Lr^3}
		=
		\frac{\chi a(1-e^2)\sin v}{Lr^3}.
	\end{equation}
	As a check, the Gauss equation \eqref{eq:gauss_planar_e} with $F_\phi=0$ gives $\dot e=\sqrt{a(1-e^2)/(GM\mu^2)}\,\chi\sin v/r^3$. With $L=\mu\sqrt{GMa(1-e^2)}$ the two are the same.
	
	
	%----------------------------------------------------------
	\subsubsection{Rate of the argument of periapsis}
	
	The direction of $\vecA$ gives the periapsis. Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:A_definition},
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vec A}
		=
		\dot{\vecp}\times\vec L
		+
		\vecp\times\dot{\vec L}
		-
		GM\mu^2\dot{\rhat}.
	\end{equation}
	
	Since $\dot{\vec L}=0$,
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vec A}
		=
		\dot{\vecp}\times\vec L
		-
		GM\mu^2\dot{\rhat}.
	\end{equation}
	
	The momentum equation is
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vecp}
		=
		-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}\rhat
		+
		\frac{\chi}{r^3}\rhat.
	\end{equation}
	
	Therefore
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vec A}
		=
		\left(
		-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}
		+
		\frac{\chi}{r^3}
		\right)
		\underbrace{\rhat\times\vec L}_{\mathclap{=-L\phihat}}
		-
		\underbrace{GM\mu^2\underbrace{\dot{\rhat}}_{\mathclap{=\dot \phi\,\phihat}}}_{=\frac{GM\mu L}{r^2}\phihat}.
	\end{equation}
	The terms with the central gravitational force cancel, leaving
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vec A}
		=
		-\frac{\chi L}{r^3}\phihat.
		\label{eq:Adot}
	\end{equation}
	
	We use the periapsis frame $(\hat A,\hat q)$ of Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:periapsis_frame}. Here $\hat A$ points toward the periapsis, $\hat A=\cos\omega\,\hatn+\sin\omega\,\hat y$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:Ahat}, and $\hat q$ is $\hat A$ turned by $90^\circ$ towards increasing true anomaly. Then
	\begin{equation}
		\vec A
		=
		A\hat A,
	\end{equation}
	and, since $\dot{\hat A}=\dot\omega\,\hat q$ (Section~\ref{tb:sec:planar_pert}),
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vec A}
		=
		\dot A\,\hat A
		+
		A\dot\omega\,\hat q.
		\label{eq:Adot_decomposition}
	\end{equation}
	
	The radial and angular unit vectors in the periapsis frame are, from Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:periapsis_frame},
	\begin{equation}
		\rhat
		=
		\cos v\,\hat A
		+
		\sin v\,\hat q,
	\end{equation}
	and
	\begin{equation}
		\phihat
		=
		-\sin v\,\hat A
		+
		\cos v\,\hat q.
	\end{equation}
	
	Substituting the latter into Eq.~\eqref{eq:Adot},
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\vec A}
		=
		-\frac{\chi L}{r^3}
		\left(
		-\sin v\,\hat A
		+
		\cos v\,\hat q
		\right)
		=
		\frac{\chi L}{r^3}\sin v\,\hat A
		-
		\frac{\chi L}{r^3}\cos v\,
		\hat q.
	\end{equation}
	
	Comparing this with Eq.~\eqref{eq:Adot_decomposition},
	\begin{equation}
		\dot A
		=
		\frac{\chi L}{r^3}\sin v,
		\label{eq:Adot_magnitude_radial_pert}
	\end{equation}
	and
	\begin{equation}
		A\dot\omega
		=
		-\frac{\chi L}{r^3}\cos v.
	\end{equation}
	
	With $A=GM\mu^2e$,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot\omega
			=
			-\frac{\chi L}{GM\mu^2e\,r^3}\cos v
		}.
		\label{eq:omegadot}
	\end{equation}
	
	\subsection{Semi-major axis}
	
	We now average the three rates over one orbit, starting with $a$. As a check on Eq.~\eqref{eq:adot}, the Gauss equation \eqref{eq:gauss_planar_a} gives
	\begin{equation}
		\dot a
		=
		\sqrt{
			\frac{4a^3}{GM\mu^2(1-e^2)}
		}
		\,e\sin v\,
		\frac{\chi}{r^3}
		\eqby{1}
		\frac{2a^2}{\mu\sqrt{GM\lambda}}\,\frac{\mu\lambda\dot r}{L}\,\frac{\chi}{r^3},
	\end{equation}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\sqrt{4a^3/(GM\mu^2(1-e^2))}=2a^2/(\mu\sqrt{GM\lambda})$, and $e\sin v=\mu\lambda\dot r/L$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:rdot_true_anomaly}.
	\end{explain}
	and with $L=\mu\sqrt{GM\lambda}$,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot a
			=
			\frac{2\chi a^2}{GM\mu r^3}\dot r .
		}
		\label{eq:worked_adot}
	\end{equation}
	
	For the Keplerian ellipse we use the eccentric anomaly $u$,
	\begin{equation}
		r=a(1-e\cos u),
	\end{equation}
	and, from Eqs.~\eqref{eq:rdot_u} and~\eqref{eq:kepler3},
	\begin{equation}
		\dot r
		=
		\frac{ane\sin u}{1-e\cos u},
		\qquad
		n=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a^3}}.
	\end{equation}
	Therefore
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot a
			=
			\frac{
				2\chi e n\sin u
			}{
				GM\mu(1-e\cos u)^4
			}.
		}
		\label{eq:worked_adot_u}
	\end{equation}
	
	Over one unperturbed orbit, from Eq.~\eqref{eq:udot},
	\begin{equation}
		dt
		=
		\frac{1-e\cos u}{n}\,du,
	\end{equation}
	so
	\begin{align}
		\Delta a
		&=
		\int_0^{2\pi}\dot a\,dt
		=
		\frac{2\chi e}{GM\mu}
		\int_0^{2\pi}
		\frac{\sin u}{(1-e\cos u)^3}\,du
		\nonumber\\
		&\eqby{1}
		\frac{2\chi e}{GM\mu}\left[-\frac{1}{2e\,(1-e\cos u)^2}\right]_0^{2\pi}
		=0 .
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\dfrac{d}{du}(1-e\cos u)^{-2}=-2e\sin u\,(1-e\cos u)^{-3}$.
	\end{explain}
	The same follows without any integral. Eq.~\eqref{eq:worked_adot} is $\dot a=-(\chi a^2/GM\mu)\,d(r^{-2})/dt$, and $r$ returns to its starting value after one orbit. Thus
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\avg{\dot a}=0
		}
		\label{eq:worked_a_secular}
	\end{equation}
	to first order in the perturbation.
	
	The osculating semi-major axis oscillates during an orbit but has no secular drift.
	
	\subsection{Eccentricity}
	
	Angular momentum is conserved, since the force is radial, so
	\begin{equation}
		L^2=GM\mu^2a(1-e^2)
	\end{equation}
	is constant, and therefore
	\begin{equation}
		0
		=
		(1-e^2)\dot a
		-
		2ae\dot e .
	\end{equation}
	Hence
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot e
			=
			\frac{1-e^2}{2ae}\dot a .
		}
		\label{eq:worked_edot_relation}
	\end{equation}
	To first order the factor $(1-e^2)/(2ae)$ is constant over the orbit, so since $\avg{\dot a}=0$,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\avg{\dot e}=0 .
		}
		\label{eq:worked_e_secular}
	\end{equation}
	
	\subsection{Apsidal precession}
	
	For the radial force, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gauss_planar_omega} with $F_\phi=0$ and $L/(GM\mu^2e)=\sqrt{a(1-e^2)/(GM\mu^2)}/e$ reduces to Eq.~\eqref{eq:omegadot},
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot\omega
			=
			-\frac{\chi L\cos v}
			{GM\mu^2e\,r^3}.
		}
		\label{eq:worked_omega_dot}
	\end{equation}
	
	To first order in $\chi$ we evaluate the secular change on the unperturbed Keplerian orbit,
	\begin{equation}
		r=\frac{\lambda}{1+e\cos v},
		\qquad
		\lambda=a(1-e^2),
	\end{equation}
	together with Eq.~\eqref{eq:dt_df},
	\begin{equation}
		dt=\frac{\mu r^2}{L}\,dv.
	\end{equation}
	Then
	\begin{align}
		\Delta\omega
		&=
		\int_0^{2\pi}\dot\omega\,\frac{\mu r^2}{L}\,dv
		\eqby{1}
		-\frac{\chi}{GM\mu e}
		\int_0^{2\pi}
		\frac{\cos v}{r}\,dv
		\nonumber\\
		&\eqby{2}
		-\frac{\chi}{GM\mu e}
		\int_0^{2\pi}
		\frac{1+e\cos v}{\lambda}\cos v\,dv
		\nonumber\\
		&=
		-\frac{\chi}{GM\mu e\lambda}
		\left[
		\int_0^{2\pi}\cos v\,dv
		+
		e\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2v\,dv
		\right].
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\dfrac{\chi L}{GM\mu^2e\,r^3}\cdot\dfrac{\mu r^2}{L}=\dfrac{\chi}{GM\mu e\,r}$.
		\item $1/r=(1+e\cos v)/\lambda$.
	\end{explain}
	Since
	\begin{equation}
		\int_0^{2\pi}\cos v\,dv=0,
		\qquad
		\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2v\,dv=\pi,
	\end{equation}
	we obtain
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\Delta\omega
			=
			-\frac{\pi\chi}
			{GM\mu\,a(1-e^2)} .
		}
		\label{eq:worked_precession_per_orbit}
	\end{equation}
	
	Dividing by the period
	\begin{equation}
		P=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM}},
	\end{equation}
	the secular rate is
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\avg{\dot\omega}
			=
			-\frac{\chi}{2\mu\sqrt{GM}\,a^{5/2}(1-e^2)} .	}
		\label{eq:worked_precession_rate}
	\end{equation}
	An outward push ($\chi>0$) turns the periapsis backwards, against the motion, and an extra inward pull ($\chi<0$) turns it forwards. The size $a$ and the shape $e$ do not drift.
	
	\paragraph{A second route, exact.} The same result follows from the exact orbit equation \eqref{eq:orbiteq} for the full radial force
	\begin{equation}
		F_r=-\frac{GM\mu}{r^2}+\frac{\chi}{r^3},
	\end{equation}
	which is $-V'(r)$. With $\rho=1/r$ the right side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:orbiteq} is $-(\mu/L^2)F_r/\rho^2=GM\mu^2/L^2-\chi\mu\rho/L^2$, so
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{d^2\rho}{d\phi^2}+
		\left(
		1+\frac{\chi\mu}{L^2}
		\right)\rho
		=
		\frac{GM\mu^2}{L^2}.
	\end{equation}
	This is the shifted oscillator of Section~\ref{tb:sec:kepler_orbits} with a stiffer spring. In $\phi$ the oscillation of $\rho$ has the angular frequency $\sqrt{1+\chi\mu/L^2}$, so one periapsis follows the next after the angle
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta\phi_{\rm apsis}
		=
		\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{1+\chi\mu/L^2}}.
	\end{equation}
	The exact shift of the periapsis per orbit, relative to $2\pi$, is therefore
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta\omega_{\rm exact}
		=
		2\pi
		\left[
		\frac{1}{
			\sqrt{1+\chi\mu/L^2}
		}
		-1
		\right].
	\end{equation}
	For a weak perturbation,
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{\chi\mu}{L^2}\ll1,
	\end{equation}
	so
	\begin{align}
		\Delta\omega_{\rm exact}
		&\eqby{1}
		-\frac{\pi\chi\mu}{L^2}
		+O(\chi^2)
		\nonumber\\
		&\eqby{2}
		-\frac{\pi\chi}
		{GM\mu\,a(1-e^2)}
		+O(\chi^2),
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $(1+x)^{-1/2}=1-x/2+O(x^2)$.
		\item $L^2=GM\mu^2a(1-e^2)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:L_of_ae}.
	\end{explain}
	which agrees with the orbit-averaged result \eqref{eq:worked_precession_per_orbit}.
	\section{Nearly circular orbits}
	\label{sec:nearly_circular}
	
	The Gauss equation \eqref{eq:gauss_planar_omega} for $\dot\omega$ carries a factor $1/e$.
	
	As $e\to0$ the periapsis loses its meaning, because a circle has no nearest point. Then $\dot\omega$ blows up, although nothing physical does.
	
	Ask which point of a round table is nearest the wall. For a table almost centred in the room the answer swings right round with the smallest nudge, while the nudge itself is tiny. It is better to give how far the table's centre has moved east and north. Those two numbers are always well defined and change smoothly.
	
	For an orbit the two numbers are the components of the eccentricity vector \eqref{tb:eq:ecc_vector}. The idea is to use $e_x=e\cos\omega$ and $e_y=e\sin\omega$ in place of $e$ and $\omega$. They go smoothly to zero with $e$, and the factor $1/e$ disappears.
	
	The table in numbers. Take $e=0.001$ and $\omega=0$, so $(e_x,e_y)=(0.001,0)$. A kick that adds $0.001$ to $e_y$ turns $\omega$ by $45^\circ$ and raises $e$ only to $0.0014$, while neither component changed by more than $0.001$.
	
	The picture is the tip of the vector $\vec e$ wandering near the origin of a plane. Its angle swings about wildly, but its position does not.
	
	The same trouble is the meaningless longitude at the Earth's pole (Chapter~\ref{ch:coords}), cured the same way by Cartesian components. Here $e$ is the small number and terms are kept to first order in it (P6). The result is $r$ and $\phi$ to first order in $e$, and the Gauss equations in $e_x$ and $e_y$.
	
	\paragraph{The orbit to first order in $e$.} The components of the eccentricity vector along $\hatn$ and $\hat y$ are
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			e_x=e\cos\omega,
			\qquad
			e_y=e\sin\omega.
		}
		\label{eq:LL_vars}
	\end{equation}
	
	For small $e$ we define the \textbf{mean longitude}
	\begin{equation}
		\bar\phi\equiv\omega+\ell ,
	\end{equation}
	the polar angle of a body moving round at the mean rate. For $e=0$ it is the polar angle itself. To first order in $e$, $v=\ell+2e\sin\ell+O(e^2)$, derived from Kepler's second law in Eq.~\eqref{ti:eq:equation_of_centre}, where it gives the equation of time. Then
	\begin{align}
		\phi
		=\omega+v
		&\eqby{1}
		\bar\phi
		+
		2e\sin(\bar\phi-\omega)
		+O(e^2)
		\eqby{2}
		\bar\phi
		+
		2e_x\sin\bar\phi
		-
		2e_y\cos\bar\phi
		+O(e^2).
		\label{eq:small_e_theta}
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $v=\ell+2e\sin\ell$ and $\ell=\bar\phi-\omega$.
		\item $e\sin(\bar\phi-\omega)=e\cos\omega\sin\bar\phi-e\sin\omega\cos\bar\phi$.
	\end{explain}
	In the same way $r=a(1-e\cos u)=a(1-e\cos\ell)+O(e^2)$ and $e\cos(\bar\phi-\omega)=e_x\cos\bar\phi+e_y\sin\bar\phi$, so
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			r
			=
			a
			\left(
			1-e_x\cos\bar\phi-e_y\sin\bar\phi
			\right)
			+O(e^2).
		}
		\label{eq:small_e_r}
	\end{equation}
	
	For fixed elements $\dot{\bar\phi}=\dot\ell=n$, with
	\begin{equation}
		n=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a^3}},
	\end{equation}
	so differentiating,
	\begin{equation}
		\dot r
		=
		an
		\left(
		e_x\sin\bar\phi-e_y\cos\bar\phi
		\right)
		+O(e^2).
	\end{equation}
	Also,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot\phi
			=
			n
			\left(
			1+2e_x\cos\bar\phi
			+2e_y\sin\bar\phi
			\right)
			+O(e^2).
		}
		\label{eq:small_e_theta_dot}
	\end{equation}
	
	We name the first-order offset of the polar angle from the mean longitude,
	\begin{equation}
		\delta\phi
		\equiv
		\phi-\bar\phi
		=
		2e_x\sin\bar\phi
		-
		2e_y\cos\bar\phi .
	\end{equation}
	Then $\dot r=an\,\delta\phi/2$, and to first order $\xi=e\sin v/(1+e\cos v)=e\sin(\bar\phi-\omega)+O(e^2)=\delta\phi/2$.
	
	The variables $e_x$ and $e_y$ are better than $\omega$ near a circle because they stay finite and smooth as $e\to0$.
	
	\subsection{First-order equations for nearly circular motion}
	
	We now write the Gauss equations in $a$, $e_x$, $e_y$ to first order in $e$. For $a$, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:osculating_energy} and~\eqref{eq:planar_Edot} give $\dot a=(2a^2/GM\mu)\big[\dot rF_r+(L/\mu r)F_\phi\big]$. To first order, $\dot r=an\,\delta\phi/2$ and $L/(\mu r)=\sqrt{GMa}\,(1+e_x\cos\bar\phi+e_y\sin\bar\phi)/a=an(1+e_x\cos\bar\phi+e_y\sin\bar\phi)$. With $2a^3n/(GM\mu)=\sqrt{4a^3/(GM\mu^2)}$,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot a
			=
			\sqrt{\frac{4a^3}{GM\mu^2}}
			\left[
			\tfrac12\delta\phi\,F_r
			+
			\left(
			1+e_x\cos\bar\phi+e_y\sin\bar\phi
			\right)F_{\phi}
			\right]
			+O(e^2F).
		}
		\label{eq:LL_adot}
	\end{equation}
	
	The eccentricity vector is, exactly,
	\begin{equation}
		\vec A
		=
		GM\mu^2
		\left(
		e_x\hatn+e_y\hat y
		\right).
	\end{equation}
	The frame $(\hatn,\hat y)$ is fixed for a planar force, so $\dot e_x=\dot{\vecA}\cdot\hatn/(GM\mu^2)$ and $\dot e_y=\dot{\vecA}\cdot\hat y/(GM\mu^2)$. With $\rhat\cdot\hatn=\cos\phi$, $\phihat\cdot\hatn=-\sin\phi$, $\rhat\cdot\hat y=\sin\phi$ and $\phihat\cdot\hat y=\cos\phi$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:rhat_phihat}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:planar_Adot_xi} gives
	\begin{align}
		\dot e_x
		&\eqby{1}\frac{L}{GM\mu^2}\Big[F_r\sin\phi+F_\phi\big(2\cos\phi+\xi\sin\phi\big)\Big] \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{2}\sqrt{\frac{a}{GM\mu^2}}\Big[F_r\big(\sin\bar\phi+\delta\phi\cos\bar\phi\big)+F_\phi\big(2\cos\bar\phi-2\delta\phi\sin\bar\phi+\tfrac12\delta\phi\sin\bar\phi\big)\Big]+O(e^2F),
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\dot{\vecA}\cdot\hatn=L\big[2F_\phi\cos\phi+(F_r+\xi F_\phi)\sin\phi\big]$.
		\item $L/(GM\mu^2)=\sqrt{a(1-e^2)/(GM\mu^2)}=\sqrt{a/(GM\mu^2)}+O(e^2)$, $\sin\phi=\sin\bar\phi+\delta\phi\cos\bar\phi$, $\cos\phi=\cos\bar\phi-\delta\phi\sin\bar\phi$ and $\xi=\delta\phi/2$, all to first order.
	\end{explain}
	and in the same way for $\dot e_y$, with $\dot{\vecA}\cdot\hat y=L\big[-F_r\cos\phi+F_\phi(2\sin\phi-\xi\cos\phi)\big]$. Collecting terms,
	\begin{align}
		\boxed{
			\dot e_x
			=
			\sqrt{\frac{a}{GM\mu^2}}
			\left[
			\sin\bar\phi\,F_r
			+
			2\cos\bar\phi\,F_{\phi}
			+
			\tfrac12\delta\phi
			\left(
			2\cos\bar\phi\,F_r-3\sin\bar\phi\,F_{\phi}
			\right)
			\right]
			+O(e^2F),
		}
		\label{eq:LL_eps1dot}
		\\[6pt]
		\boxed{
			\dot e_y
			=
			\sqrt{\frac{a}{GM\mu^2}}
			\left[
			-\cos\bar\phi\,F_r
			+
			2\sin\bar\phi\,F_{\phi}
			+
			\tfrac12\delta\phi
			\left(
			2\sin\bar\phi\,F_r+3\cos\bar\phi\,F_{\phi}
			\right)
			\right]
			+O(e^2F).
		}
		\label{eq:LL_eps2dot}
	\end{align}
	Neither equation contains $1/e$.
	
	For the unperturbed orbit $\dot{\bar\phi}=n$ exactly. The force changes this rate only by $O(F)$,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\dot{\bar\phi}=n+O(F).
		}
		\label{eq:LL_Phi_dot}
	\end{equation}
	The rates above are already $O(F)$, so dividing them by $\dot{\bar\phi}$ instead of $n$ changes them only at $O(F^2)$.
	
	Using $\bar\phi$ as the independent variable, $d/d\bar\phi=n^{-1}d/dt$, with $\sqrt{a/(GM\mu^2)}/n=a^2/(GM\mu)$ and $\sqrt{4a^3/(GM\mu^2)}/n=2a^3/(GM\mu)$,
	\begin{align}
		\boxed{
			\frac{da}{d\bar\phi}
			=
			\frac{2a^3}{GM\mu}
			\left[
			\tfrac12\delta\phi\,F_r
			+
			\left(
			1+e_x\cos\bar\phi+e_y\sin\bar\phi
			\right)F_{\phi}
			\right],
		}
		\label{eq:LL_dadPhi}
		\\
		\boxed{
			\frac{de_x}{d\bar\phi}
			=
			\frac{a^2}{GM\mu}
			\left[
			\sin\bar\phi\,F_r
			+
			2\cos\bar\phi\,F_{\phi}
			+
			\tfrac12\delta\phi
			\left(
			2\cos\bar\phi\,F_r-3\sin\bar\phi\,F_{\phi}
			\right)
			\right],
		}
		\label{eq:LL_deps1dPhi}
		\\
		\boxed{
			\frac{de_y}{d\bar\phi}
			=
			\frac{a^2}{GM\mu}
			\left[
			-\cos\bar\phi\,F_r
			+
			2\sin\bar\phi\,F_{\phi}
			+
			\tfrac12\delta\phi
			\left(
			2\sin\bar\phi\,F_r+3\cos\bar\phi\,F_{\phi}
			\right)
			\right].
		}
		\label{eq:LL_deps2dPhi}
	\end{align}
	
	
	
	\section{Rømer delay for a Keplerian binary}
	\label{sec:romer}
	
	We have the position of each star in its orbit, and its distance along the line of sight, the $Z$ of Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:sky_xyz}.
	
	For a pulsar in a binary we cannot see the position at all. What we measure is the arrival time of each pulse.
	
	A drummer marching to and fro along the line between us and a distant field beats a perfectly steady rhythm. We hear the beats bunch up as he comes towards us and spread out as he goes away, because the sound from the far end has farther to travel.
	
	A pulsar in a binary is the drummer, and its pulses are the beats. The idea is that each pulse arrives late by the extra light-travel distance $\vecx_1\cdot\hat R/c$, so the timing measures the line-of-sight coordinate of the orbit.
	
	The Earth in numbers. Light crosses $1\,\mathrm{AU}$ in $499\,\mathrm s$. A source in the plane of the Earth's orbit has its signals arrive up to $499\,\mathrm s$ early or late over a year, as the Earth moves towards it and away. R{\o}mer found the speed of light in 1676 from this delay in the eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io.
	
	The picture is the orbit seen edge on, with the pulsar moving nearer to us and farther away.
	
	The same delay, for the Earth's own orbit, is part of correcting every pulse arrival time to the centre of mass of the Solar System, and in a pulsar's timing it reveals planets around the pulsar. The orbit comes from P1 and P2, and the rest is geometry. It gives the delay in terms of the eccentric anomaly, and with it the masses of neutron stars in Chapter~\ref{ch:compact}.
	
	\paragraph{The delay.} For a binary of component masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, $M=m_1+m_2$. Let $\vecr$ be the relative separation. The primary's position relative to the centre of mass is, by Section~\ref{sec:com_separation},
	\begin{equation}
		\vecx_1
		=
		\frac{m_2}{M}\vecr .
	\end{equation}
	
	$\hat R$ points from the observer towards the source (Section~\ref{sec:coord_orbital_elements}). A primary that is farther away by $\vecx_1\cdot\hat R$ than the centre of mass sends pulses that arrive later by
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta_R
		=
		\frac{\vecx_1\cdot\hat R}{c}.
	\end{equation}
	
	In the orbital-plane basis,
	\begin{equation}
		\vecr
		=
		r
		\left[
		\cos\phi\,\hatn+\sin\phi\,\hat y
		\right],
	\end{equation}
	with
	\begin{equation}
		\phi=\omega+v,
	\end{equation}
	and, from the third components of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:node} and~\eqref{eq:yhat},
	\begin{equation}
		\hat y\cdot\hat R=\sin\iota,
		\qquad
		\hatn\cdot\hat R=0,
	\end{equation}
	so that
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta_R
		=
		\frac{m_2}{Mc}\,
		r\sin\iota\,\sin(\omega+v).
	\end{equation}
	This is $(m_2/M)\,Z/c$ with $Z$ from Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:sky_xyz}.
	
	We define the primary's semi-major axis about the centre of mass,
	\begin{equation}
		a_1=\frac{m_2}{M}a,
	\end{equation}
	and use the eccentric-anomaly relations \eqref{tb:eq:xy_of_u},
	\begin{align}
		r\cos v
		&=
		a(\cos u-e),
		\\
		r\sin v
		&=
		a\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin u.
	\end{align}
	Then
	\begin{align}
		\Delta_R
		&=\frac{m_2}{Mc}\sin\iota\,\big(r\cos v\,\sin\omega+r\sin v\,\cos\omega\big) \nonumber\\
		&\eqby{1}\frac{a_1\sin\iota}{c}\Big[(\cos u-e)\sin\omega+\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin u\cos\omega\Big],
	\end{align}
	\begin{explain}
		\item $\sin(\omega+v)=\sin\omega\cos v+\cos\omega\sin v$ was used in the first line, then Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:xy_of_u} and $a_1=(m_2/M)a$.
	\end{explain}
	so
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{
			\Delta_R
			=
			\frac{a_1\sin\iota}{c}
			\left[
			(\cos u-e)\sin\omega
			+
			\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin u\cos\omega
			\right].
		}
		\label{eq:romer_delay}
	\end{equation}
	Only the projected size $a_1\sin\iota$ enters, the same combination that a radial-velocity curve gives (Problem~9). Timing alone does not give $\iota$.

\label{tb:sec:problems}

\subsection{Problem 1: Astrometric Binary White Dwarf System}
\label{tb:prob:astrometric}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Two white dwarfs in a binary system appear on the sky plane as concentric circles of radii $10\,\mu\text{as}$ and $5\,\mu\text{as}$, respectively, moving anticlockwise. The observed orbital period is $1\,\text{day}$. The system has a parallax of $1\,\text{mas}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item What is the semi-major axis of the relative orbit?
	\item The stars appear to move with constant angular speed along circular trajectories. What are the orbital eccentricity $e$ and inclination $\iota$?
	\item Find the individual masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ and the total mass $M$.
	\item If the orbit were inclined, what would be the orientation of the orbit in space? Specify the angles $\Omega$ and $\omega$.
	\item What is the magnitude of the Runge--Lenz vector for this system?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Center of mass}: For a binary system, both stars orbit their mutual center of mass with identical period $P$. Their individual barycentric semi-major axes satisfy
	\[
	a=a_1+a_2, \qquad m_1a_1=m_2a_2,
	\]
	where $a$ is the semi-major axis of the relative orbit.
	
	\begin{figure}[htbp]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Two Body Problem.png}
		\caption{Two stars and their common center of mass, with the position vectors $\vec r_1=(m_2/M)\vec r$ and $\vec r_2=-(m_1/M)\vec r$ along the relative separation $\vec r$.}
		\label{fig:two-body-problem}
	\end{figure}
	
	\item \textbf{Projected orbit}: A circular orbit tilted with respect to the sky plane projects to an ellipse with axis ratio $\cos\iota$ (Section~\ref{sec:coord_orbital_elements}). Therefore an observed circle requires $\iota=0^\circ$. Constant orbital speed then implies a circular Kepler orbit, so $e=0$.
	
	\item \textbf{Runge--Lenz vector}: The Runge--Lenz vector points towards the periapsis with magnitude
	\[
	|\vec A| = GM\mu^2 e .
	\]
	For a circular orbit ($e=0$), $\vec A = \vec 0$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Semi-major axis of the relative orbit.}
	
	The distance $d$ to the binary follows from the parallax of $1\,\text{mas} = 10^{-3\,\prime\prime}$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:astrometry}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:parallax_distance_pc}),
	\[
	d = \frac{1\,\mathrm{pc}}{10^{-3}} = 10^3\,\mathrm{pc}.
	\]
	An angle of $1''$ at $1\,\mathrm{pc}$ is $1\,\mathrm{AU}$, so the physical barycentric orbital radii are
	\begin{align*}
		a_1 &= \theta_1 d = (10\,\mu\text{as})(10^3\,\text{pc}) = (10^{-5\,\prime\prime})(10^3\,\text{pc}) = 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{AU} \simeq 1.496 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{m}, \\
		a_2 &= \theta_2 d = (5\,\mu\text{as})(10^3\,\text{pc}) = (5 \times 10^{-6\,\prime\prime})(10^3\,\text{pc}) = 0.5 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{AU} \simeq 7.48 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m}.
	\end{align*}
	Therefore the relative separation has the semi-major axis
	\[
	\boxed{a = a_1 + a_2 = 1.5 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{AU} = 2.244 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{m}}.
	\]
	
	\begin{figure}[htbp]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Binary_Star.png}
		\caption{Two stars on circular orbits about their common centre of mass, with the barycentric radii $r_1$ and $r_2$ marked.}
		\label{fig:binarystar}
	\end{figure}
	
	\item \textbf{Eccentricity and inclination.}
	
	Since the observed trajectories are circles, the projection factor satisfies $\cos\iota = 1$, so $\boxed{\iota = 0^\circ}$. Since the orbital speed is constant, the orbit is circular, $\boxed{e = 0}$.
	
	\item \textbf{Masses.}
	
	The mass ratio is the inverse of the ratio of the barycentric semi-major axes,
	\[
	\frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{a_2}{a_1} = \frac{5}{10} = \frac{1}{2} \implies m_2 = 2m_1.
	\]
	From Kepler's third law, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kepler3},
	\[
	P^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{G(m_1+m_2)} = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{3Gm_1}.
	\]
	With $P = 1\,\text{day} = 86400\,\text{s}$ and $a = 2.244 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{m}$,
	\[
	M = m_1 + m_2 = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{G P^2} \simeq 8.95 \times 10^{29}\,\mathrm{kg} \simeq 0.450\,M_\odot.
	\]
	Thus
	\[
	\boxed{m_1 \simeq 0.150\,M_\odot}, \qquad \boxed{m_2 \simeq 0.300\,M_\odot}.
	\]
	The same follows in solar units, $M/M_\odot=(a/\mathrm{AU})^3/(P/\mathrm{yr})^2=(1.5\times10^{-2})^3\times365.25^2=0.450$. Both masses are well below the Chandrasekhar mass and are typical of the low-mass white dwarfs found in close double-degenerate binaries. Their separation, $3.2\,R_\odot$, is small enough for a one-day orbit and far larger than either white dwarf. The orbit spans $10\,\mu\text{as}$ on the sky, near the limit of what Gaia can measure.
	
	\item \textbf{Orientation angles $\Omega$ and $\omega$.}
	
	For a circular face-on orbit ($\iota=0^\circ$, $e=0$), the line of nodes is undefined, since the orbital and sky planes do not intersect, and the periapsis is undefined, since the distance is constant. Hence neither $\Omega$ nor $\omega$ is defined. If the same circular orbit were inclined, the node line would exist and $\Omega$ would be defined, but $\omega$ would still be undefined because a circle has no periapsis.
	
	\item \textbf{Runge--Lenz vector.}
	
	Because the orbit is circular ($e=0$), $\vec A = \vec 0$. A circle has no distinguished periapsis direction, which is why the Runge--Lenz vector vanishes.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 2: Bounds on the Symmetric Mass Ratio}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Prove that the symmetric mass ratio $\eta = \frac{m_1m_2}{M^2}$ (where $M = m_1+m_2$) satisfies $\eta \in (0, 0.25]$.

\textbf{Solution}: Since $m_1, m_2 > 0$, $\eta > 0$. For the upper bound,
\begin{align*}
	(m_1-m_2)^2 \ge 0 &\implies m_1^2 - 2m_1m_2 + m_2^2 \ge 0 \\
	&\implies (m_1+m_2)^2 \ge 4m_1m_2 \\
	&\implies \eta = \frac{m_1m_2}{(m_1+m_2)^2} \le \frac{1}{4}.
\end{align*}
Equality holds if and only if $m_1 = m_2$. Hence $\boxed{\eta \in (0, 0.25]}$. Since $\eta=\mu/M$, the reduced mass is at most a quarter of the total mass.

\subsection{Problem 3: Numerical Solution of Kepler's Equation}
\label{tb:prob:kepler_newton}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Solve Kepler's equation $\ell = u - e\sin u$ using Newton's method and plot $u(\ell)$ for $e = 0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9$.

\textbf{Newton's Iteration Scheme}:
For fixed mean anomaly $\ell$ and eccentricity $e$, let $g(u) = u - e\sin u - \ell$. Then $g'(u) = 1 - e\cos u$, and Newton's method gives the recurrence
\begin{equation}
	u_{k+1} = u_k - \frac{u_k - e\sin u_k - \ell}{1 - e\cos u_k}.
\end{equation}
The derivative $1-e\cos u\ge1-e>0$ never vanishes for $e<1$, so each step is defined. Starting from $u_0=\ell$, the error is already $e\sin\ell$, and Newton's method then roughly squares it at each step. The listing below implements it (the code calls the mean anomaly \texttt{l}).

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

def solver(l_vals, e, tol=1e-12, max_iter=100):
    u_vals = np.zeros_like(l_vals)
    for i, l in enumerate(l_vals):
        u = l
        for _ in range(max_iter):
            f = u - e * np.sin(u) - l
            fp = 1 - e * np.cos(u)
            u = u - f / fp
            if abs(f / fp) < tol:
                break
        u_vals[i] = u
    return u_vals

l_grid = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 500)
eccentricities = [0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9]

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 6))
for e in eccentricities:
    u_grid = solver(l_grid, e)
    plt.plot(l_grid, u_grid, label=f"e = {e}")
plt.xlabel(r"Mean anomaly $l$ (rad)")
plt.ylabel(r"Eccentric anomaly $u$ (rad)")
plt.title(r"Kepler equation: $u(l)$")
plt.grid(True)
plt.legend()
plt.show()
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Problem 4: Relation Between True and Eccentric Anomalies}
\label{tb:prob:anomalies}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Prove that the eccentric anomaly $u$ and true anomaly $v$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
	\tan\frac{v}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan\frac{u}{2}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Proof}:
Equating the two expressions for the orbital radius, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:r_of_u} and~\eqref{eq:conic},
\[
r = a(1-e\cos u) = \frac{a(1-e^2)}{1+e\cos v} \implies 1+e\cos v = \frac{1-e^2}{1-e\cos u}.
\]
Solving for $\cos v$ as in Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:cosv},
\[
\cos v = \frac{\cos u - e}{1 - e\cos u}.
\]
Then
\[
1 - \cos v = \frac{1-e\cos u-\cos u+e}{1-e\cos u}=\frac{(1+e)(1-\cos u)}{1-e\cos u},
\]
\[
1 + \cos v = \frac{1-e\cos u+\cos u-e}{1-e\cos u}=\frac{(1-e)(1+\cos u)}{1-e\cos u}.
\]
Dividing the two equations, and using $\tan^2(x/2)=(1-\cos x)/(1+\cos x)$,
\[
\frac{1-\cos v}{1+\cos v} = \frac{1+e}{1-e}\frac{1-\cos u}{1+\cos u} \implies \tan^2\frac{v}{2} = \frac{1+e}{1-e}\tan^2\frac{u}{2}.
\]
The half-angles $u/2$ and $v/2$ lie in the same quadrant, since $u$ and $v$ are on the same side of the periapsis, so taking the positive root gives
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\tan\frac{v}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan\frac{u}{2}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Problem 5: Effective Potential and Hohmann Transfer Orbits}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{
	\includegraphics{fig_ch6_effective_potential_well.pdf}
	}
	\caption{The effective potential $V_{\rm eff}(r) = -k/r^n + L^2/(2mr^2)$ against $r$, with the energy levels of parabolic, circular, elliptical and hyperbolic orbits, the turning points $r_{\rm min}$ and $r_{\rm max}$ at one bound energy, and the curve for $L=0$.}
	\label{fig:effective_potential_well}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: The relative-orbit energy \eqref{tb:eq:energy_polar}, divided by $\mu$ as in Problem~10, reads
\[
\mathcal E\equiv\frac{E}{\mu}=\frac12\dot r^2+V_{\rm eff}(r), \qquad V_{\rm eff}(r)\equiv\frac{h^2}{2r^2}-\frac{GM}{r}, \qquad h\equiv\frac{L}{\mu}.
\]
This is one-dimensional motion in the \emph{effective potential} $V_{\rm eff}(r)$ per unit mass. It is Newtonian gravity's case of the general power-law form $V_{\rm eff}=-k/r^n+L^2/(2mr^2)$ of Figure~\ref{fig:effective_potential_well}, with $n=1$, $k=GM$ and $m=1$. Since $\tfrac12\dot r^2\ge0$ always, the radii reachable at fixed $\mathcal E$ are exactly those with $V_{\rm eff}(r)\le\mathcal E$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item Find the circular-orbit radius $r_{\rm circle}$, where $V_{\rm eff}$ is stationary, and the corresponding energy $\mathcal E_{\rm circle}\equiv V_{\rm eff}(r_{\rm circle})$, in terms of $h$ and $GM$.
	\item For $\mathcal E_{\rm circle}<\mathcal E<0$, solve $V_{\rm eff}(r)=\mathcal E$ for its two roots $r_{\rm min},r_{\rm max}$, and show they are exactly the periapsis and apoapsis $a(1\mp e)$ already implied by Eqs.~\eqref{eq:visviva_E} and~\eqref{eq:L_of_ae}.
	\item Show that $\mathcal E=\mathcal E_{\rm circle}$, $\mathcal E=0$, and $\mathcal E>0$ correspond to the circular, parabolic, and hyperbolic rows of the table of conic sections in Section~\ref{tb:sec:kepler_orbits}.
	\item An object orbits the Sun ($GM_\odot=1.327\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$) with $h=4.089\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $\mathcal E=-4.423\times10^{8}\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$. Find $r_{\rm min}$, $r_{\rm max}$, and $e$.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item Setting $V_{\rm eff}'(r)=-h^2/r^3+GM/r^2=0$,
	\[
	\boxed{r_{\rm circle}=\frac{h^2}{GM}}, \qquad
	\mathcal E_{\rm circle}=\frac{h^2}{2r_{\rm circle}^2}-\frac{GM}{r_{\rm circle}}=\frac{GM}{2r_{\rm circle}}-\frac{GM}{r_{\rm circle}}
	\;\Longrightarrow\;
	\boxed{\mathcal E_{\rm circle}=-\frac{G^2M^2}{2h^2}.}
	\]
	
	\item Multiplying $V_{\rm eff}(r)=\mathcal E$ through by $2r^2$ gives the quadratic $2\mathcal E r^2+2GMr-h^2=0$, with roots
	\[
	r=\frac{-GM\pm\sqrt{G^2M^2+2\mathcal E h^2}}{2\mathcal E}.
	\]
	Substituting $\mathcal E=-GM/(2a)$ and $h^2=GMa(1-e^2)$, which are Eqs.~\eqref{eq:visviva_E} and~\eqref{eq:L_of_ae} divided by $\mu$ and $\mu^2$, gives $2\mathcal E h^2=-G^2M^2(1-e^2)$. The discriminant becomes $G^2M^2e^2$, and with $1/(2\mathcal E)=-a/GM$,
	\[
	\boxed{r_{\rm min}=a(1-e), \qquad r_{\rm max}=a(1+e),}
	\]
	the periapsis and apoapsis distances. The turning points of the effective potential and the geometry of the conic are two descriptions of the same orbit.
	
	\item From $\mathcal E=-GM/(2a)$. $\mathcal E=\mathcal E_{\rm circle}$ forces $r_{\rm min}=r_{\rm max}$, so $e=0$ (circular). $\mathcal E=0$ forces $a\to\infty$, so $e=1$ (parabolic). $\mathcal E>0$ forces $a<0$, so $e>1$ (hyperbolic). This is the classification in the table of Section~\ref{tb:sec:kepler_orbits}.
	
	\item With $GM=1.327\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$, $r_{\rm circle}=h^2/GM=1.260\times10^{11}\,\mathrm m$. From $\mathcal E=-GM/(2a)$, $a=-GM/(2\mathcal E)=1.500\times10^{11}\,\mathrm m$. Then $h^2=GMa(1-e^2)$ gives $1-e^2=h^2/(GMa)=0.8400$, so
	\[
	\boxed{e\simeq0.400, \qquad r_{\rm min}=a(1-e)\simeq9.00\times10^{10}\,\mathrm m, \qquad r_{\rm max}=a(1+e)\simeq2.10\times10^{11}\,\mathrm m.}
	\]
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 6: Hohmann Transfer Impulses and Energy Budget}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch6_hohmann_transfer_ellipse.pdf}
	\caption{A Hohmann transfer ellipse between an initial circular orbit of radius $r_1$ and a geostationary orbit of radius $r_2$.}
	\label{fig:hohmann_transfer_ellipse}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: A satellite of mass $m=1000\,\mathrm{kg}$ transfers from an initial circular orbit of period $P_1 = 6\,\mathrm{h}$ to a geostationary orbit ($P_{\rm GEO} \approx 86164\,\mathrm{s}$), using $GM_\oplus=3.986\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item Find the intermediate transfer eccentricity $e$.
	\item Find the impulsive velocity boosts $\Delta v_1, \Delta v_2$ and the required impulses $m\Delta v_1$, $m\Delta v_2$.
	\item Find the total energy and angular momentum supplied.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Solution}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item By Kepler's third law \eqref{eq:kepler3}, the initial and final radii are
	\[
	r_1 = \left(\frac{GM_\oplus P_1^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3} \simeq 1.676 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{m}, \qquad r_2 = \left(\frac{GM_\oplus P_{\rm GEO}^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3} \simeq 4.216 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{m}.
	\]
	The transfer ellipse touches both circles, with periapsis $r_1=a_t(1-e)$ and apoapsis $r_2=a_t(1+e)$. So $a_t = (r_1+r_2)/2$, and the eccentricity is
	\[
	\boxed{e = \frac{r_2-r_1}{r_2+r_1} \simeq 0.431}.
	\]
	\item The circular speeds are $v_{c1} = \sqrt{GM_\oplus/r_1} \simeq 4.876\,\mathrm{km/s}$ and $v_{c2} = \sqrt{GM_\oplus/r_2} \simeq 3.075\,\mathrm{km/s}$.
	At perigee of the transfer orbit, the vis-viva equation \eqref{tb:eq:visviva_speed} gives
	\[
	v_p = \sqrt{GM_\oplus(2/r_1 - 1/a_t)} \simeq 5.833\,\mathrm{km/s}.
	\]
	Thus
	\[
	\Delta v_1 = v_p - v_{c1} \simeq 0.957\,\mathrm{km/s} \implies \boxed{m\Delta v_1 \simeq 9.57 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{N\,s}}.
	\]
	At apogee, $v_a = \sqrt{GM_\oplus(2/r_2 - 1/a_t)} \simeq 2.319\,\mathrm{km/s}$. Thus
	\[
	\Delta v_2 = v_{c2} - v_a \simeq 0.755\,\mathrm{km/s} \implies \boxed{m\Delta v_2 \simeq 7.55 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{N\,s}}.
	\]
	\item The energy of a circular orbit is $-GM_\oplus m/(2r)$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:visviva_E}, and its angular momentum is $m\sqrt{GM_\oplus r}$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:L_of_ae} with $e=0$. The total energy and angular momentum supplied are
	\[
	\boxed{\Delta E = -\frac{GM_\oplus m}{2r_2} + \frac{GM_\oplus m}{2r_1} \simeq 7.16 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{J}},
	\]
	\[
	\boxed{\Delta L = m\sqrt{GM_\oplus r_2} - m\sqrt{GM_\oplus r_1} \simeq 4.79 \times 10^{13}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}}.
	\]
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 7: Determining Orbital Elements from Initial State Vectors}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Given the initial relative position $\vecr_0$ and velocity $\dot{\vecr}_0$ at $t=0$, derive the orbital elements $(a, e, \Omega, \iota, \omega, t_0)$ of a bound orbit.

\textbf{Solution}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Conserved quantities.} The specific energy $\mathcal E=E/\mu = \dot{\vecr}_0^{\,2}/2 - GM/r_0$, the specific angular momentum $\vec h = \vec L/\mu=\vecr_0 \times \dot{\vecr}_0$, and the eccentricity vector $\vec e=\vecA/(GM\mu^2) = \dot{\vecr}_0 \times \vec h/GM - \hat r_0$, Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:ecc_vector}.
	\item \textbf{Orbital elements.} From the vis-viva equation \eqref{tb:eq:visviva_speed} and Eqs.~\eqref{eq:A_magnitude}, \eqref{eq:kepler3} and~\eqref{eq:Lhat},
	\[
	a = \left(\frac{2}{r_0} - \frac{\dot{\vecr}_0^{\,2}}{GM}\right)^{-1}, \qquad e = |\vec e|, \qquad n = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{a^3}}, \qquad \iota = \arccos\left(\frac{\vec h\cdot\hat R}{h}\right).
	\]
	The node vector $\vec N = \hat R \times \vec h$ points to the ascending node, since $\hatn$ is perpendicular to both $\hat R$ and $\hat L$. Then
	\[
	\Omega = \operatorname{atan2}(\vec N\cdot\hat\delta,\ \vec N\cdot\hat\alpha), \qquad \omega = \operatorname{atan2}\left(\frac{\hat h \cdot (\vec N \times \vec e)}{Ne}, \frac{\vec N \cdot \vec e}{Ne}\right),
	\]
	from Eqs.~\eqref{eq:node} and~\eqref{eq:Ahat}.
	\item \textbf{Time of periapsis.} The initial true anomaly has $\cos v_0=\vec e\cdot\hat r_0/e$, with $\sin v_0$ of the same sign as $\dot r_0=\hat r_0\cdot\dot{\vecr}_0$. The eccentric anomaly $u_0$ follows from Eq.~\eqref{eq:v_of_u}, $\tan(u_0/2)=\sqrt{(1-e)/(1+e)}\tan(v_0/2)$. Kepler's equation at $t=0$ gives $\ell_0=-nt_0=u_0-e\sin u_0$, so the time of periapsis passage is
	\[
	t_0 = -\frac{u_0 - e\sin u_0}{n}.
	\]
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 8: Continuous Tangential Thrust Orbital Evolution}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: A $500\,\mathrm{kg}$ satellite initially moves in a circular Earth orbit of period $P = 8\,\mathrm{h}$. At $t=0$, a thruster of force $F_{\rm T}=100\,\mathrm N$ is switched on, always directed along the instantaneous velocity $\dot{\vecr}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item Find the initial orbital radius $a(0)$.
	\item Derive the rate of change of the semi-major axis $\dot a$.
	\item Derive the rate of change of the eccentricity $\dot e$ from the exact rate of the Laplace--Runge--Lenz vector $\vec A$.
	\item Can the coupled evolution equations for $(a, e)$ be solved analytically?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Thrust acceleration and initial radius}: The satellite's mass is negligible next to the Earth's, so $\mu=m=500\,\mathrm{kg}$. The thrust acceleration is $F_{\rm T}/\mu = 100\,\mathrm{N}/500\,\mathrm{kg} = 0.2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. For an initially circular orbit ($e=0$) of period $P = 8\,\mathrm{h} = 28{,}800\,\mathrm{s}$ around the Earth ($GM_\oplus \approx 3.986 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$), Kepler's third law gives
	\[
	a(0) = \left(\frac{GM_\oplus P^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3} \simeq 2.03 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{m} \approx 20{,}300\,\mathrm{km}.
	\]
	\item \textbf{Perturbation force vector}: The thrust is tangential to the path, $\vec F = F_{\rm T}\,\dot{\vecr}/|\dot{\vecr}|$. In polar coordinates the velocity is $\dot{\vecr} = \dot r\,\rhat + r\dot\phi\,\phihat = \dot r\,\rhat + \frac{L}{\mu r}\,\phihat$, so $\vec F = F_r\rhat + F_\phi\phihat$ has the components
	\[
	F_r = F_{\rm T}\frac{\dot r}{|\dot{\vecr}|}, \qquad F_\phi = \frac{F_{\rm T}L}{\mu r|\dot{\vecr}|}.
	\]
	With $\xi \equiv \mu r\dot r/L = e\sin v/(1+e\cos v)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:xi_def}), the radial component is $F_r = \xi F_\phi$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Initial semi-major axis}.
	\[
	\boxed{a(0) = \left(\frac{GM_\oplus P^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\frac{3.986 \times 10^{14} \times (28800)^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3} \simeq 2.03 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{m}}.
	\]
	
	\item \textbf{Evolution of the semi-major axis $\dot a$}.
	The work done per unit time by the tangential thrust is, by Eq.~\eqref{eq:perturbed_energy_rate},
	\[
	\dot E = \vec F \cdot \dot{\vecr} = F_{\rm T}|\dot{\vecr}|.
	\]
	The energy $E = -GM_\oplus\mu / (2a)$ changes at the rate $\dot E=(GM_\oplus\mu/2a^2)\dot a$, so
	\[
	\frac{GM_\oplus\mu}{2a^2}\dot a = F_{\rm T}|\dot{\vecr}| \implies \dot a = \frac{2a^2}{GM_\oplus}\,\frac{F_{\rm T}}{\mu}\,|\dot{\vecr}|.
	\]
	The vis-viva equation \eqref{tb:eq:visviva_speed} with $r=a(1-e^2)/(1+e\cos v)$ gives
	\[
	|\dot{\vecr}|^2 = GM_\oplus\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right) = GM_\oplus\,\frac{2(1+e\cos v)-(1-e^2)}{a(1-e^2)}=\frac{GM_\oplus}{a}\,\frac{1+2e\cos v + e^2}{1-e^2},
	\]
	so the exact rate of change of the semi-major axis is
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{\frac{da}{dt} = \frac{2F_{\rm T}}{\mu}\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM_\oplus}}\sqrt{\frac{1+2e\cos v+e^2}{1-e^2}}}.
		\label{eq:prob8_adot}
	\end{equation}
	
	\item \textbf{Evolution of the eccentricity $\dot e$ via the Laplace--Runge--Lenz vector}.
	The Laplace--Runge--Lenz vector $\vec A = \vec p\times\vec L - GM\mu^2\hat r$ has magnitude $A = GM_\oplus\mu^2 e$ and points to the periapsis, $\hat A = \cos v\,\rhat - \sin v\,\phihat$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:Ahat_f}).
	
	Under a general planar perturbing force $\vec F = F_r\rhat + F_\phi\phihat$, the exact rate of change of $\vec A$ is Eq.~\eqref{eq:planar_Adot_xi},
	\[
	\dot{\vec A} = \vec F\times\vec L + \vec p\times(\vec r\times\vec F) = L\left[2F_\phi\rhat - (F_r + \xi F_\phi)\phihat\right].
	\]
	Since $\hat A \cdot \hat A = 1$ gives $\hat A \cdot \dot{\hat A} = 0$, the rate $\dot A$ is the projection of $\dot{\vec A}$ on $\hat A$,
	\begin{align*}
		\dot A &= \dot{\vec A}\cdot\hat A = L\left[2F_\phi\rhat - (F_r+\xi F_\phi)\phihat\right]\cdot (\cos v\,\rhat - \sin v\,\phihat) \\
		&= L\left[ 2F_\phi\cos v + (F_r + \xi F_\phi)\sin v \right] \\
		&= L\left[ F_r\sin v + F_\phi(2\cos v + \xi\sin v) \right].
	\end{align*}
	Evaluating this on the instantaneous osculating orbit with $A = GM_\oplus\mu^2 e$ and $L = \mu\sqrt{GM_\oplus a(1-e^2)}$ gives Gauss's equation for the eccentricity, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gauss_edot},
	\begin{equation}
		\dot e = \sqrt{\frac{a(1-e^2)}{GM_\oplus\mu^2}}\left[ F_r\sin v + F_\phi(2\cos v + \xi\sin v) \right].
	\end{equation}
	Now we substitute the components for tangential thrust, $F_r = \xi F_\phi$ and $F_\phi = \dfrac{L}{r}\,\dfrac{F_{\rm T}}{\mu|\dot{\vecr}|}$,
	\begin{align*}
		F_r\sin v + F_\phi(2\cos v + \xi\sin v) &= \frac{F_{\rm T}L}{\mu r|\dot{\vecr}|}\left[ \xi\sin v + 2\cos v + \xi\sin v \right] \\
		&= \frac{2F_{\rm T}L}{\mu r|\dot{\vecr}|}\left(\cos v + \xi\sin v\right).
	\end{align*}
	Using $\xi = e\sin v/(1+e\cos v)$,
	\[
	\cos v + \xi\sin v = \cos v + \frac{e\sin^2 v}{1+e\cos v} = \frac{\cos v(1+e\cos v) + e\sin^2 v}{1+e\cos v} = \frac{\cos v + e}{1+e\cos v}.
	\]
	With $\dfrac{L}{r} = \dfrac{\mu\sqrt{GM_\oplus a(1-e^2)}}{a(1-e^2)/(1+e\cos v)} = \dfrac{\mu\sqrt{GM_\oplus}\,(1+e\cos v)}{\sqrt{a(1-e^2)}}$,
	\begin{align*}
		\dot e &= \sqrt{\frac{a(1-e^2)}{GM_\oplus\mu^2}} \cdot \frac{2F_{\rm T}}{\mu|\dot{\vecr}|} \left[\frac{\mu\sqrt{GM_\oplus}(1+e\cos v)}{\sqrt{a(1-e^2)}}\right] \left[\frac{\cos v + e}{1+e\cos v}\right] \\
		&= \frac{2F_{\rm T}}{\mu}\,\frac{\cos v + e}{|\dot{\vecr}|}.
	\end{align*}
	Writing $|\dot{\vecr}|$ in terms of the elements with the vis-viva equation,
	\begin{equation}
		\boxed{\frac{de}{dt} = \frac{2F_{\rm T}}{\mu}\,\frac{\cos v + e}{|\dot{\vecr}|} = \frac{2F_{\rm T}}{\mu}\sqrt{\frac{a(1-e^2)}{GM_\oplus}}\,\frac{\cos v + e}{\sqrt{1+2e\cos v+e^2}}}.
		\label{eq:prob8_edot}
	\end{equation}
	Differentiating $e^2 = 1 + 2\mathcal E h^2/(GM_\oplus)^2$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:a_e_from_EL} with $\mathcal E=E/\mu$ and $h=L/\mu$) would give $\dot e$ divided by $e$, an undefined $0/0$ at $e=0$. The rate of the Runge--Lenz vector avoids this and gives the finite initial rate $\dot e\big|_{e=0} = (2F_{\rm T}/\mu)\sqrt{a/GM_\oplus}\cos v$.
	
	\item \textbf{Analytical solvability}.
	Equations~\eqref{eq:prob8_adot} and~\eqref{eq:prob8_edot} do not form a closed system for $a$ and $e$, because both rates depend on the orbital phase $v(t)$. The phase itself evolves by Eq.~\eqref{eq:gauss_planar_f},
	\[
	\dot v = \frac{L}{\mu r^2} - \dot\omega,
	\]
	where the periapsis rate $\dot\omega$ is also driven by the thrust. The thrust changes the orbit throughout each revolution, so the coupled system $(a(t), e(t), v(t))$ has no closed-form solution in elementary functions. In mission design it is solved by numerical integration, or by \emph{orbit averaging} over each orbital period (Section~\ref{tb:sec:averaged}),
	\[
	\avg{\dot a} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\dot a\,d\ell=\frac{1}{P}\int_0^{2\pi}\dot a\,\frac{\mu r^2}{L}\,dv .
	\]
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 9: Double-Lined Spectroscopic Binary --- Radial Velocities and Masses}
\label{prob:sb2}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: In a \emph{double-lined spectroscopic binary} (SB2), we see absorption lines from \emph{both} stars in the combined spectrum. Each line is Doppler-shifted by that star's own orbital motion around the center of mass. A Doppler shift only measures the \emph{radial velocity} $v_r\equiv\dot{\vecx}\cdot\hat R$, the part of a star's velocity $\dot{\vecx}$ along the line of sight $\hat R$. Motion across the sky, perpendicular to $\hat R$, does not stretch or compress the wavelength, so it leaves no Doppler signal at all. That sideways motion only shows up as proper motion (Chapter~\ref{ch:astrometry}), not in the spectrum. Tracking each star's wavelength shift separately gives two radial-velocity curves, $v_{r,1}(t)$ and $v_{r,2}(t)$. Both share the same period $P$ and eccentricity $e$, but usually have different semi-amplitudes $K_1$ and $K_2$ (Figures~\ref{fig:sb2_schematic} and \ref{fig:sb2_rvcurve}). A particular system is measured to have $P=4.20\,\mathrm{d}$, $e=0.15$, $K_1=88\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and $K_2=132\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item Without knowing the orbital inclination $\iota$, what can already be said about the mass ratio $m_1/m_2$?
	\item Derive $(m_1+m_2)\sin^3\iota$ in terms of $P$, $K_1$, $K_2$, and $e$, and evaluate it numerically.
	\item Hence find $m_1\sin^3\iota$ and $m_2\sin^3\iota$ individually.
	\item This same system is also observed to be eclipsing, so $\iota\simeq90^\circ$. What are the actual masses $m_1$ and $m_2$?
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\textwidth}
		\centering
		\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{%
		\includegraphics{fig_ch6_sb2_schematic.pdf}%
		}
		\caption{A binary at quadrature, with $m_1$, $m_2$, and the COM in a line. $m_1$ moves away from and $m_2$ towards the observer, along the line of sight $\hat R$.}
		\label{fig:sb2_schematic}
	\end{minipage}
	\hfill
	\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\textwidth}
		\centering
		\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{%
		\includegraphics{fig_ch6_sb2_rvcurve.pdf}%
		}
		\caption{Radial-velocity curves of both stars for a circular orbit. Two sine curves in antiphase about $\gamma$, with semi-amplitudes $K_1\neq K_2$.}
		\label{fig:sb2_rvcurve}
	\end{minipage}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Start from $\vec x_1=(m_2/M)\vec r$, $\vec x_2=-(m_1/M)\vec r$ (Section~\ref{sec:com_separation}), shifted by the centre-of-mass motion $\vec V_{\rm CM}t$. Differentiate and project onto $\hat R$,
	\[
	v_{r,1}-\gamma = \frac{m_2}{M}\,v_{r,\rm rel}, \qquad v_{r,2}-\gamma = -\frac{m_1}{M}\,v_{r,\rm rel}, \qquad \gamma\equiv\vec V_{\rm CM}\cdot\hat R .
	\]
	Here $v_{r,\rm rel}\equiv v_{r,1}-v_{r,2}$, and $\gamma$ is constant (Eq.~\eqref{eq:comEOM}). This holds at every phase. So
	\[
	\frac{K_1}{K_2}=\frac{m_2}{m_1},
	\]
	whatever $\iota$ and $e$ are.
	\item A Keplerian orbit's line-of-sight speed has semi-amplitude $K=2\pi a\sin\iota/(P\sqrt{1-e^2})$ (Problem~11). Apply this to the relative orbit ($a=a_1+a_2$). Then $v_{r,\rm rel}=v_{r,1}-v_{r,2}$ has amplitude $K_1+K_2$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Mass ratio}.
	\[
	\boxed{\frac{m_1}{m_2}=\frac{K_2}{K_1}=\frac{132}{88}=1.50.}
	\]
	
	\item \textbf{Total mass}. Apply $K=2\pi a\sin\iota/(P\sqrt{1-e^2})$ to the relative orbit, whose amplitude is $K_1+K_2$, so $a\sin\iota=(K_1+K_2)P\sqrt{1-e^2}/(2\pi)$. Kepler's third law \eqref{eq:kepler3} is $P^2=4\pi^2a^3/[G(m_1+m_2)]$, so
	\[
	(m_1+m_2)\sin^3\iota=\frac{4\pi^2(a\sin\iota)^3}{GP^2} = \frac{4\pi^2}{GP^2}\,\frac{(K_1+K_2)^3P^3(1-e^2)^{3/2}}{8\pi^3}
	\]
	\[
	=\frac{P(K_1+K_2)^3(1-e^2)^{3/2}}{2\pi G}.
	\]
	With $P=3.629\times10^5\,\mathrm s$, $K_1+K_2=2.20\times10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $(1-e^2)^{3/2}=0.966$,
	\[
	\boxed{(m_1+m_2)\sin^3\iota \simeq 8.91\times10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}\simeq 4.48\,M_\odot.}
	\]
	
	\item \textbf{Individual masses}. Use $m_1=\dfrac{K_2}{K_1+K_2}(m_1+m_2)$ and $m_2=\dfrac{K_1}{K_1+K_2}(m_1+m_2)$, the same centre-of-mass weighting as in (a).
	\[
	\boxed{m_1\sin^3\iota \simeq 2.69\,M_\odot,\qquad m_2\sin^3\iota \simeq 1.79\,M_\odot.}
	\]
	
	\item \textbf{Eclipsing case}. $\iota\simeq90^\circ$, so $\sin^3\iota\simeq1$ and the factors in (c) drop out.
	\[
	\boxed{m_1\simeq2.69\,M_\odot, \qquad m_2\simeq1.79\,M_\odot.}
	\]
	Double-lined eclipsing binaries are valuable for exactly this reason. Both masses, and from the light curve both radii, come out with almost no model assumptions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 10: Dynamical Mass of a Host Halo from a Satellite's Orbit}
\label{prob:halo_mass}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: A satellite of mass $m_s$, for example a dwarf galaxy or globular cluster, orbits a much bigger dark-matter halo of mass $M_h$. We measure its present distance $r$ from the Galactic center and its full relative velocity $\dot{\vecr}$ (from proper motion plus radial velocity). We also estimate its orbital eccentricity $e$ independently, for example from similar orbits in cosmological simulations. As in Section~\ref{sec:com_separation}, let $M\equiv m_s+M_h$ be the total mass and $\mu\equiv m_sM_h/M$ the reduced mass. If the satellite sits well outside the halo's inner mass, we can treat the halo as a point mass (P2). Then the satellite follows an ordinary Keplerian orbit, with energy $E=-GM\mu/(2a)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:visviva_E}) and angular momentum $\vec L=\mu\,\vec r\times\dot{\vecr}$, $L^2=GM\mu^2a(1-e^2)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:L_of_ae}).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item Show that $\mu$ cancels out of both the vis-viva equation and the angular-momentum relation, leaving two equations in the directly observable quantities $r$, $|\dot{\vecr}|$, and $h\equiv|\vec r\times\dot{\vecr}|=L/\mu$. Combine them to show that the total mass $M$ satisfies
	\[
	\frac{G^2(1-e^2)}{h^2}M^2-\frac{2G}{r}M+\dot{\vecr}^{\,2}=0,
	\]
	and solve for $M$ in terms of $r,|\dot{\vecr}|,h,e$.
	\item Suppose instead the satellite happens to be caught exactly at apocenter, where the velocity is purely tangential ($\dot{\vecr}\perp\vec r$) and $r=r_{\rm apo}=a(1+e)$. Show that the mass estimate then becomes the much simpler
	\[
	M=\frac{v_{\rm apo}^2\,r_{\rm apo}}{G(1-e)},
	\]
	where $v_{\rm apo}=|\dot{\vecr}|$ at apocenter.
	\item A dwarf satellite is observed at apocenter with $r_{\rm apo}=250\,\mathrm{kpc}$ and $v_{\rm apo}=95\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and its orbit is modeled to have $e=0.6$. Evaluate $M$, in kg and in $M_\odot$, and argue that it is an excellent estimate of $M_h$ alone.
	\item Eccentricity is only one possible choice of the ``extra'' datum used here. What property of the governing equations makes some third quantity beyond $r$ and $|\dot{\vecr}|$ necessary at all, and what else could play that role?
	\item This method returns only the total mass $M=m_s+M_h$, never $m_s$ and $M_h$ separately. Explain why no amount of additional \emph{orbital} data --- more precise $r$, $\dot{\vecr}$, or $e$, or even more satellites on more orbits --- could ever split $M$ into $m_s$ and $M_h$ individually, and say what kind of information could.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\mu$ cancels. In the vis-viva equation $\dot{\vecr}^{\,2}=GM(2/r-1/a)$, Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:visviva_speed}, the orbit's shape and motion depend on $m_s$ and $M_h$ only through $M$. $\mu$ drops out once $E$ and $L$ are turned into relations among $r$, $|\dot{\vecr}|$ and $h\equiv|\vec r\times\dot{\vecr}|$.
	\item There are three unknowns $(a,e,M)$ but only two equations relating $r$, $|\dot{\vecr}|$ and $h$. So one more independent piece of data is needed, and $e$ is the usual choice.
	\item At apocenter $\dot r=0$, so the velocity is purely tangential and $r_{\rm apo}=a(1+e)$ directly, without needing $h$.
	\item Test-particle limit. Since $m_s\ll M_h$, $M=m_s+M_h$ is almost exactly $M_h$.
	\item \textbf{Degeneracy, not just ignorance}. $\mu$ is the one combination of $m_s$ and $M_h$ that is \emph{not} their sum, and it cancels completely from both relations. So every orbital observable depends on the masses only through $M$, and any split of $M$ gives the identical orbit. This is different from Problem~9's mass \emph{ratio}, where two tracers gave one comparison. Here there is only one tracer, so only one orbit.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{General mass estimator}. Divide $E=\frac12\mu\dot{\vecr}^{\,2}-\dfrac{GM\mu}{r}=-\dfrac{GM\mu}{2a}$ by $\mu$ to recover vis-viva. Divide $L^2=GM\mu^2a(1-e^2)$ by $\mu^2$ and write $h\equiv L/\mu=|\vec r\times\dot{\vecr}|$, which needs no mass.
	\[
	\dot{\vecr}^{\,2}=GM\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right),\qquad h^2=GMa(1-e^2)\;\Longrightarrow\;a=\frac{h^2}{GM(1-e^2)}.
	\]
	Substituting $1/a$ into the first,
	\[
	\dot{\vecr}^{\,2}=\frac{2GM}{r}-\frac{G^2M^2(1-e^2)}{h^2}
	\;\Longrightarrow\;
	\boxed{\frac{G^2(1-e^2)}{h^2}M^2-\frac{2G}{r}M+\dot{\vecr}^{\,2}=0,}
	\]
	a quadratic in $M$, giving
	\[
	\boxed{M=\frac{h^2}{G(1-e^2)}\left[\frac1r\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{r^2}-\frac{(1-e^2)\dot{\vecr}^{\,2}}{h^2}}\right].}
	\]
	The product of the two roots is $h^2\dot{\vecr}^{\,2}/[G^2(1-e^2)]>0$ and their sum is positive, so both roots are positive and both are possible orbits. With $\lambda=h^2/(GM)$ the conic gives $e\cos v=\lambda/r-1$. The $+$ root, the larger $M$, has $r>\lambda$ and puts the satellite on the far half of its orbit. The $-$ root puts it on the near half. The sign of $\dot r$ does not decide between them, so one more fact about the phase is needed. At apocenter, part (b), it is the $+$ root.
	
	\item \textbf{Apocenter simplification}. Here $\dot r=0$, and $r=r_{\rm apo}=a(1+e)$, so $1/a=(1+e)/r_{\rm apo}$.
	\[
	v_{\rm apo}^2=GM\left(\frac{2}{r_{\rm apo}}-\frac{1+e}{r_{\rm apo}}\right)=\frac{GM(1-e)}{r_{\rm apo}}
	\;\Longrightarrow\;
	\boxed{M=\frac{v_{\rm apo}^2\,r_{\rm apo}}{G(1-e)}.}
	\]
	This matches the $+$ root of part (a) at apocenter, where $h=r_{\rm apo}v_{\rm apo}$ and the square root becomes $e/r_{\rm apo}$.
	
	\item \textbf{Numerical evaluation}. Use $r_{\rm apo}=7.714\times10^{21}\,\mathrm m$, $v_{\rm apo}=9.5\times10^4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $e=0.6$.
	\[
	M=\frac{(9.5\times10^4)^2(7.714\times10^{21})}{(6.674\times10^{-11})(1-0.6)}
	\;\Longrightarrow\;
	\boxed{M\simeq2.61\times10^{42}\,\mathrm{kg}\simeq1.31\times10^{12}\,M_\odot,}
	\]
	which is typical for a Milky-Way-sized halo. Dwarf-satellite masses ($10^4$--$10^8\,M_\odot$) are tiny next to this, at most about $10^{-4}$ of it, so $M\simeq M_h$ to four figures or better.
	
	\item \textbf{Why a third datum is unavoidable}. $r$ and $|\dot{\vecr}|$ give two independent numbers, but the orbit has three unknowns $(a,e,M)$, related by only two equations, vis-viva and $h^2=GMa(1-e^2)$. So one more independent piece of data is needed to close the system. It could be $e$, as used here, or $h$ itself, or the time elapsed since a known pericenter passage, found from Kepler's equation (Section~\ref{sec:kepler_equation}). This last option is the basis of the Local Group timing argument for the Milky Way--M31 mass.
	
	\item \textbf{The barycenter location is the missing parameter}. To specify $\vec x_s(t)$ and $\vec x_h(t)$ separately, we need the six orbital elements $(a,e,\iota,\Omega,\omega,t_0)$ of the \emph{relative} orbit $\vec r\equiv\vec x_s-\vec x_h$, the total mass $M$, and one more number. The six elements describe only the shape, orientation, and timing of $\vec r(t)$. They say nothing about how the total mass $M$ is split between the two bodies, and it is that split we are still missing. The extra number is the mass fraction $M_h/M$, which is the same as the barycenter's location on the line joining the two bodies. Parts (a)--(d) give only the relative orbit and $M$. The relative orbit cannot see $M_h/M$, and this is an exact symmetry, not just a practical limit. Newton's third law gives $\ddot{\vec x}_s=-GM_h\hat r/r^2$ and $\ddot{\vec x}_h=Gm_s\hat r/r^2$, so
	\[
	\ddot{\vec r}=\ddot{\vec x}_s-\ddot{\vec x}_h=-\frac{GM}{r^2}\hat r,
	\]
	which depends on $m_s$ and $M_h$ only through $M$. So $\vec r(t)$ stays exactly the same under $m_s\to m_s+\delta$, $M_h\to M_h-\delta$ at fixed $M$. No relative-orbit measurement, however precise, can pin down $M_h/M$.
	
	The individual position $\vec x_s=\vec X+(M_h/M)\vec r$ does depend on $M_h/M$. And $M_h/M=|\vec x_s-\vec X|/r$ is a purely geometric ratio, so it needs no dynamics to measure. This is the missing parameter. It is the same idea as Problem~1, where $m_1/m_2=a_2/a_1$ came from each star's distance to the visible barycenter.
	
	For a satellite and its halo, the problem is one of scale, not principle. Since $m_s/M_h\sim10^{-4}$--$10^{-8}$, the barycenter sits only that tiny fraction of $r$ from the halo's center, far too close to measure astrometrically (unlike Problem~1's pair of comparable masses). Without it, we estimate $m_s$ some other way, for example from its luminosity via a mass-to-light ratio, or from its own internal velocity dispersion via the virial theorem ($M \sim \sigma_v^2 R / G$, Section~\ref{sg:sec:virial}). Then $M_h=M-m_s$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 11: The Line-of-Sight Direction, in Matrices and Without Them}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Problem~9 projected an orbital velocity onto the line of sight $\hat R$, through the semi-amplitude $K$ and the systemic velocity $\gamma=\vec V_{\rm CM}\cdot\hat R$, without ever pinning down what $\hat R$ \emph{is} in terms of the orbital elements $(\iota,\Omega,\omega)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item Starting from the rotation relating the orbital frame $(\hatn,\hat y,\hat L)$ to the observer frame $(\hat\alpha,\hat\delta,\hat R)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:orbital_frame_rotation}, invert it to express $\hat R$ in terms of $\hatn,\hat y,\hat L$. Show that the result does not depend on $\Omega$ at all,
	\[
	\hat R = \sin\iota\,\hat y+\cos\iota\,\hat L.
	\]
	\item Rederive the same identity without any matrix algebra. Use only two facts. First, the node line $\hatn$ is, by construction, the intersection of the orbital plane and the sky plane. Second, the inclination is the angle between the two planes' normals, $\hat L$ and $\hat R$.
	\item A body follows a Keplerian orbit of elements $(a,e,\iota,\omega)$ in the plane spanned by $\hatn,\hat y$, at true anomaly $v$ (so its polar angle from $\hatn$ is $\phi=\omega+v$). Using $\hat R=\sin\iota\,\hat y+\cos\iota\,\hat L$ together with the fact that the velocity lies entirely in the orbital plane, show that the line-of-sight (radial) velocity $v_r=\dot{\vecr}\cdot\hat R$ takes the form
	\[
	v_r = \gamma + K\big[\cos(\omega+v)+e\cos\omega\big],
	\]
	and identify $K$ in terms of $G$, $M$, $a$, $e$, $\iota$.
	\item Using Kepler's third law, show that this $K$ is the semi-amplitude
	\[
	K=\frac{2\pi a\sin\iota}{P\sqrt{1-e^2}}
	\]
	quoted without proof in Problem~9.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item A rotation matrix is orthogonal, so $\mathbf R^{-1}=\mathbf R^T$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:coords}, Eq.~\eqref{co:eq:orthogonal}). Undoing it costs no more than transposing and reversing the product order.
	\item Simple geometric facts about an orthonormal triad, such as which vectors are perpendicular, or what a named angle is the cosine of, can rebuild a rotation result with less algebra than the matrix itself.
	\item Angular momentum conservation confines $\vec r$ and $\dot{\vecr}$ to the orbital plane, so $\vec r\cdot\hat L=\dot{\vecr}\cdot\hat L=0$. This drops $\hat L$ from $v_r=\dot{\vecr}\cdot\hat R$ in part (c).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Matrix version}. Both $3\times3$ factors in Eq.~\eqref{eq:orbital_frame_rotation} are rotation matrices, so they are orthogonal, $\mathbf R^{-1}=\mathbf R^T$. Inverting the product reverses the order and transposes each factor.
	\begin{align*}
	\begin{pmatrix}\hat\alpha\\\hat\delta\\\hat R\end{pmatrix}
	&=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\Omega&\sin\Omega&0\\-\sin\Omega&\cos\Omega&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}^{\!T}
	\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&\cos\iota&\sin\iota\\0&-\sin\iota&\cos\iota\end{pmatrix}^{\!T}
	\begin{pmatrix}\hatn\\\hat y\\\hat L\end{pmatrix}\\[4pt]
	&=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\Omega&-\sin\Omega&0\\\sin\Omega&\cos\Omega&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&\cos\iota&-\sin\iota\\0&\sin\iota&\cos\iota\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{pmatrix}\hatn\\\hat y\\\hat L\end{pmatrix}.
	\end{align*}
	The third row of the product is the third row of the first matrix, $(0,0,1)$, times the second matrix. That picks out the second matrix's third row, $(0,\sin\iota,\cos\iota)$, so
	\[
	\hat R = \underbrace{(0)}_{\hatn\text{-coefficient}}\hatn+\sin\iota\,\hat y+\cos\iota\,\hat L
	\;\Longrightarrow\;
	\boxed{\hat R=\sin\iota\,\hat y+\cos\iota\,\hat L,}
	\]
	since $\Omega$ enters only the first two rows of the first matrix, which never touch the row producing $\hat R$.
	
	\item \textbf{Geometric version}. $\hatn$ is the intersection of the orbital and sky planes. So it is perpendicular to both planes' normals, $\hatn\cdot\hat L=0$ and $\hatn\cdot\hat R=0$. Since $\{\hatn,\hat y,\hat L\}$ is orthonormal, any unit vector with zero $\hatn$-component is a combination of $\hat y$ and $\hat L$ alone.
	\[
	\hat R = (\hat R\cdot\hat y)\,\hat y+(\hat R\cdot\hat L)\,\hat L.
	\]
	By definition, $\iota$ is the angle between the planes' normals, so $\hat R\cdot\hat L=\cos\iota$. Since $\hat R\cdot\hatn=0$ and $\hat R$ is a unit vector, $(\hat R\cdot\hat y)^2=\sin^2\iota$. The orientation convention, that $\hat y$ leads $\hatn$ by $90^\circ$ toward $\hat R$, fixes the sign.
	\[
	\boxed{\hat R=\sin\iota\,\hat y+\cos\iota\,\hat L,}
	\]
	the same as part (a), with no matrix multiplied.
	
	\item \textbf{Radial velocity}. Angular momentum conservation confines the orbit to the plane spanned by $\hatn,\hat y$, so $\dot{\vecr}\cdot\hat L=0$, and
	\[
	v_r=\dot{\vecr}\cdot\hat R = \sin\iota\,(\dot{\vecr}\cdot\hat y).
	\]
	With $\vec r=r\cos\phi\,\hatn+r\sin\phi\,\hat y$ and the planar-polar velocity $\dot{\vecr}=\dot r\,\rhat+r\dot\phi\,\phihat$,
	\[
	\dot{\vecr}\cdot\hat y = \dot r\sin\phi+r\dot\phi\cos\phi .
	\]
	Substitute $\dot r=\dfrac{Le\sin v}{\mu\lambda}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:rdot_true_anomaly}) and $r\dot\phi=\dfrac{L}{\mu r}=\dfrac{L(1+e\cos v)}{\mu\lambda}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta_dot_keplerian}), with $\phi=\omega+v$.
	\[
	\dot{\vecr}\cdot\hat y = \frac{L}{\mu\lambda}\Big[e\sin v\sin(\omega+v)+(1+e\cos v)\cos(\omega+v)\Big]
	=\frac{L}{\mu\lambda}\Big[\cos(\omega+v)+e\cos\omega\Big],
	\]
	using $\sin v\sin(\omega+v)+\cos v\cos(\omega+v)=\cos\omega$. Since $L=\mu\sqrt{GM\lambda}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:L_of_ae}) and $\lambda=a(1-e^2)$, $L/(\mu\lambda)=\sqrt{GM/[a(1-e^2)]}$. Add back $\gamma$, Problem~9's line-of-sight velocity of the centre of mass, as the baseline.
	\[
	v_r = \sin\iota\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a(1-e^2)}}\Big[\cos(\omega+v)+e\cos\omega\Big] + \gamma.
	\]
	Matching to the stated form,
	\[
	\boxed{K=\sin\iota\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a(1-e^2)}}.}
	\]
	
	\item \textbf{Consistency with Problem~9}. Kepler's third law gives $GM=4\pi^2a^3/P^2$, so
	\[
	K=\sin\iota\sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2a^3/P^2}{a(1-e^2)}}=\sin\iota\sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2a^2}{P^2(1-e^2)}}
	\;\Longrightarrow\;
	\boxed{K=\frac{2\pi a\sin\iota}{P\sqrt{1-e^2}},}
	\]
	the radial-velocity semi-amplitude of a spectroscopic binary, Problem~9's $K$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 12: The Gravitational Slingshot}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: A spacecraft flies by Jupiter. Jupiter's own speed around the Sun is $V_p\simeq13.1\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Relative to Jupiter, the spacecraft moves on a hyperbolic orbit with $e=1.2$ and $v_\infty=10\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:vinf}). Find the change in the spacecraft's speed around the Sun, given that its speed relative to Jupiter cannot change.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\resizebox{0.68\textwidth}{!}{%
	\includegraphics{fig_ch6_slingshot_classic.pdf}%
	}
	\caption{The gravitational slingshot. Left, Jupiter's frame, with the incoming and outgoing branches of the hyperbola. The dashed lines are the asymptotes, crossing at the hyperbola's center, with the angle $\delta$ between them and the angle $v_{\rm asy}$ from the periapsis direction. $\vec w_{\rm in}$ and $\vec w_{\rm out}$ are the velocities along the asymptotes. Right, $\vec w_{\rm in}$ and $\vec w_{\rm out}$ added to Jupiter's heliocentric velocity $\vec V_p$.}
	\label{fig:slingshot_classic}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Solution}. In the solar frame, we drop the Jupiter--Sun terms that do not involve $\vec x_{\rm sc}$, and write the Lagrangian of the spacecraft,
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal L = \frac12 m_{\rm sc}\dot{\vec x}_{\rm sc}^2 + \frac{GM_\odot m_{\rm sc}}{|\vec x_{\rm sc}|} + \frac{Gm_{\rm sc}M_J}{|\vec x_{\rm sc}-\vec X_J(t)|}.
\end{equation}
Let $\vec r\equiv\vec x_{\rm sc}-\vec X_J(t)$. Over the brief encounter, $\vec X_J(t)\approx\vec X_{J,0}+\vec V_pt$ and $|\vec x_{\rm sc}|\approx X_J(t)$, dropping the small term $O(r/X_J)$. Also $\dot{\vec x}_{\rm sc}=\vec V_p+\dot{\vec r}$, so
\begin{align}
	\mathcal L &\approx \underbrace{\tfrac12m_{\rm sc}V_p^2+\dfrac{GM_\odot m_{\rm sc}}{X_J(t)}}_{g(t)} + \underbrace{m_{\rm sc}\vec V_p\cdot\dot{\vec r}}_{\frac{d}{dt}(m_{\rm sc}\vec V_p\cdot\vec r)} + \frac12m_{\rm sc}\dot{\vec r}^{\,2}+\frac{Gm_{\rm sc}M_J}{r}\\
	&\Longrightarrow\quad \mathcal L(\vec r,\dot{\vec r}) = \frac12m_{\rm sc}\dot{\vec r}^{\,2}+\frac{Gm_{\rm sc}M_J}{r},
	\label{eq:slingshot_effective_L}
\end{align}
since a function of time alone and a total time derivative do not change the equations of motion. This $\mathcal L$ has no explicit $t$, so by Noether's theorem the energy is conserved,
\begin{align}
	E &= \dot{\vec r}\cdot\pdv{\mathcal L}{\dot{\vec r}}-\mathcal L = \frac12m_{\rm sc}\dot{\vec r}^{\,2}-\frac{Gm_{\rm sc}M_J}{r}=\text{const}\\
	&\Longrightarrow\quad v_{\infty,\rm in}=v_{\infty,\rm out}\equiv v_\infty=\sqrt{-GM_J/a},
	\label{eq:slingshot_speed_conserved}
\end{align}
the Jupiter-relative speed at $r\to\infty$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:vinf}. The solar-frame velocity is $\vec V_p+\vec w$, where $\vec w\equiv\dot{\vec r}$ far from Jupiter. Its size $|\vec w|=v_\infty$ is fixed, but its direction is free (Figure~\ref{fig:slingshot_classic}).

Put the $x$-axis along the periapsis direction. The orbit is $r(v)=\lambda/(1+e\cos v)$, and $r\to\infty$ at $v=\pm v_{\rm asy}$, where
\begin{equation}
	\cos v_{\rm asy}=-\frac1e,
\end{equation}
as in Section~\ref{tb:sec:hyperbolic}. So $\hat w_{\rm out}$ sits at the angle $v_{\rm asy}$ from the axis. At periapsis $\dot r=0$, so the velocity is purely tangential, perpendicular to the axis. The trajectory is a mirror image about the periapsis, so the total turn $\delta$ splits evenly on each side. This puts $\hat w_{\rm out}$ at $\delta/2$ past that perpendicular.
\begin{align}
	v_{\rm asy} &= \frac\pi2+\frac\delta2
	\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
	\sin\frac\delta2 = \sin\!\left(v_{\rm asy}-\frac\pi2\right) = -\cos v_{\rm asy} = \frac1e,
	\label{eq:slingshot_deflection}
\end{align}
the deflection angle $\delta=\pi-2\theta_\infty$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:scattering}. For $e=1.2$ this gives $\delta\simeq112.9^\circ$. As $e\to1^+$, $\delta\to\pi$, and as $e\to\infty$, $\delta\to0$. By the same symmetry, $\hat w_{\rm in}$ sits at $v_{\rm asy}-\delta=\pi/2-\delta/2$ from the axis.

Take $\vec V_p=-V_p\hat x$, so Jupiter moves parallel to the periapsis direction, to the left. $\hat w_{\rm in}$ sits at $\pi-v_{\rm asy}$ and $\hat w_{\rm out}$ at $v_{\rm asy}$ from the axis, so their angles with $\vec V_p$ are $v_{\rm asy}$ and $\pi-v_{\rm asy}$. By the law of cosines,
\begin{align}
	v_{\rm in}^2 &= V_p^2+v_\infty^2+2V_pv_\infty\cos v_{\rm asy} = V_p^2+v_\infty^2-\frac{2V_pv_\infty}{e}\\
	&= 13.1^2+10^2-\frac{2(13.1)(10)}{1.2}
	\;\Longrightarrow\; v_{\rm in}\simeq7.3\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}},\\
	v_{\rm out}^2 &= V_p^2+v_\infty^2+2V_pv_\infty\cos(\pi-v_{\rm asy}) = V_p^2+v_\infty^2+\frac{2V_pv_\infty}{e}\\
	&= 13.1^2+10^2+\frac{2(13.1)(10)}{1.2}
	\;\Longrightarrow\; v_{\rm out}\simeq22.1\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.
\end{align}
The spacecraft's speed around the Sun grows by $\boxed{v_{\rm out}-v_{\rm in}\simeq14.8\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}}$.

By momentum conservation, $m_{\rm sc}\Delta\vec v_{\rm sc}=-M_J\Delta\vec V_p$, so for a spacecraft of about a tonne
\begin{equation}
	\Delta V_p=\frac{m_{\rm sc}}{M_J}\,\Delta v_{\rm sc}<10^{-20}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}},
\end{equation}
undetectable on Jupiter.
