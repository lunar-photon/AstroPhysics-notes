\chapter{Radiation from Hot and Fast Particles}
\label{ch:fastparticles}

\input{results/ch15/synchrotron.tex}

Hot, opaque stars shine nearly as blackbodies (Chapter~\ref{ch:radiation}). Many other sources do not. Thin gas between the galaxies of a cluster glows in X-rays. Radio galaxies shine across many decades of frequency with no single temperature that could explain their spectra. A quasar can outshine its host galaxy from a region only a few light-days across.

Charged particles make this light when ions, magnetic fields, or other particles accelerate them. We start with the power radiated by an accelerated charge and use the six principles to build the spectra. For particles moving near the speed of light, we also need the Lorentz transformation in matrix form.

\section{An Accelerated Charge Radiates}
\label{fp:sec:larmor}

In Chapter~\ref{ch:ism}, a passing wave shook a slow electron. Its radiation gave the Thomson cross-section of Eq.~\eqref{eq:thomson_cross_section}. Ions and magnetic fields can accelerate electrons much more strongly, and electrons in jets can move close to $c$. Their radiation depends on both the acceleration and the motion of the source toward the observer.

Shake the end of a long rope and a pulse runs away from your hand. A sharper jerk sends a stronger pulse. The charge's electric field behaves in the same way when its motion changes. An electron passing a proton gets one sideways kick. In a magnetic field it turns continuously. In each case, its changing field carries energy away (P1). The radiated power rises with the square of the acceleration. We will compare that loss with the energy of the motion before treating radiation as a small correction (P6).

Consider a classical electron circling a proton at the Bohr radius, $5.29\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm m$. Its acceleration is $9.0\times10^{22}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, so it would radiate $4.7\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm W$. At that rate, its $13.6\,\mathrm{eV}$ of energy would last $4.7\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm s$. One orbit takes only $1.5\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm s$, so the loss per orbit is $1.6\times10^{-6}$ of its energy. A real hydrogen atom is stable because of quantum mechanics. The classical comparison shows why radiation can often be calculated after finding the particle's motion. The escaping field is strongest at right angles to the acceleration and vanishes along it. The same rule describes radio antennas, X-ray tubes, laboratory synchrotrons, and the scattering in Chapter~\ref{ch:ism}.

\paragraph{The Larmor formula.} A charge $e$ moving slowly, $v\ll c$, with acceleration $\dot{\vec v}$ radiates the power
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal P=\frac{e^2\,|\dot{\vec v}|^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3},\qquad
	\dv{\mathcal P}{\Omega}=\frac{e^2\,|\dot{\vec v}|^2}{16\pi^2\varepsilon_0c^3}\,\sin^2\Theta ,
	\label{fp:eq:larmor}
\end{equation}
Here $\Theta$ is the angle between the acceleration and the direction of emission. These results come from electromagnetism. Integrating the angular pattern gives the total power, since $\int\sin^2\Theta\,d\Omega=8\pi/3$. Equal power goes in opposite directions, so the radiation carries energy but no net momentum in this frame.

\paragraph{How fast a charge loses its energy.} Take an electron of mass $m_e$ oscillating as $x=x_0\cos\omega t$. We compare the energy it radiates in one radian of motion with the energy it has (P6). Its acceleration is $\dot v=-\omega^2x$, its energy is $\tfrac12m_e\omega^2x_0^2$, and a time $1/\omega$ lets it radiate $\avg{\mathcal P}/\omega$.
\begin{align}
	\frac{\text{energy radiated per radian}}{\text{energy of the motion}}
	&\eqby{1} \frac{e^2\,\omega^4x_0^2/(12\pi\varepsilon_0c^3)}{\omega\cdot\tfrac12m_e\omega^2x_0^2} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \omega\,\frac{e^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0m_ec^3}
	\eqby{3} \omega\,t_e .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:larmor} with $|\dot v|^2=\omega^4x_0^2\cos^2\omega t$, whose average over a cycle is $\omega^4x_0^2/2$.
	\item The factors $x_0^2$ and $\omega^3$ cancel.
	\item Define the \textbf{radiation time} $t_e$ below.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{t_e=\frac{e^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0m_ec^3}=\frac23\,\frac{r_e}{c}=6.27\times10^{-24}\,\mathrm s .}
	\label{fp:eq:tau_e}
\end{equation}
Here $r_e$ is the classical electron radius of Eq.~\eqref{eq:thomson_cross_section}. Light takes only a little longer than $t_e$ to cross it. The electron loses a fraction $\omega t_e$ of its energy per radian, and $\omega t_e\ll1$ below about $10^{23}$ turns per second. We can first find the motion as though the charge did not radiate, then use Larmor's formula to calculate its light.

\paragraph{Fast charges and the boost matrix.} Let the charge move along $x$ at speed $\beta c$ in the observer's frame, which we call the lab. The charge's own frame is primed. To turn a position, time, energy, or momentum from the charge's frame into lab quantities, use the boost
\begin{equation}
	\begin{pmatrix}ct\\x\\y\end{pmatrix}=\Lambda(\beta)\begin{pmatrix}ct'\\x'\\y'\end{pmatrix},\qquad
	\Lambda(\beta)=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_L&\gamma_L\beta&0\\ \gamma_L\beta&\gamma_L&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
	\gamma_L=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}} ,
	\label{fp:eq:boost}
\end{equation}
The $z$ component is unchanged and omitted. The same matrix transforms four-momentum, with components $E/c$, momentum along $x$, and momentum along $y$. To go from the lab to the charge's frame, use its inverse $\Lambda(-\beta)$. The subscript on the \textbf{Lorentz factor} $\gamma_L$ distinguishes it from the adiabatic exponent $\gamma$.

\paragraph{The power is the same in both frames.} At one instant, the charge is at rest in its own frame, where Larmor's formula applies. During $dt'$ it radiates energy $dE'$ but no net momentum, by the symmetry of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:larmor}. The radiation is emitted at the charge, $x'=y'=0$. Boost the radiated energy and the time interval to the lab,
\begin{align}
	\Lambda(\beta)\begin{pmatrix}dE'/c\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}
	&\eqby{1} \begin{pmatrix}\gamma_L\,dE'/c\\ \gamma_L\beta\,dE'/c\\0\end{pmatrix},
	&
	\Lambda(\beta)\begin{pmatrix}c\,dt'\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}
	&\eqby{1} \begin{pmatrix}\gamma_L\,c\,dt'\\ \gamma_L\beta\,c\,dt'\\0\end{pmatrix}, \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad dE&=\gamma_L\,dE', & dt&=\gamma_L\,dt' .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The matrix of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:boost} times a vector with only its first entry non-zero is that entry times the first column.
\end{explain}
The factors $\gamma_L$ cancel in the ratio,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\mathcal P=\dv{E}{t}=\dv{E'}{t'}=\frac{e^2\,|\dot{\vec v}'|^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3},}
	\label{fp:eq:power_invariant}
\end{equation}
Here $\dot{\vec v}'$ is the acceleration in the charge's frame. We can find the power of a fast charge by calculating that acceleration and applying Larmor's formula there. An observer may receive the light in a shorter burst because the source moves toward them. That arrival-time effect sets the frequencies of synchrotron light in Section~\ref{fp:sec:sync}.

\paragraph{Beaming.} A photon of frequency $\nu'$ emitted in the charge's frame at angle $\theta'$ to the direction of motion has four-momentum $(h\nu'/c)(1,\cos\theta',\sin\theta')$. In the lab it has frequency $\nu$ and angle $\theta$,
\begin{align}
	\frac{h\nu}{c}\begin{pmatrix}1\\ \cos\theta\\ \sin\theta\end{pmatrix}
	&\eqby{1} \Lambda(\beta)\,\frac{h\nu'}{c}\begin{pmatrix}1\\ \cos\theta'\\ \sin\theta'\end{pmatrix}
	\eqby{2} \frac{h\nu'}{c}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_L(1+\beta\cos\theta')\\ \gamma_L(\cos\theta'+\beta)\\ \sin\theta'\end{pmatrix} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \tan\theta
	&\eqby{3} \frac{\sin\theta'}{\gamma_L(\cos\theta'+\beta)},\qquad
	\nu \eqby{4} \gamma_L(1+\beta\cos\theta')\,\nu' .
	\label{fp:eq:beaming}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The boost \eqref{fp:eq:boost} applied to the photon's four-momentum.
	\item The matrix product, row by row.
	\item The third row divided by the second.
	\item The first row.
\end{explain}
A photon emitted sideways in the charge's frame, $\theta'=\pi/2$, appears at $\tan\theta=1/(\gamma_L\beta)$ in the lab. The entire forward hemisphere of emission fits inside this angle,
\[
\boxed{\theta\lesssim\frac{1}{\gamma_L}\qquad(\gamma_L\gg1).}
\]
For $\gamma_L=10$, that cone has a half-angle of about $6^\circ$ (Figure~\ref{fp:fig:beaming}). The forward photons are also shifted to higher frequencies. Their energy rises by $\gamma_L(1+\beta\cos\theta')$, compared with the all-direction average of $\gamma_L$. The forward half therefore carries the fraction $\tfrac12+\tfrac\beta4$ of the radiated energy, $0.74$ at $\gamma_L=3$ and nearly $\tfrac34$ at high speed. At $\theta'=0$, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:beaming} gives $\gamma_L(1+\beta)\nu'=\sqrt{(1+\beta)/(1-\beta)}\,\nu'$, the Doppler shift of an approaching source (Eq.~\eqref{eq:doppler_relativistic}).

\section{Bremsstrahlung}
\label{fp:sec:brems}

The gas between the galaxies of a cluster is hot, about $7\times10^7\,\mathrm K$, because it lies deep in the cluster's gravitational well (Problem~\ref{sg:prob:cluster}). It is fully ionised, so it has no bound electrons to make spectral lines. It is also thin enough for light to escape without reaching blackbody equilibrium (Eq.~\eqref{rad:eq:slab}). What makes its X-rays?

Think of a car rounding a bend. Even if its speed stays steady, it accelerates sideways because its direction changes. An electron passing an ion also turns. The ion's electric field pulls it inward for a short time, and the accelerating electron sends out a pulse of light (Section~\ref{fp:sec:larmor}). A closer pass gives a stronger, shorter pulse. The combined light of many such encounters is \textbf{bremsstrahlung}, or braking radiation. We find the spectrum of one pulse, count passes at different distances (P6), then average over electron speeds (P3).

For the cluster gas, $k_BT=6.0\,\mathrm{keV}$, corresponding to $1.5\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Its spectrum begins to fall steeply near this X-ray energy. Electrons in the fast tail of the Maxwell distribution can still make higher-energy photons, but each photon must draw its energy from the electron that made it (P1). The same process makes X-rays in an X-ray tube and solar flares, and radio light in ionised gas around hot stars. In reverse, a free electron can absorb a photon while passing an ion. That process contributes to the opacity of stellar interiors (P4).

The ion is much heavier than the passing electron, so we treat it as fixed. The ion's electric field pulls the electron toward it and bends its path (Figure~\ref{fp:fig:brems_dipole}). The pair's electric dipole moment changes as the electron moves. With the ion fixed, $\boldsymbol p=-e\boldsymbol r_e+\text{constant}$, so $\ddot{\boldsymbol p}=-e\boldsymbol a_e$. The acceleration produces the dipole radiation of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:larmor}, strongest across the direction of the kick and absent along it.

	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch15_brems_dipole.pdf}
	\caption{A free electron passes a heavy ion and curves toward it (left). The lobes show the angular pattern of its radiation at low speed (right).}
	\label{fp:fig:brems_dipole}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{A gas of free charges.} We can count separate electron--ion encounters only if an electron feels one ion more strongly than the surrounding charges during a close pass. Put an ion of charge $Z_ie$ at the origin, in gas of electron density $n_e$ and temperature $T$. Its electric potential $\phi$ draws in electrons. Their density rises by the Boltzmann factor $e^{e\phi/k_BT}$ (P3). Away from the ion, Poisson's equation gives
\begin{align}
	\nabla^2\phi
	&\eqby{1} -\frac{e}{\varepsilon_0}\Big(Z_in_i-n_e\,e^{e\phi/k_BT}\Big) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \frac{n_ee^2}{\varepsilon_0k_BT}\,\phi
	\eqby{3} \frac{\phi}{\lambda_D^2} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \phi
	&\eqby{4} \frac{Z_ie}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\,e^{-r/\lambda_D} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The charge density is that of the ions, $Z_ien_i$, minus that of the electrons. The ions are heavy and stay spread evenly on the scale considered.
	\item Far from the ion $e\phi\ll k_BT$, so $e^{e\phi/k_BT}\approx1+e\phi/k_BT$. The gas is neutral on average, $Z_in_i=n_e$.
	\item Define the \textbf{Debye length} $\lambda_D$ below.
	\item The spherically symmetric solution that matches the bare ion's potential at small $r$. Substituting it back gives $\nabla^2\phi=\phi/\lambda_D^2$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\lambda_D=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_0k_BT}{n_ee^2}}=\frac{\sqrt{k_BT/m_e}}{2\pi\nu_p} .}
	\label{fp:eq:debye}
\end{equation}
The second form uses the plasma frequency of Eq.~\eqref{re:eq:plasma_frequency}. The \textbf{Debye length} is roughly the distance a thermal electron travels during one plasma oscillation. Beyond this distance, the cloud of electrons around an ion screens its field. In cluster gas, $n_e=10^3\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ and $T=7\times10^7\,\mathrm K$ give $\lambda_D=18\,\mathrm{km}$, with $2.5\times10^{16}$ electrons inside a sphere of that radius. Gas around a hot star, $n_e=10^8\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ and $T=10^4\,\mathrm K$, gives $\lambda_D=0.7\,\mathrm m$ and $1.4\times10^8$ electrons (P6). Each screening cloud thus contains many electrons. Its average effect is smooth, while a passing electron feels the nearest ion strongly when it gets much closer than $\lambda_D$. We can count such encounters one at a time. The gas is a \textbf{plasma}, and it blocks radiation below the plasma frequency $\nu_p$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:resolution}).

\paragraph{One encounter.} An electron approaches an ion of charge $Z_ie$ at speed $v$ and impact parameter $b$. If the deflection is small, its nearly straight path is enough to calculate the force. Set $t=0$ at closest approach. The sideways force is $Z_ie^2b/[4\pi\varepsilon_0(b^2+v^2t^2)^{3/2}]$. Integrating it over the passage gives the sideways change in speed,
\begin{align}
	\Delta v
	&\eqby{1} \frac{Z_ie^2b}{4\pi\varepsilon_0m_e}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{(b^2+v^2t^2)^{3/2}}
	\eqby{2} \frac{2Z_ie^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0m_e\,bv} .
	\label{fp:eq:kick}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Newton's law across the path, integrated over the passage.
	\item $\int_{-\infty}^\infty dt/(b^2+v^2t^2)^{3/2}=2/(b^2v)$.
\end{explain}
The kick tells us the total change in velocity, but the spectrum also depends on how long the kick lasts. We describe the acceleration at each angular frequency $\omega$ by its Fourier transform $\tilde{\dot v}(\omega)=\int\dot v\,e^{i\omega t}dt$. For a real signal, Parseval's theorem gives $\int\dot v^2dt=\tfrac1\pi\int_0^\infty|\tilde{\dot v}|^2d\omega$. Applying it to Larmor's power, the pulse energy $W$ becomes
\begin{align}
	W
	&\eqby{1} \frac{e^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\dot v^2\,dt
	\eqby{2} \int_0^\infty\frac{e^2\,|\tilde{\dot v}(\omega)|^2}{6\pi^2\varepsilon_0c^3}\,d\omega \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \dv{W}{\omega}
	&\eqby{3} \frac{e^2\,(\Delta v)^2}{6\pi^2\varepsilon_0c^3}\Big[uK_1(u)\Big]^2,\qquad u=\frac{\omega b}{v} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Larmor's power \eqref{fp:eq:larmor} integrated over the passage.
	\item Parseval's theorem.
	\item The transform of the force law above is $\tilde{\dot v}=\Delta v\,uK_1(u)$, with $K_1$ a modified Bessel function. The component of the acceleration along the path changes sign during the passage and adds much less at low frequency, so it is dropped.
\end{explain}
The factor $uK_1(u)$ is close to $1$ when $u\ll1$ and falls rapidly, as $e^{-u}$, when $u\gg1$. We can therefore keep the low-frequency part and cut the spectrum off near $\omega=v/b$,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\dv{W}{\omega}\approx\frac{e^2}{6\pi^2\varepsilon_0c^3}\left(\frac{2Z_ie^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0m_e\,bv}\right)^2\quad\text{for }\omega\lesssim\frac vb,\qquad\approx0\ \text{above}.}
	\label{fp:eq:one_encounter}
\end{equation}
The electron spends about $b/v$ near the ion. A shorter encounter makes a shorter pulse and reaches higher frequencies (P6). Below the cutoff, each encounter contributes nearly the same energy per unit frequency.

\paragraph{Counting encounters.} A single pulse is faint. To get the light from a gas, count how often electrons of speed $v$ pass ions at each impact parameter. In a unit volume, encounters between $b$ and $b+db$ occur at the rate $n_en_iv\,2\pi b\,db$. At frequency $\omega$, only encounters with $b<v/\omega$ contribute appreciably, by Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:one_encounter}.
\begin{align}
	\frac{dW}{dt\,dV\,d\omega}
	&\eqby{1} n_en_iv\int_{b_{\min}}^{b_{\max}}2\pi b\,\dv{W}{\omega}\,db \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} n_en_iv\,2\pi\,\frac{e^2}{6\pi^2\varepsilon_0c^3}\left(\frac{2Z_ie^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0m_ev}\right)^2\int_{b_{\min}}^{b_{\max}}\frac{db}{b} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \frac{16}{3}\,\frac{Z_i^2e^6}{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^3c^3m_e^2}\,\frac{n_en_i}{v}\,\ln\frac{b_{\max}}{b_{\min}} .
	\label{fp:eq:brems_v}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Rate of encounters times the energy each gives per unit frequency.
	\item Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:one_encounter}. Its $b^{-2}$ against the $b$ of the ring leaves $db/b$.
	\item Collect constants. $2\pi\times4/(6\pi^2)=4/(3\pi)$, and $e^2/\varepsilon_0=4\pi e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$.
\end{explain}
The upper limit is $b_{\max}=v/\omega$. Beyond it, the encounter is too slow to radiate much at frequency $\omega$. The lower limit is the larger of two distances. At $Z_ie^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0m_ev^2)$ the deflection ceases to be small, and below the quantum wavelength $\hbar/(m_ev)$ the classical path ceases to be a good description. Because the result depends on their ratio only through a logarithm, the exact limits have a modest effect. A full quantum calculation replaces $\ln(b_{\max}/b_{\min})$ by $(\pi/\sqrt3)\,\bar g$. The \textbf{Gaunt factor} $\bar g$ is of order one. The $1/v$ dependence remains: a slower electron spends longer near the ion and radiates more.

\paragraph{Averaging over electron speeds.} Electrons in gas at temperature $T$ have the Maxwell distribution of Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:ideal_gas}. Their speed intervals carry the weight $4\pi v^2e^{-m_ev^2/2k_BT}dv$ (P3). A photon of energy $h\nu$ requires an electron with at least that much kinetic energy, so $\tfrac12m_ev^2\ge h\nu$ (P1). We therefore average the $1/v$ in Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:brems_v} over electrons above this threshold,
\begin{align}
	\avg{\frac1v}
	&\eqby{1} \frac{\int_{v_{\min}}^\infty v\,e^{-m_ev^2/2k_BT}\,dv}{\int_0^\infty v^2\,e^{-m_ev^2/2k_BT}\,dv}
	\eqby{2} \frac{(k_BT/m_e)\,e^{-h\nu/k_BT}}{\sqrt{\pi/2}\,(k_BT/m_e)^{3/2}}
	\eqby{3} \sqrt{\frac{2m_e}{\pi k_BT}}\;e^{-h\nu/k_BT} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The weight $v^2$ of the Maxwell distribution times $1/v$, normalised. The lower limit is $\tfrac12m_ev_{\min}^2=h\nu$.
	\item The two Gaussian integrals, with $m_ev_{\min}^2/2k_BT=h\nu/k_BT$.
	\item Simplify.
\end{explain}
Substitute this average into Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:brems_v} and change from angular frequency to frequency with $d\omega=2\pi\,d\nu$. We write the power per unit volume and frequency as $4\pi j_\nu$, where $j_\nu$ is the emissivity per steradian of Chapter~\ref{ch:radiation}.
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{4\pi j_\nu=\frac{32\pi^2}{3\sqrt3}\,\frac{Z_i^2e^6}{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^3c^3m_e^2}\sqrt{\frac{2m_e}{\pi k_BT}}\;\bar g\,n_en_i\,e^{-h\nu/k_BT}
	=6.8\times10^{-51}\,Z_i^2\,\bar g\,\frac{n_en_i}{\sqrt T}\,e^{-h\nu/k_BT},}
	\label{fp:eq:brems_jnu}
\end{equation}
The units are $\mathrm{W\,m^{-3}\,Hz^{-1}}$ when $n_e,n_i$ are in $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ and $T$ is in kelvin. The spectrum is nearly flat below $h\nu\approx k_BT$, apart from the slow change of $\bar g$. Above that energy, it falls exponentially because fast electrons are rare (Figure~\ref{fp:fig:brems}). The bend thus measures the gas temperature. At low frequencies, hotter gas is fainter at fixed density, as $T^{-1/2}$, because faster electrons spend less time near each ion.

\paragraph{Cooling.} The spectrum tells us how much energy the gas loses. Integrating over all frequencies, with $\int_0^\infty e^{-h\nu/k_BT}d\nu=k_BT/h$, gives the radiated power per unit volume,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{4\pi\int_0^\infty j_\nu\,d\nu=6.8\times10^{-51}\,\frac{k_B}{h}\,Z_i^2\,\bar g\,n_en_i\sqrt T=1.4\times10^{-40}\,Z_i^2\,\bar g\,n_en_i\sqrt T\ \ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}} .}
	\label{fp:eq:brems_total}
\end{equation}
The power rises with $n_en_i$ because an electron must pass an ion to radiate. It also rises as $\sqrt T$ after we add all frequencies. A hydrogen plasma with $n_e=n_i=n$ holds thermal energy $\tfrac32(n_e+n_i)k_BT=3nk_BT$ per unit volume. Dividing that energy by the radiated power gives its cooling time,
\[
t_{\rm cool}=\frac{3nk_BT}{1.4\times10^{-40}\,\bar g\,n^2\sqrt T}=\frac{3k_B\sqrt T}{1.4\times10^{-40}\,\bar g\,n} .
\]
For cluster gas with $n=10^3\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ and $T=7\times10^7\,\mathrm K$, this is $2.4\times10^{18}\,\mathrm s$, or $8\times10^{10}$ years when $\bar g=1$. That exceeds the age of the universe (Chapter~\ref{ch:universe}). Most cluster gas therefore remains hot and emits X-rays long after it was heated (P6). A sphere of this gas $1\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ in radius radiates $1.5\times10^{38}\,\mathrm W$, comparable to the X-ray luminosity of a rich cluster. Dense cluster centres cool faster, and in some of them the cooling time is shorter than the age of the universe.

\paragraph{Free-free absorption and Kramers opacity.} A free electron can also absorb a photon while passing an ion. In thermal equilibrium, Kirchhoff's law relates this reverse process to the emission we have just found (P4). Equation~\eqref{rad:eq:kirchhoff} gives $\kappa_\nu\rho=j_\nu/B_\nu$.
\begin{align}
	\kappa_\nu\rho
	&\eqby{1} \frac{6.8\times10^{-51}}{4\pi}\,Z_i^2\bar g\,\frac{n_en_i}{\sqrt T}\,e^{-h\nu/k_BT}\;\frac{c^2\big(e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1\big)}{2h\nu^3} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \frac{6.8\times10^{-51}\,c^2}{8\pi h}\,Z_i^2\bar g\,\frac{n_en_i}{\sqrt T}\,\frac{1-e^{-h\nu/k_BT}}{\nu^3} .
	\label{fp:eq:ff_absorption}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:brems_jnu} divided by $4\pi$, over the Planck function \eqref{pr:eq:planck}.
	\item $e^{-h\nu/k_BT}(e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1)=1-e^{-h\nu/k_BT}$.
\end{explain}
At radio frequencies, $h\nu\ll k_BT$, so the last factor approaches $h\nu/k_BT$. The absorption then becomes $1.8\times10^{-12}\,Z_i^2\bar g\,n_en_iT^{-3/2}\nu^{-2}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Ionised gas around hot stars absorbs low-frequency radio light strongly but lets higher-frequency radio light through. Where the gas is opaque, its spectrum rises as $\nu^2$, the Rayleigh–Jeans part of $B_\nu(T)$ (Eq.~\eqref{rad:eq:slab}). Where it is transparent, $I_\nu\approx\tau_\nu B_\nu\propto\nu^{-2}\nu^2$ is nearly flat.

Inside a star, photons with $h\nu$ of a few $k_BT$ carry most of the radiative flux (Section~\ref{rad:sec:diffusion}). To see how the opacity changes with temperature, write $\nu=x\,k_BT/h$ in Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:ff_absorption}.
\[
\kappa_\nu\rho\propto\frac{n_en_i}{\sqrt T}\,\frac{1-e^{-x}}{x^3}\left(\frac{h}{k_BT}\right)^3\propto n_en_i\,T^{-7/2}\,\frac{1-e^{-x}}{x^3} .
\]
The Rosseland average over $x$ changes the coefficient but adds no temperature dependence. Since $n_en_i\propto\rho^2$, the opacity per unit mass obeys
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\kappa_{\rm ff}\propto\rho\,T^{-7/2}.}
	\label{fp:eq:kramers}
\end{equation}
This is the free--free part of Kramers' law (Section~\ref{st:sec:eos}). Its $T^{-7/2}$ comes from the $T^{-1/2}$ in the emission and the $\nu^{-3}$ in the absorption, evaluated at frequencies that rise with $T$. Bound electrons also absorb light. Their contribution has the same density and temperature powers, but a larger coefficient in gas rich in heavy elements.

\section{Synchrotron Radiation}
\label{fp:sec:sync}

We know how to find the power of a fast charge from its acceleration in its own frame (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:power_invariant}). We also know that its light is beamed into an angle of about $1/\gamma_L$ (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:beaming}). The radio light of the Milky Way and radio galaxies has a different shape from thermal light. It falls roughly as $\nu^{-0.7}$ across many decades of frequency and is strongly polarised. A single temperature cannot produce that spectrum.

Think of a lighthouse. A distant ship sees a flash only while the beam points toward it. A narrower beam makes a shorter flash, even when the lamp turns at the same rate. A fast electron turning in a magnetic field sends most of its light along its velocity, so an observer sees brief flashes as the beam sweeps past. The electron also moves toward the observer during each flash, shortening its arrival time further. Short flashes reach high frequencies. Each electron radiates near $\gamma_L^2$ times its slow gyration frequency, with power proportional to $\gamma_L^2B^2$. Counting electrons of different energies then gives the broad radio spectrum (P3).

The Galactic magnetic field is about $0.5\,\mathrm{nT}$. A slow electron circles in it at $14\,\mathrm{Hz}$. An electron with $\gamma_L=10^4$ has an energy of $5\,\mathrm{GeV}$ and circles $10^4$ times more slowly, about once every twelve minutes. Yet it radiates near $10^8\times14\,\mathrm{Hz}=1.4\,\mathrm{GHz}$, in the radio. Picture a helix around a field line and a narrow beam sweeping with the electron's velocity. Synchrotron light also comes from laboratory electron rings, pulsar nebulae, radio jets and lobes, and gamma-ray burst afterglows. The field turns the electron without doing work (P1). The flash length sets the frequency (P6), while the number of electrons at each energy sets the spectrum (P3).

\paragraph{Gyration.} An electron of charge $-e$ and velocity $\vec v$ in a field $\vec B$ obeys
\begin{align}
	\dv{t}\big(\gamma_Lm_e\vec v\big)&=-e\,\vec v\times\vec B . \nonumber\\
	\intertext{Taking the dot product with $\gamma_Lm_e\vec v$,}
	\frac12\dv{t}\big(\gamma_L^2m_e^2v^2\big)
	&\eqby{1} -e\,\gamma_Lm_e\,\vec v\cdot(\vec v\times\vec B)\eqby{2}0
	\quad\implies\quad \gamma_L=\text{const}.
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $\vec a\cdot\dot{\vec a}=\tfrac12\,d(a^2)/dt$ with $\vec a=\gamma_Lm_e\vec v$.
	\item $\vec v\times\vec B$ is perpendicular to $\vec v$. Since $\gamma_L^2v^2=c^2(\gamma_L^2-1)$, a constant $\gamma_L^2v^2$ means a constant $\gamma_L$.
\end{explain}
The magnetic force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it changes the direction but not the energy (P1). With $\gamma_L$ constant, the equation becomes $\gamma_Lm_e\dot{\vec v}=-e\,\vec v\times\vec B$. Motion along $\vec B$ continues unchanged. Across the field, the velocity turns at the angular frequency
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\frac{eB}{\gamma_Lm_e}=\frac{2\pi\nu_g}{\gamma_L},\qquad \nu_g=\frac{eB}{2\pi m_e}=2.80\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{Hz}\times\frac{B}{1\,\mathrm T} ,}
	\label{fp:eq:gyro}
\end{equation}
Here $\nu_g$ is the \textbf{gyrofrequency} of a slow electron. The combined motion is a helix. A fast electron takes $\gamma_L$ times longer to circle, though its observed radiation reaches frequencies about $\gamma_L^2$ above $\nu_g$ (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:sync_freq}).

\paragraph{Power.} Let the electron move along $x$ through a magnetic field $B$ along $z$. There is no electric field in the lab, but there is one in the electron's frame. Electromagnetism gives the boost of the field pair $(E_y,cB_z)$ by the same matrix as $(ct,x)$. To enter the electron's frame, use $\Lambda(-\beta)$,
\begin{align}
	\begin{pmatrix}E'_y\\ cB'_z\end{pmatrix}
	&= \begin{pmatrix}\gamma_L&-\gamma_L\beta\\-\gamma_L\beta&\gamma_L\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0\\ cB\end{pmatrix}
	\eqby{1} \begin{pmatrix}-\gamma_L\beta cB\\ \gamma_LcB\end{pmatrix} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The second column of the matrix times $cB$.
\end{explain}
The electron is momentarily at rest in this frame, so only the electric field $E'=\gamma_LvB$ pushes it. Its acceleration has magnitude $eE'/m_e$. Equation~\eqref{fp:eq:power_invariant} then gives
\begin{align}
	\mathcal P
	&\eqby{1} \frac{e^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3}\left(\frac{e\gamma_LvB}{m_e}\right)^2
	\eqby{2} \frac{e^4}{6\pi\varepsilon_0^2m_e^2c^4}\;\varepsilon_0c\;\gamma_L^2\beta^2c^2B^2 \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \sigma_Tc\,\gamma_L^2\beta^2\,\frac{B^2}{\mu_0}
	\eqby{4} 2\sigma_Tc\,\gamma_L^2\beta^2\,u_B .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Larmor's formula in the electron's frame, with $|\dot{\vec v}'|=eE'/m_e$.
	\item Multiply and divide by $\varepsilon_0c$, and write $v=\beta c$.
	\item The Thomson cross-section of Eq.~\eqref{eq:thomson_cross_section} is $\sigma_T=e^4/(6\pi\varepsilon_0^2m_e^2c^4)$, and $\varepsilon_0c^2=1/\mu_0$.
	\item The magnetic energy density $u_B=B^2/(2\mu_0)$.
\end{explain}
For an electron moving at an angle to the field, only the velocity across $\vec B$ produces the force. The average square of that part over all velocity directions is $\tfrac23\beta^2c^2$, so
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\mathcal P=\frac43\,\sigma_Tc\,\gamma_L^2\beta^2\,u_B .}
	\label{fp:eq:sync_power}
\end{equation}
The power rises with the square of the electron's energy and with the field energy density $u_B$. This form will make the comparison with inverse Compton scattering direct.

\paragraph{How long the electrons last.} For $\beta\approx1$, divide the electron's energy $\gamma_Lm_ec^2$ by its radiated power to find its cooling time,
\[
t_{\rm sync}=\frac{\gamma_Lm_ec^2}{\mathcal P}=\frac{3m_ec}{4\sigma_T\gamma_Lu_B},
\]
Faster electrons cool sooner. The Crab Nebula is the debris of the supernova seen in 1054. In a field of about $10\,\mathrm{nT}$, its $1\,\mathrm{keV}$ X-rays come from electrons with $\gamma_L\approx2.4\times10^7$ (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:sync_freq}). They last only about $100$ years, much less than the nebula's age (P6). The central pulsar must keep supplying them. Electrons making $1\,\mathrm{GHz}$ radio light have $\gamma_L\approx1.5\times10^3$ and last over a million years, so they can accumulate throughout the nebula's life.

\paragraph{The frequency from the flash length.} Consider an electron moving across the field. We see it only while its velocity lies within about $1/\gamma_L$ of our line of sight (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:beaming}). During that flash, its velocity turns through $2/\gamma_L$. Equation~\eqref{fp:eq:gyro} gives the lab duration $\Delta t=(2/\gamma_L)\big/(2\pi\nu_g/\gamma_L)=2/(2\pi\nu_g)$. The electron also approaches us by $\beta c\,\Delta t$. Light sent at the end of the flash has less distance to travel and catches up with the light sent at the start (P6).
\begin{align}
	\Delta t_{\rm obs}
	&\eqby{1} \Delta t-\frac{\beta c\,\Delta t}{c}=\Delta t\,(1-\beta) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \frac{\Delta t}{\gamma_L^2(1+\beta)}
	\eqby{3} \frac{1}{\gamma_L^2}\,\frac{1}{2\pi\nu_g} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The arrival time of the last light, minus the head start it gains.
	\item $1-\beta=(1-\beta^2)/(1+\beta)=1/[\gamma_L^2(1+\beta)]$.
	\item $\Delta t=2/(2\pi\nu_g)$ and $1+\beta\approx2$.
\end{explain}
The received flash is about $\gamma_L^2$ times shorter than a turn of a slow electron. Its spectrum therefore reaches frequencies near $\gamma_L^2\nu_g$.

\paragraph{The frequency from the electron's frame.} In the electron's frame, the same interval lasts $\Delta t'=\Delta t/\gamma_L$, by Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:boost}. Its forward light is shortened again by the Doppler factor $\gamma_L(1+\beta)$ of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:beaming}. Thus $\Delta t_{\rm obs}=\Delta t'/[\gamma_L(1+\beta)]=\Delta t/[\gamma_L^2(1+\beta)]$, exactly as in the lab-frame calculation. Both routes give
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\nu_c\approx\gamma_L^2\,\nu_g .}
	\label{fp:eq:sync_freq}
\end{equation}
The full field calculation gives the \textbf{critical frequency} $\nu_c=\tfrac32\gamma_L^2\nu_g$ for motion across the field. At other angles, multiply $\nu_g$ by the sine of the angle between $\vec v$ and $\vec B$. One electron has the spectrum $F(\nu/\nu_c)$, with $F(x)=x\int_x^\infty K_{5/3}(t)\,dt$. It rises as $\nu^{1/3}$, peaks at $\nu=\FifteenSyncXpeak\,\nu_c$, then falls as $e^{-\nu/\nu_c}$ (Figure~\ref{fp:fig:sync}, top). For the Galactic example, $\gamma_L=10^4$ in $0.5\,\mathrm{nT}$ gives $\nu_c=2.1\,\mathrm{GHz}$.

\paragraph{A power law of electrons.} Shocks can give electrons a power-law distribution, $n(\gamma_L)\,d\gamma_L\propto\gamma_L^{-p}\,d\gamma_L$, between $\gamma_{\min}$ and $\gamma_{\max}$. Often $p$ lies between $2$ and $3$. Each electron radiates most strongly near $\nu=\gamma_L^2\nu_g$. At a given $\nu$, the main contributors thus have $\gamma_L=(\nu/\nu_g)^{1/2}$. Count their power (P3),
\begin{align}
	4\pi j_\nu
	&\eqby{1} \mathcal P(\gamma_L)\,n(\gamma_L)\,\dv{\gamma_L}{\nu} \nonumber\\
	&\relby{2}{\propto} \gamma_L^2B^2\;\gamma_L^{-p}\;\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\nu\nu_g}} \nonumber\\
	&\relby{3}{\propto} B^2\left(\frac{\nu}{\nu_g}\right)^{(2-p)/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu\nu_g}}
	\ \relby{4}{\propto}\ B^{(p+1)/2}\,\nu^{-(p-1)/2} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The power at frequencies $\nu$ to $\nu+d\nu$ is that of the electrons in the matching $d\gamma_L$.
	\item Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:sync_power} with $u_B\propto B^2$ and $\beta\approx1$. From $\gamma_L=(\nu/\nu_g)^{1/2}$, $d\gamma_L/d\nu=1/(2\sqrt{\nu\nu_g})$.
	\item $\gamma_L^{2-p}=(\nu/\nu_g)^{(2-p)/2}$.
	\item The powers of $\nu$ add to $(2-p)/2-\tfrac12=-(p-1)/2$. Since $\nu_g\propto B$, the powers of $B$ add to $2-(2-p)/2-\tfrac12=(p+1)/2$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{j_\nu\propto\nu^{-(p-1)/2}.}
	\label{fp:eq:sync_index}
\end{equation}
The electron distribution sets the slope of the light between its two ends. The one-electron spectrum shapes those ends. Summing the exact $F(x)$ over electrons with $p=\FifteenSyncP$ and $\gamma_{\max}=1000\,\gamma_{\min}$ gives a middle slope of $\FifteenSyncSlope$, close to the $-0.75$ from Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:sync_index}. Below $\gamma_{\min}^2\nu_c$ the spectrum rises with the single-electron slope $\FifteenSyncSlopeLow$. Above $\gamma_{\max}^2\nu_c$ it falls exponentially (Figure~\ref{fp:fig:sync}, bottom).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch15_sync_spectrum.pdf}
	\caption{Top, the synchrotron spectrum $F(\nu/\nu_c)$ of one electron. Bottom, the spectrum of electrons with $n(\gamma_L)\propto\gamma_L^{-2.5}$ between $\gamma_{\min}$ and $1000\,\gamma_{\min}$, against $\nu/\nu_c(\gamma_{\min})$. Ticks on the frequency axis mark $\nu_c(\gamma_{\min})$ and $\nu_c(\gamma_{\max})$. The dashed line has slope $-0.75$. Computed with \texttt{code/ch15/01\_synchrotron.py}.}
	\label{fp:fig:sync}
\end{figure}

Most radio galaxies have $\alpha\approx0.7$ to $0.8$ in $j_\nu\propto\nu^{-\alpha}$, corresponding to electron slopes $p\approx2.4$ to $2.6$. The Crab's radio spectrum is flatter, with $\alpha\approx0.3$ and $p\approx1.6$. Cooling can steepen either spectrum (P4). Electrons supplied at a rate $\propto\gamma_L^{-p}$ survive for $t_{\rm sync}\propto1/\gamma_L$, leaving a population $\propto\gamma_L^{-(p+1)}$ where cooling is fast. Above the resulting break, $\alpha$ increases by $\tfrac12$. Its frequency can reveal the age of a radio source. Synchrotron light is linearly polarised across the magnetic field projected on the sky. Polarisation maps therefore trace cosmic magnetic fields; the strong optical polarisation of the Crab helped identify its light as synchrotron radiation.

\section{Compton and Inverse Compton Scattering}
\label{fp:sec:compton}

Equation~\eqref{eq:thomson_cross_section} gives the cross-section for light scattered by a free electron. In the Thomson limit, the photon leaves with almost the energy it arrived with. A hard X-ray can instead recoil from the electron and lose energy. A fast electron can give energy to the photon. These are Compton and inverse Compton scattering, the energy-changing processes listed in Table~\ref{tab:scattering_regimes}.

Think of a ball hitting a racket. A still racket sends it back with nearly its arrival speed. A racket moving toward it sends it back faster. The motion is easiest to describe from the racket, where the ball arrives faster, then to translate back to the court. A photon meeting a fast electron behaves similarly. It is blueshifted by about $\gamma_L$ on the way into the electron's frame and again on the way out. In that frame the scattering is nearly Thomson scattering. The boost matrix of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:boost} carries the photon there and back.

A microwave-background photon has a mean energy of $6.3\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{eV}$. After meeting an electron with $\gamma_L=10^4$, its average energy can be $85\,\mathrm{keV}$, a hard X-ray. The same exchange of energy between frames appears in the spacecraft gravity assist of Chapter~\ref{ch:twobody}. Inverse Compton scattering makes X-rays in radio lobes, gamma rays in pulsar nebulae and blazars, and X-rays above accretion disks. We use conservation of energy and momentum (P1) for one encounter, a thermal distribution for hot gas (P3), and a random walk in photon energy for many encounters (P5). These lead to the Compton shift, inverse Compton power, the Compton $y$ parameter, and the Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect.

\paragraph{The Compton shift.} First let the electron be at rest. A photon of frequency $\nu$ leaves the encounter at angle $\psi$ with frequency $\nu_1$. Write a four-momentum as (energy over $c$, momentum), with dot product $A\cdot B=A^0B^0-\vec A\cdot\vec B$. The photon has $k=(h\nu/c)(1,\hat n)$ before and $k_1=(h\nu_1/c)(1,\hat n_1)$ after. The electron has $q=(m_ec,\vec0)$ before and $q_1$ after. Conservation (P1) gives $q+k=q_1+k_1$.
\begin{align}
	m_e^2c^2
	&\eqby{1} q_1\cdot q_1=(q+k-k_1)\cdot(q+k-k_1) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} m_e^2c^2+2\,q\cdot k-2\,q\cdot k_1-2\,k\cdot k_1 \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad m_eh(\nu-\nu_1)
	&\eqby{3} \frac{h^2\nu\nu_1}{c^2}\,(1-\cos\psi) \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \frac{c}{\nu_1}-\frac{c}{\nu}
	&\eqby{4} \frac{h}{m_ec}\,(1-\cos\psi) .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The electron's mass is the same before and after. Conservation gives $q_1$.
	\item Expand. $q\cdot q=m_e^2c^2$, and $k\cdot k=k_1\cdot k_1=0$ for photons.
	\item $q\cdot k=m_eh\nu$, $q\cdot k_1=m_eh\nu_1$, and $k\cdot k_1=(h^2\nu\nu_1/c^2)(1-\hat n\cdot\hat n_1)$ with $\hat n\cdot\hat n_1=\cos\psi$.
	\item Divide by $m_eh\nu\nu_1/c$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\lambda_1-\lambda=\lambda_C\,(1-\cos\psi),\qquad h\nu_1=\frac{h\nu}{1+(h\nu/m_ec^2)(1-\cos\psi)},}
	\label{fp:eq:compton_shift}
\end{equation}
Here the Compton wavelength is $\lambda_C=h/(m_ec)=2.43\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm m$, and $m_ec^2=511\,\mathrm{keV}$. The wavelength shift depends on the scattering angle, not the incoming wavelength. It becomes important when $\lambda$ approaches $\lambda_C$, or $h\nu$ approaches $m_ec^2$. At $h\nu\ll m_ec^2$, recoil is small and we recover the Thomson limit. A $10\,\mathrm{keV}$ X-ray loses about $2\%$ of its energy per scattering from cold electrons.

\paragraph{Inverse Compton, there and back.} Now let the electron move along $x$ with Lorentz factor $\gamma_L$. A photon of frequency $\nu$ approaches at angle $\theta$ to its velocity. Boost the photon into the electron's frame with $\Lambda(-\beta)$,
\begin{align}
	\frac{h\nu'}{c}\begin{pmatrix}1\\ \cos\theta'\\ \sin\theta'\end{pmatrix}
	&\eqby{1} \begin{pmatrix}\gamma_L&-\gamma_L\beta&0\\-\gamma_L\beta&\gamma_L&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\frac{h\nu}{c}\begin{pmatrix}1\\ \cos\theta\\ \sin\theta\end{pmatrix}
	=\frac{h\nu}{c}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_L(1-\beta\cos\theta)\\ \gamma_L(\cos\theta-\beta)\\ \sin\theta\end{pmatrix} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \nu'
	&\eqby{2} \gamma_L(1-\beta\cos\theta)\,\nu .
	\label{fp:eq:to_electron_frame}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The inverse boost of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:boost} applied to the photon's four-momentum.
	\item The first row.
\end{explain}
When $h\nu'\ll m_ec^2$, recoil is small in the electron's frame. The photon keeps frequency $\nu'$ and leaves at angle $\theta'_1$. Boosting it back to the lab with $\Lambda(\beta)$ gives, from Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:beaming},
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\nu_1=\gamma_L(1+\beta\cos\theta'_1)\,\nu'=\gamma_L^2\,(1-\beta\cos\theta)(1+\beta\cos\theta'_1)\,\nu .}
	\label{fp:eq:ic_boost}
\end{equation}
The largest gain occurs when the photon arrives head on, $\theta=\pi$, and leaves forward, $\theta'_1=0$. Then $\nu_1=\gamma_L^2(1+\beta)^2\nu\approx4\gamma_L^2\nu$. Each boost supplies one factor of $\gamma_L$. This Thomson result requires $h\nu'\approx\gamma_Lh\nu\ll m_ec^2$ in the electron's frame. At higher energies, recoil matters (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:compton_shift}), and the photon cannot gain more than about the electron's energy $\gamma_Lm_ec^2$.

\paragraph{The power.} Let the electron cross a photon field of energy density $u_{\rm rad}$, equal in all directions. Photons arriving at angle $\theta$ meet it at the closing speed $c(1-\beta\cos\theta)$. The share arriving within $d\Omega$ carries energy density $u_{\rm rad}\,d\Omega/4\pi$ and scatters at a rate proportional to $\sigma_Tc(1-\beta\cos\theta)$. In the electron's frame, Thomson scattering is symmetric forward and backward, so the average $\cos\theta'_1$ in Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:ic_boost} is zero. Subtract the incoming photon power from the outgoing power,
\begin{align}
	\mathcal P
	&\eqby{1} \sigma_Tc\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\,u_{\rm rad}\,(1-\beta\cos\theta)\Big[\gamma_L^2(1-\beta\cos\theta)-1\Big] \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \sigma_Tc\,u_{\rm rad}\Big[\gamma_L^2\Big(1+\frac{\beta^2}{3}\Big)-1\Big] \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \sigma_Tc\,u_{\rm rad}\Big[\gamma_L^2\beta^2+\frac{\gamma_L^2\beta^2}{3}\Big] .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Rate of scattering times the energy gained, which is $\gamma_L^2(1-\beta\cos\theta)$ times the arriving energy by Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:ic_boost}, minus the arriving energy.
	\item $\int(1-\beta\cos\theta)^2\,d\Omega/4\pi=1+\beta^2/3$ and $\int(1-\beta\cos\theta)\,d\Omega/4\pi=1$.
	\item $\gamma_L^2-1=\gamma_L^2\beta^2$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\mathcal P=\frac43\,\sigma_Tc\,\gamma_L^2\beta^2\,u_{\rm rad} .}
	\label{fp:eq:ic_power}
\end{equation}
This is the synchrotron power of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:sync_power} with photon energy density in place of magnetic energy density. The electron's losses to the two processes have the ratio $u_{\rm rad}/u_B$. Dividing the scattered power by the photon scattering rate $\sigma_Tc\,u_{\rm rad}/\avg{h\nu}$ gives the mean scattered energy $\tfrac43\gamma_L^2\avg{h\nu}$.

At $2.725\,\mathrm K$, the microwave background has $u_{\rm rad}=a_{\rm rad}T^4=4.2\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$ (Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:photon_gas}). A magnetic field of $0.32\,\mathrm{nT}$ has the same energy density. In radio lobes, where fields are of order $1\,\mathrm{nT}$, scattering the background can take a tenth or more as much electron energy as synchrotron emission and produce X-rays. In the Crab, electrons scatter their own synchrotron photons up to gamma-ray energies.

\paragraph{Many scatterings in hot gas.} Consider slow, thermal electrons with $\beta\ll1$ and photons with $h\nu\ll k_BT$. We need the mean gain and the spread of frequency changes in one scattering. The mean follows from Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:ic_power} divided by the scattering rate. For Maxwellian electrons, $\avg{\gamma_L^2\beta^2}\approx\avg{v^2}/c^2=3k_BT/m_ec^2$ (P3). Let $\Delta\nu=\nu_1-\nu$ mean after minus before one scattering. To first order in $\beta$, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:ic_boost} gives $\Delta\nu/\nu=\beta(\cos\theta'_1-\cos\theta)$. The two directions are independent of the electron velocity and each has $\avg{\cos^2}=\tfrac13$,
\begin{equation}
	\avg{\frac{\Delta\nu}{\nu}}=\frac43\cdot\frac{3k_BT}{m_ec^2}=\frac{4k_BT}{m_ec^2},\qquad
	\avg{\Big(\frac{\Delta\nu}{\nu}\Big)^2}=\frac{3k_BT}{m_ec^2}\Big(\frac13+\frac13\Big)=\frac{2k_BT}{m_ec^2} .
	\label{fp:eq:ic_moments}
\end{equation}
Recoil would remove a fraction $h\nu/m_ec^2$ of the photon energy (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:compton_shift}). It is small here because $h\nu\ll k_BT$. The cloud's effect depends on the number of scatterings multiplied by the small gain per scattering. That product is the \textbf{Compton $y$ parameter},
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{y=\int\frac{k_BT}{m_ec^2}\,n_e\sigma_T\,dl ,}
	\label{fp:eq:compton_y}
\end{equation}
the optical depth weighted by $k_BT/m_ec^2$. A thin cloud gives about $\tau=\int n_e\sigma_Tdl$ scatterings per photon. In a thick cloud, the photon wanders and scatters about $\tau^2$ times (Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:walk_time}, P5).

\paragraph{The random walk in frequency.} Let $x=h\nu/k_BT_\gamma$, with $T_\gamma$ fixed. Then $x$ is simply frequency in chosen units. Each scattering changes it by a small, random amount. Equation~\eqref{fp:eq:ic_moments} gives the moments of that change per scattering. We first express them per unit $y$, then track how photons move between frequency intervals.

\paragraph{What $\Delta x$ means.} Follow one photon through the cloud, using $y$ of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:compton_y} as a clock that starts at entry. Let $x(y)$ be its frequency variable at that point. Over a short stretch $dy$, it changes by
\begin{equation}
	\Delta x=x(y+dy)-x(y) .
	\label{fp:eq:delta_x}
\end{equation}
The two $\Delta$ symbols cover different intervals. $\Delta\nu=\nu_1-\nu$ is the change in one scattering, while $\Delta x$ covers the whole stretch $dy$. A photon might not scatter during that stretch. If it does, the size of the change depends on the electron it meets. We therefore average over many photons starting at the same $x$ to find $\avg{\Delta x}$ and $\avg{(\Delta x)^2}$.

\paragraph{From one scattering to a stretch of $y$.} Let $n_{\rm sc}$ count the expected scatterings. Along $dl$, it grows by $dn_{\rm sc}=n_e\sigma_T\,dl$. Equation~\eqref{fp:eq:compton_y} then gives $dn_{\rm sc}=(m_ec^2/k_BT)\,dy$. In a sufficiently short stretch, the photon scatters once with probability $dn_{\rm sc}$ and twice only with probability of order $dn_{\rm sc}^2$. With no scattering, $\Delta x=0$. With one, $\Delta x=x\,\Delta\nu/\nu$, since $x$ and $\nu$ change by the same fraction. Averaging these possibilities gives
\begin{align}
	\avg{\Delta x}
	&\eqby{1} dn_{\rm sc}\;x\avg{\frac{\Delta\nu}{\nu}} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \frac{m_ec^2}{k_BT}\,dy\;x\,\frac{4k_BT}{m_ec^2} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} 4x\,dy , \\[4pt]
	\avg{(\Delta x)^2}
	&\eqby{4} dn_{\rm sc}\;x^2\avg{\Big(\frac{\Delta\nu}{\nu}\Big)^2}
	\eqby{5} \frac{m_ec^2}{k_BT}\,dy\;x^2\,\frac{2k_BT}{m_ec^2}
	=2x^2\,dy . \nonumber
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The step is $x\,\Delta\nu/\nu$ with chance $dn_{\rm sc}$ and zero otherwise.
	\item $dn_{\rm sc}=(m_ec^2/k_BT)\,dy$ from the definition of $y$, and the mean of $\Delta\nu/\nu$ from Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:ic_moments}.
	\item The factors $k_BT/m_ec^2$ cancel. This is why $y$ is the right clock.
	\item The same for the square of the step.
	\item The mean square of $\Delta\nu/\nu$ from Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:ic_moments}.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\frac{\avg{\Delta x}}{dy}=4x,\qquad \frac{\avg{(\Delta x)^2}}{dy}=2x^2 .}
	\label{fp:eq:x_moments}
\end{equation}
Per unit $y$, a photon starting at $x$ moves upward by $4x$ on average, with a mean square step of $2x^2$. These are local rates, measured over a short stretch. The second is the mean square of one step; it is not the width of the full photon spectrum.

\paragraph{How the photon numbers change.} Let $N(x,y)\,dx$ count photons between $x$ and $x+dx$. To connect single-photon steps to this distribution, choose a smooth test function $w(x)$ that vanishes outside a finite range. Sum its value over all photons, $W=\int N\,w\,dx$. When each photon moves from $x$ to $x+\Delta x$ during $dy$, the sum changes by
\begin{align}
	dW
	&\eqby{1} \sum_{\rm photons}\big[w(x+\Delta x)-w(x)\big] \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \sum_{\rm photons}\Big[\Delta x\,w'(x)+\frac12(\Delta x)^2\,w''(x)\Big] \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \int N\Big[4x\,w'+x^2\,w''\Big]dx\;dy , \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \int w\,\pdv{N}{y}\,dx
	&\eqby{4} \int N\Big[4x\,w'+x^2\,w''\Big]dx \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{5} \int w\Big[-\pdv{x}\big(4xN\big)+\pdv[2]{x}\big(x^2N\big)\Big]dx .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $W$ counts $w$ once for each photon, so it changes by the change of $w$ at each photon.
	\item Taylor expansion of $w$ about the starting $x$. Each step $\Delta\nu/\nu$ is of order $\beta$, so the third and higher moments per unit $y$ are smaller by further factors of $\sqrt{k_BT/m_ec^2}\ll1$ and are dropped. This small-step approximation is the whole of the physics assumed.
	\item The $N\,dx$ photons near $x$ all start at $x$, so their steps average to Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:x_moments}, $\avg{\Delta x}=4x\,dy$ and $\tfrac12\avg{(\Delta x)^2}=x^2\,dy$.
	\item Divide by $dy$. The function $w$ does not depend on $y$, so $dW/dy=\int w\,\partial N/\partial y\,dx$.
	\item Integrate by parts, once in the first term and twice in the second. The end terms vanish because $w$ and $w'$ are zero at the ends of its range.
\end{explain}
Because the last line holds for every smooth $w$, the expressions it weighs must agree at each $x$,
\begin{equation}
	\pdv{N}{y}=-\pdv{x}\Big[4xN-\pdv{x}\big(x^2N\big)\Big] .
	\label{fp:eq:fokker_planck}
\end{equation}
This is a continuity equation in frequency space (P1). Scattering moves photons between frequencies without creating or destroying them. The bracket is the flux toward higher $x$. Its first term, $4xN$, describes the average upward drift. The second describes random spreading (P5), like diffusion in Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:fick}, with larger steps at higher $x$. Write $N=x^2f$, where $f$ is the photon occupation number of Section~\ref{pr:sec:p3},
\begin{align}
	\pdv{N}{y}
	&\eqby{1} -\pdv{x}\Big[4x^3f-\pdv{x}\big(x^4f\big)\Big] \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \pdv{x}\Big(x^4\pdv{f}{x}\Big) .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $N=x^2f$ in Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:fokker_planck}.
	\item $\partial(x^4f)/\partial x=4x^3f+x^4\,\partial f/\partial x$, and the $4x^3f$ terms cancel.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\pdv{\big(x^2f\big)}{y}=\pdv{x}\Big(x^4\pdv{f}{x}\Big).}
	\label{fp:eq:kompaneets}
\end{equation}
This is the Kompaneets equation in the limit of negligible recoil. Multiply by $x$ and integrate over frequency to get $d\avg{x}/dy=4\avg{x}$. The mean photon energy then grows as $e^{4y}$ while the approximation holds. In a hot corona above an accretion disk, repeated scattering can lift cooler seed photons into an X-ray spectrum.

\paragraph{The Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect.} Microwave-background photons crossing a hot galaxy cluster have $y\ll1$. Choose $T_\gamma$ as the background temperature. Before the cluster, their occupation number is $f_0=1/(e^x-1)$. To first order in $y$, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:kompaneets} gives
\begin{align}
	\frac{\Delta f}{f_0}
	&\eqby{1} \frac{y}{x^2f_0}\,\dv{x}\Big(x^4\dv{f_0}{x}\Big) \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} y\,\frac{xe^x}{e^x-1}\Big(x\,\frac{e^x+1}{e^x-1}-4\Big)
	\ \xrightarrow{\ x\ll1\ }\ -2y .
	\label{fp:eq:sz}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Integrate Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:kompaneets} across the cluster with the right-hand side evaluated on $f_0$.
	\item $df_0/dx=-e^x/(e^x-1)^2$, differentiated once more. For $x\ll1$ the first factor tends to $1$ and the bracket to $2-4$.
\end{explain}
At low frequencies, intensity is proportional to temperature. The background seen through the cluster therefore appears cooler,
\[
\boxed{\frac{\Delta T}{T_{\rm CMB}}=-2y\qquad(h\nu\ll k_BT_{\rm CMB}).}
\]
Scattering shifts photons upward in frequency without removing them. A deficit at low frequencies therefore comes with an excess at high frequencies. The change crosses zero at $x=3.83$, or $217\,\mathrm{GHz}$ (Figure~\ref{fp:fig:sz}). For a cluster with $n_e=10^3\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, $k_BT=6\,\mathrm{keV}$, and a path length of $1\,\mathrm{Mpc}$, we have $\tau=2.1\times10^{-3}$, $k_BT/m_ec^2=0.012$, and $y=2.4\times10^{-5}$. The low-frequency background appears dimmer by $130\,\mu\mathrm K$. This fractional distortion does not fade with the cluster's distance (P6), which lets surveys find distant clusters.

\section{Accretion and Active Galaxies}
\label{fp:sec:accretion}

A quasar can shine at $10^{40}\,\mathrm W$, about a thousand times a large galaxy of $10^{10}$ to $10^{11}$ Suns, while its observed brightness rises or falls noticeably over a few days. The region producing that variable light can be only a few light-days across. Hydrogen burning releases nuclear binding energy because the fusion products have slightly less mass than the fuel. That mass difference is about $0.7\%$ of the fuel's rest mass (Section~\ref{st:sec:eos}), too little to power a quasar from so small a region. Accretion draws on a different source, gravitational energy. As gas spirals inward, its gravitational potential energy falls even as its orbital speed rises. Friction between disk rings turns the decrease in total orbital energy into heat, which the gas radiates.

Water falling over a dam turns a turbine. The power depends on both the height of the fall and how much water flows each second. Accreting gas follows the same energy accounting. The depth of the gravitational well sets the energy available per kilogram, while the inflow rate sets how many kilograms arrive. In a disk, friction transfers orbital energy between rings and heats the gas, which radiates that heat (P1, P4).

Falling onto a neutron star can release gravitational energy equal to $0.17$ of the gas's rest-mass energy, $25$ times the yield per kilogram from burning hydrogen. A quasar shining at $10^{40}\,\mathrm W$ with efficiency $0.1$ needs $1.1\times10^{24}\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$, or $18$ solar masses a year. Picture a thin disk growing hotter toward the centre, sometimes with narrow jets along its axis. Accretion also powers X-ray binaries, white-dwarf outbursts, and disks around young stars. Energy and angular momentum conservation (P1), gravity (P2), local heating and cooling balance (P4), and light-travel times (P6) give the luminosity, disk spectrum, and apparent jet speeds.

\paragraph{Accretion luminosity.} Let gas fall from far away onto a body of mass $M$ and radius $R$ at a rate $\dot M$. Outside the body, the gravitational potential energy per kilogram is $\Phi(r)=-GM/r$ (P2). The gas starts nearly at rest, where $\Phi\approx0$, and gains speed as it falls. When it reaches the surface, that motion turns to heat. In steady state the heat leaves as radiation at the rate it is made (P4). Multiply the energy released per kilogram by $\dot M$,
\begin{align}
	L
	&\eqby{1} \big[\Phi(\infty)-\Phi(R)\big]\,\dot M \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \Big[0-\Big(-\frac{GM}{R}\Big)\Big]\dot M \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \frac{GM\dot M}{R} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Energy conservation (P1). Each kilogram releases the drop of its potential energy on the way down, and in a steady state all of it is radiated (P4).
	\item $\Phi(r)=-GM/r$ outside the body, so $\Phi(\infty)=0$ and $\Phi(R)=-GM/R$.
	\item The two minus signs cancel.
\end{explain}
Divide the radiated power by $\dot Mc^2$, the rest-mass energy carried in each second. This defines the \textbf{efficiency} $\eta$. The denominator sets a scale for comparison. The light comes from gravitational energy lost during infall, and the ratio is
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{L=\frac{GM\dot M}{R},\qquad \eta\equiv\frac{L}{\dot Mc^2}=\frac{GM}{Rc^2} .}
	\label{fp:eq:accretion_L}
\end{equation}
This $\eta$ equals the compactness in Table~\ref{cs:tab:compactness}. It is $2.6\times10^{-4}$ for a white dwarf like Sirius B and $0.17$ for a neutron star. A black hole has no surface, so gas radiates as it spirals toward the last stable orbit. For a black hole without spin, general relativity puts that orbit at $6GM/c^2$ and gives efficiency $1-\sqrt{8/9}=0.057$. For the fastest allowed spin, it can reach $0.42$. These efficiencies come from general relativity. The Newtonian disk estimate below releases half of $GM/R$ by $R=6GM/c^2$, giving $1/12=0.083$.

\paragraph{The Eddington limit and black-hole masses.} Radiation pushes back on the infalling gas. Equation~\eqref{rad:eq:eddington} limits the steady luminosity of mass $M$ to $1.26\times10^{31}\,\mathrm W\,(M/M_\odot)$. A quasar at $10^{40}\,\mathrm W$ therefore needs at least $8\times10^8\,M_\odot$ (Problem~\ref{rad:prob:eddington}). If it brightens within one day, the emitting region cannot be much larger than one light-day, $2.6\times10^{13}\,\mathrm m$ (P6). The Schwarzschild radius of $10^9\,M_\odot$ is $3\times10^{12}\,\mathrm m$. The source thus holds roughly a billion solar masses within only a few tens of Schwarzschild radii, pointing to a black hole.

\paragraph{The thin disk, angular momentum.} Gas approaching the hole has angular momentum, so it settles into a disk rather than falling straight in. At radius $r$, a ring orbits at angular speed $\sqrt{GM/r^3}$ and has angular momentum $\sqrt{GMr}$ per unit mass. It can move inward only by passing angular momentum to outer rings. Let $\mathcal T(r)$ be the torque exerted by gas inside $r$ on gas outside. In steady flow, the inward angular momentum carried by gas minus the outward amount carried by the torque is constant with radius (P1). At the inner edge $R_{\rm in}$, the gas plunges toward the hole and we take the torque to vanish.
\begin{align}
	\dot M\sqrt{GMr}-\mathcal T(r)
	&\eqby{1} \dot M\sqrt{GMR_{\rm in}}-0 \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \mathcal T(r)
	&= \dot M\sqrt{GM}\,\big(\sqrt r-\sqrt{R_{\rm in}}\big) .
	\label{fp:eq:torque}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The constant evaluated at the inner edge, where $\mathcal T=0$.
\end{explain}

\paragraph{The thin disk, energy.} Energy also flows between rings. Gas moving inward carries orbital energy $-GM/2r$ per kilogram, equivalent to an outward energy flow $\dot MGM/2r$. The torque does work on outer gas at a rate $\mathcal T\sqrt{GM/r^3}$. A ring between $r$ and $r+dr$ radiates the energy it takes from these flows (P1). Both faces emit locally as blackbodies at temperature $T(r)$ (P4, Eq.~\eqref{eq:surface_flux_sb}).
\begin{align}
	2\cdot2\pi r\,dr\;\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4
	&\eqby{1} -\dv{r}\Big[\frac{GM\dot M}{2r}+\mathcal T\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r^3}}\,\Big]dr \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} -GM\dot M\,\dv{r}\Big[\frac{1}{2r}+\frac1r-\frac{\sqrt{R_{\rm in}}}{r^{3/2}}\Big]dr \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} GM\dot M\Big[\frac{3}{2r^2}-\frac{3\sqrt{R_{\rm in}}}{2r^{5/2}}\Big]dr
	= \frac{3GM\dot M}{2r^2}\Big(1-\sqrt{\frac{R_{\rm in}}{r}}\Big)dr .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Radiation from the two faces of the ring equals the energy flow into it from inside minus the flow out of it.
	\item The torque \eqref{fp:eq:torque}, so $\mathcal T\sqrt{GM/r^3}=GM\dot M\big(r^{-1}-\sqrt{R_{\rm in}}\,r^{-3/2}\big)$.
	\item Differentiate term by term.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4=\frac{3GM\dot M}{8\pi r^3}\Big(1-\sqrt{\frac{R_{\rm in}}{r}}\Big),\qquad T\propto r^{-3/4}\ \text{for }r\gg R_{\rm in}.}
	\label{fp:eq:disk_T}
\end{equation}
The product $M\dot M$ sets the temperature at a given radius. Far from the inner edge, a ring radiates three times the energy released locally by gas moving through it. The torque transports the other two thirds from smaller radii.

Integrating over the disk checks the energy budget,
\[
\int_{R_{\rm in}}^\infty\frac{3GM\dot M}{2r^2}\Big(1-\sqrt{\frac{R_{\rm in}}{r}}\Big)dr=\frac{GM\dot M}{2R_{\rm in}},
\]
This is half the energy $GM\dot M/R_{\rm in}$ available from the fall. The gas still carries the other half as orbital kinetic energy at $R_{\rm in}$, as required for a circular orbit (Section~\ref{sg:sec:virial}). A star releases that energy where the gas reaches its surface. A black hole swallows it.

Define $T_*=(3GM\dot M/8\pi\sigma_{\rm SB}R_{\rm in}^3)^{1/4}$. Then $T=T_*(R_{\rm in}/r)^{3/4}(1-\sqrt{R_{\rm in}/r})^{1/4}$, with a peak at $r=\tfrac{49}{36}R_{\rm in}$ and $T=0.488\,T_*$ (Figure~\ref{fp:fig:disk}). A $10^8\,M_\odot$ black hole fed $1\,M_\odot$ a year, with $R_{\rm in}=6GM/c^2$, has a hottest ring at $1.1\times10^5\,\mathrm K$. Its disk shines mainly in the ultraviolet with $4.7\times10^{38}\,\mathrm W$. For a $10\,M_\odot$ black hole fed $10^{-8}\,M_\odot$ a year, the peak is $3.5\times10^6\,\mathrm K$, or $k_BT=0.30\,\mathrm{keV}$, in X-rays. Because $R_{\rm in}\propto M$, we have $T_*\propto(\dot M/M^2)^{1/4}$. At a fixed fraction of the Eddington limit, $\dot M\propto M$ and $T_*\propto M^{-1/4}$. Smaller black holes therefore have hotter disks.

\paragraph{The spectrum of the disk.} Each face of a ring emits flux $\pi B_\nu(T(r))$ (Eq.~\eqref{ph:eq:flux_pi_B}). Far from the inner edge, $T=T_*(R_{\rm in}/r)^{3/4}$. To add the rings at one frequency $\nu$, use $u=h\nu/k_BT(r)=(h\nu/k_BT_*)(r/R_{\rm in})^{3/4}$ in place of radius. Then $r=R_{\rm in}(uk_BT_*/h\nu)^{4/3}$.
\begin{align}
	L_\nu
	&\eqby{1} \int 2\cdot2\pi r\;\pi B_\nu\big(T(r)\big)\,dr \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} 4\pi^2\,\frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\int\frac{r\,dr}{e^{u}-1} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} 4\pi^2\,\frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\,\frac43\,R_{\rm in}^2\left(\frac{k_BT_*}{h\nu}\right)^{8/3}\int_0^\infty\frac{u^{5/3}\,du}{e^u-1}
	\ \propto\ \nu^{1/3} .
	\label{fp:eq:disk_spectrum}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Two faces of each ring of area $2\pi r\,dr$.
	\item The Planck function \eqref{pr:eq:planck} with $h\nu/k_BT(r)=u$.
	\item $r\,dr=\tfrac43R_{\rm in}^2(k_BT_*/h\nu)^{8/3}u^{5/3}du$. For $k_BT(\text{outer edge})\ll h\nu\ll k_BT_*$ the limits of $u$ are $0$ and $\infty$, and the integral is the number $\Gamma(\tfrac83)\zeta(\tfrac83)=1.93$.
\end{explain}
Each ring is nearly a blackbody, but the whole disk contains many temperatures. Between the outer and inner disk frequencies, their sum follows $L_\nu\propto\nu^{1/3}$. It cuts off near the frequency of the hottest ring. In quasars, this broad hump appears in the ultraviolet.

\paragraph{Faster than light.} Radio interferometers track bright knots moving along jets from active nuclei (Chapter~\ref{ch:resolution}). Some appear to cross the sky faster than light. Let a knot leave the core at $t=0$ with speed $\beta c$ at angle $\theta$ to our line of sight. After time $t$, it has moved $\beta ct\cos\theta$ toward us and $\beta ct\sin\theta$ across the sky. Its later light starts closer to us, so the observed interval between the two positions is shorter than $t$ (P6).
\begin{align}
	\Delta t_{\rm obs}
	&\eqby{1} t-\frac{\beta ct\cos\theta}{c}=t\,(1-\beta\cos\theta) \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \beta_{\rm app}
	&\eqby{2} \frac{\beta ct\sin\theta}{c\,\Delta t_{\rm obs}}=\frac{\beta\sin\theta}{1-\beta\cos\theta} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The time between emissions minus the head start of the later light.
	\item The apparent speed across the sky, in units of $c$, is the sideways distance over the observed time.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\beta_{\rm app}=\frac{\beta\sin\theta}{1-\beta\cos\theta}.}
	\label{fp:eq:superluminal}
\end{equation}
The apparent speed peaks where the derivative $\beta(\cos\theta-\beta)/(1-\beta\cos\theta)^2$ vanishes, at $\cos\theta=\beta$. There $\sin\theta=1/\gamma_L$ and $\beta_{\rm app}=\beta\gamma_L$, which exceeds $1$ for $\beta>1/\sqrt2$ (Figure~\ref{fp:fig:superluminal}). The knot itself still moves below $c$; its later light has less distance to travel. This is the same arrival-time compression that shortened the synchrotron flash. Since $\beta_{\rm app}\le\beta\gamma_L<\gamma_L$, an apparent speed gives a lower limit on the jet's Lorentz factor. Features in M87's jet move at about $6c$, implying $\gamma_L\ge6$ (Problem~\ref{fp:prob:m87}). Beaming also brightens the approaching jet and dims the receding one (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:beaming}), making many jets look one-sided.

\section{Active Nuclei Seen From Different Angles}
\label{fp:sec:agn}

Accretion onto a black hole explains the power of a quasar (Section~\ref{fp:sec:accretion}). Yet active galaxies look different from one another. Some show both broad and narrow emission lines; others show only narrow lines. Some have vast radio lobes, while others flicker within hours from radio to gamma rays. The difference depends on what surrounds the hole, how large those parts are, and the angle from which we see them.

Imagine a lamp inside a thick, ring-shaped shade, open at the top and bottom. From above, the bulb is visible. From the side, the shade hides it, though light still reaches a distant wall. If the bulb flashes, the wall brightens later, and the delay tells us how far away it is. In an active nucleus, the hot disk is the bulb, a ring of dust is the shade, and more distant gas catches the light. Delays locate the gas. Its speed weighs the black hole, and the temperature at which dust survives locates the ring. The view changes with the sight line and with the brightness of the engine.

Take a nucleus shining at $10^{37}\,\mathrm W$. Its broad emission line follows changes in the continuum $20$ days later, placing the line-making gas about $20$ light-days from the centre. The line width gives a line-of-sight speed spread of $1500\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Holding gas that fast at that distance requires a black hole of $2.6\times10^7\,M_\odot$. Dust inside $32$ light-days would rise above $1500\,\mathrm K$ and evaporate, so the dusty ring begins beyond the fast gas. Figure~\ref{fp:fig:agn} shows the disk and hole at the centre, fast clouds nearby, slower clouds farther out, and jets along the axis. The delay uses the light-travel argument of the radar echo (Eq.~\eqref{as:eq:echo}, P6). The line width uses the virial theorem (Eq.~\eqref{ga:eq:Mvir}, P2). Dust temperature follows radiative balance (Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:teq}, P4). Together, these measurements reveal the structure and mass of the nucleus.

Black hole and accretion disk at the centre, broad-line clouds above and below the disk, dusty torus, narrow-line clouds in two cones, and two jets. Arrows are sight lines to observers who see a blazar, a quasar or Seyfert~1, and a radio galaxy or Seyfert~2.}

\paragraph{The light of an active nucleus.} Starlight fills a normal galaxy (Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxies}). An active nucleus adds radiation from a hot disk, free electrons, and jets. The disk makes the ultraviolet hump of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:disk_spectrum}. Hot electrons above it can scatter disk photons into X-rays when $y\gtrsim1$. Jets and lobes make radio synchrotron light with spectra like Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:sync_index}; inverse Compton scattering can add X-rays and gamma rays. Gas falling toward a black hole of $10^6$ to $10^{10}\,M_\odot$ supplies the energy with the efficiency of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:accretion_L}. For the size estimates below, use the $10^{37}\,\mathrm W$, $2.6\times10^7\,M_\odot$ example.

\paragraph{The hole and the disk.} For $2.6\times10^7\,M_\odot$, the Schwarzschild radius is $2GM/c^2=7.8\times10^{10}\,\mathrm m$, or four light-minutes. At efficiency $0.1$, a luminosity of $10^{37}\,\mathrm W$ requires $\dot M=L/(0.1c^2)=1.1\times10^{21}\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$, or $0.018\,M_\odot$ a year (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:accretion_L}). Far from the disk's inner edge, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:disk_T} gives the radius at temperature $T$,
\[
r=\left(\frac{3GM\dot M}{8\pi\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4}\right)^{1/3}.
\]
The disk reaches $10^4\,\mathrm K$ at $9.4\times10^{12}\,\mathrm m$, or $0.36$ light-days and $120$ Schwarzschild radii, where it shines in visible light. Its hottest ring, for $R_{\rm in}=6GM/c^2$, reaches $7.8\times10^4\,\mathrm K$ and shines in the ultraviolet. The visible and ultraviolet emitting disk fits within about a light-day.

\paragraph{The broad-line region, weighed.} Many active nuclei have emission lines thousands of $\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ wide. The Doppler shift turns that width into a spread of line-of-sight speeds $\sigma$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:doppler_classical}). Thermal motion at $10^4\,\mathrm K$ gives only $\sqrt{k_BT/m_{\rm H}}=9\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:thermal_doppler_width}). The much larger observed width comes from clouds orbiting the hole. Let their masses, speeds, and distances from a hole of mass $M$ be $m_i$, $v_i$, and $r_i$ (P2).
\begin{align}
	\sum_i m_i\avg{v_i^2}
	&\eqby{1} \sum_i GMm_i\avg{\frac{1}{r_i}} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad 3\sigma^2
	&\eqby{2} GM\avg{\frac1r}
	\eqby{3} \frac{GM}{r} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The virial theorem for a bound system, $2\avg{K}+\avg{U}=0$, Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:virial_avg}, with $K=\sum_i\tfrac12m_iv_i^2$ and $U=-\sum_iGMm_i/r_i$. The hole is so heavy that it hardly moves, and the pull of the clouds on one another is small next to the pull of the hole.
	\item Divide by $\sum_im_i$. For motions with no favoured direction the mean square speed is three times its part along the line of sight, $\avg{v^2}=3\sigma^2$, as for galaxies in a cluster (Section~\ref{ga:sec:weighing}).
	\item The clouds lie at about one distance $r$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{M=\frac{3\sigma^2r}{G}.}
	\label{fp:eq:blr_mass}
\end{equation}
Only cloud speed and distance enter. The clouds weigh the hole as long as they are gravitationally bound to it.

\paragraph{The size from the echo.} These clouds lie only a few hundredths of a parsec from the hole, too close to resolve in a distant galaxy. A time delay measures their distance instead. When the disk brightens, the line-making clouds brighten after its light reaches them. Let the disk flash at $t=0$. A cloud at distance $r$ and angle $\theta$ from our line of sight sends us the delayed light.
\begin{align}
	\Delta t(\theta)
	&\eqby{1} \frac rc-\frac{r\cos\theta}{c}=\frac rc\,(1-\cos\theta) , \nonumber\\
	\avg{\Delta t}
	&\eqby{2} \frac rc\int_0^\pi(1-\cos\theta)\,\frac{2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta}{4\pi}
	\eqby{3} \frac rc .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The flash first travels $r$ out to the cloud. The cloud is $r\cos\theta$ nearer to us than the hole, so its light then gains $r\cos\theta/c$ on the direct light.
	\item Clouds spread evenly over a shell. A fraction $2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta/4\pi$ of them lies between $\theta$ and $\theta+d\theta$.
	\item $\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta=2$ and $\int_0^\pi\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta=0$.
\end{explain}
The delay ranges from $0$ for a cloud in front of the hole to $2r/c$ for one behind it. For clouds spread around the hole, the mean is $r/c$. Measuring the lag $\Delta t$ between continuum and line light is \textbf{reverberation mapping}. Put $r=c\,\Delta t$ into Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:blr_mass},
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{M=\frac{3\sigma^2c\,\Delta t}{G}.}
	\label{fp:eq:reverb_mass}
\end{equation}
For the example nucleus, a lag of $\Delta t=20$ days gives $r=c\,\Delta t=5.2\times10^{14}\,\mathrm m=0.017\,\mathrm{pc}$. With $\sigma=1500\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, the mass is $M=3\times(1.5\times10^6)^2\times5.2\times10^{14}/(6.67\times10^{-11})\,\mathrm{kg}=5.2\times10^{37}\,\mathrm{kg}=2.6\times10^7\,M_\odot$. A circular orbit at that radius has speed $\sqrt{GM/r}=2600\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}=\sqrt3\,\sigma$, as expected. Equation~\eqref{rad:eq:eddington} puts the luminosity at $3\%$ of the Eddington limit. The main uncertainty lies in the factor $3$. The lag measures a mean radius, while the virial relation uses a mean inverse radius. Flattened cloud orbits would also change the factor by order unity.

\paragraph{The dusty torus.} Farther from the hole, dust absorbs light from the nucleus. A grain intercepts flux $L/(4\pi r^2)$ across its cross-section and radiates from its whole surface, as a planet does in the balance before Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:teq}. Dust survives only where its temperature stays below about $1500\,\mathrm K$. The inner edge therefore lies where absorbed and radiated power balance at that temperature (P4).
\begin{align}
	T
	&\eqby{1} \left(\frac{L}{16\pi\sigma_{\rm SB}r^2}\right)^{1/4} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad r
	&\eqby{2} \sqrt{\frac{L}{16\pi\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4}} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The planet's balance with the grain in place of the planet and the albedo set to zero, since dust absorbs nearly all the ultraviolet and visible light that falls on it. The size of the grain drops out, as the planet's radius did.
	\item Raise to the fourth power and solve for $r$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{r_{\rm dust}=\sqrt{\frac{L}{16\pi\sigma_{\rm SB}\,(1500\,\mathrm K)^4}}\ \propto\ L^{1/2}.}
	\label{fp:eq:dust_radius}
\end{equation}
For $L=10^{37}\,\mathrm W$, the denominator is $16\pi\sigma_{\rm SB}(1500\,\mathrm K)^4=1.44\times10^7\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. It gives $r_{\rm dust}=8.3\times10^{14}\,\mathrm m=0.027\,\mathrm{pc}$, or $32$ light-days, outside the broad-line clouds at $20$ light-days. For a $10^{40}\,\mathrm W$ quasar, the blackbody estimate is $0.85\,\mathrm{pc}$. Small grains radiate infrared light less efficiently than blackbodies and run hotter, placing the real inner edge a few times farther out. Dust absorbs ultraviolet light and reradiates it in the infrared, near $2\,\mu\mathrm m$ at the hot inner edge (Eq.~\eqref{eq:wien_law}). A thick ring of this dust, the \textbf{torus}, hides the centre when seen from the side. The central flux also sets the ionisation of the broad-line clouds, so their distance should grow roughly as $L^{1/2}$. Measured lags show about that trend.

\paragraph{The narrow-line region.} Beyond the torus, gas in two illuminated cones extends hundreds of parsecs and makes lines only a few hundred $\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ wide. The hole's pull is weak at those distances. At $100\,\mathrm{pc}$, a $2.6\times10^7\,M_\odot$ hole gives an orbital speed of only $\sqrt{GM/r}=34\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Stars in the central galaxy and outflowing gas set the larger speeds. The gas is thin, with about $10^9$ to $10^{10}$ particles per cubic metre. Equation~\eqref{is:eq:level_balance} compares radiative decay with collisions. Below the critical density of Section~\ref{is:sec:21cm}, an excited ion can emit even through a slow, forbidden transition before another collision reaches it. Thus the narrow-line region shows forbidden lines, especially the line of doubly ionised oxygen at $500.7\,\mathrm{nm}$. Broad-line clouds are much denser, near $10^{15}$ to $10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. Collisions empty these levels before they radiate, leaving allowed lines. The narrow-line region sits outside the torus and remains visible from every angle.

\paragraph{Jets and lobes.} Some nuclei launch jets along the disk axis at nearly the speed of light. Their knots can show apparent speeds of several $c$ (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:superluminal}). Synchrotron light makes the jets bright in radio (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:sync_index}); the lobes they feed can reach hundreds of kiloparsecs beyond the galaxy. The two \textbf{Fanaroff--Riley} classes describe where a radio source is brightest. In class I, jets brighten near the core and spread into fading plumes. M84 (3C~272.1) in Virgo is one example, about $14\,\mathrm{kpc}$ across on the sky. In class II, the jets stay narrow and end in bright hot spots where they meet the surrounding gas. Class II sources are, on average, more powerful radio emitters.

\paragraph{One engine, many names.} Table~\ref{fp:tab:agn} shows how the view changes with the sight line. Through the torus opening, we see the hot disk and both broad and narrow lines. From the side, the torus hides the disk and broad-line clouds; narrow lines and dust infrared remain. When a jet points nearly toward us, beaming brightens and compresses its light in time (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:beaming}). Such a source is a blazar. Its synchrotron continuum can overwhelm the lines, while inverse Compton scattering supplies gamma rays (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:ic_boost}).

A nucleus with one can appear as a radio galaxy from the side, a radio-loud quasar from closer to its axis, or a blazar when the jet points toward us. Figure~\ref{fp:fig:agn_tree} sorts the names by asking three questions in turn. The first is whether the nucleus has a strong jet. Without one, the luminosity separates a Seyfert from a quasar, and for a Seyfert the sight line separates type 1 from type 2. With a jet, the angle between the jet and our sight line decides the name.

A Seyfert 2 should hide the broad-line clouds behind its torus. Some of their light can still escape through the opening and scatter toward us from free electrons above the torus. Scattering selects the component of the electron's motion across our line of sight (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:larmor}), making the scattered light linearly polarised. Broad lines appear in this polarised light in some Seyfert 2s, including NGC~1068, even though they are hidden in direct light.

\paragraph{Size from variability.} A source that changes within a day cannot be much larger than a light-day across (Section~\ref{fp:sec:accretion}). For a distant jet, cosmic expansion lengthens the observed interval, while motion toward us shortens it. Let a blob of radius $R$ move at speed $\beta c$ at angle $\theta$ in a galaxy at redshift $z$. A change takes at least $\Delta t'\approx R/c$ to cross the blob in its own frame (P6). Call the interval in the host galaxy $\Delta t_{\rm lab}$ and the interval at our telescope $\Delta t_{\rm obs}$.
\begin{align}
	\frac{\Delta t_{\rm obs}}{1+z}
	&\eqby{1} (1-\beta\cos\theta)\,\Delta t_{\rm lab} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \gamma_L(1-\beta\cos\theta)\,\Delta t'
	\eqby{3} \frac{\Delta t'}{\mathcal D}
	\ \relby{4}{\gtrsim}\ \frac{R}{c\,\mathcal D} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The expansion stretches every interval by $1+z$ (the chain before Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:redshift}). In the host's frame the later light starts $\beta c\,\Delta t_{\rm lab}\cos\theta$ nearer to us, as in the chain before Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:superluminal}.
	\item The time row of the boost \eqref{fp:eq:boost} for two events at the blob, $x'=0$.
	\item Define the \textbf{Doppler factor} $\mathcal D$ below.
	\item $\Delta t'\gtrsim R/c$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\mathcal D=\frac{1}{\gamma_L(1-\beta\cos\theta)},\qquad R\lesssim\frac{c\,\Delta t_{\rm obs}\,\mathcal D}{1+z}.}
	\label{fp:eq:var_size}
\end{equation}
The frequency transformation offers a check. Equation~\eqref{fp:eq:to_electron_frame} gives $\nu'=\gamma_L(1-\beta\cos\theta)\,\nu$, or $\nu=\mathcal D\,\nu'$. The same factor raises frequencies and shortens time intervals. For a source at rest, $\mathcal D=1$ and we recover the ordinary light-crossing bound. At $\cos\theta=\beta$, $\mathcal D=\gamma_L$; directly along the motion, $\mathcal D=\gamma_L(1+\beta)\approx2\gamma_L$. A blazar at $z=0.5$ varying within an hour, with $\mathcal D=10$, has $R\lesssim3.0\times10^8\times3600\times10/1.5\,\mathrm m=7.2\times10^{12}\,\mathrm m=48\,\mathrm{AU}$. Without beaming, the bound would be $4.8\,\mathrm{AU}$, smaller than the $20\,\mathrm{AU}$ Schwarzschild radius of a $10^9\,M_\odot$ hole. The rapid changes therefore support a beamed jet.

Fresh gas can change the accretion rate, and an unstable disk can flicker. In a jet, even a small change of speed or direction changes $\mathcal D$. Because the observed brightness depends on a high power of $\mathcal D$, this can make the jet vary strongly.

\paragraph{Pairs of black holes.} When galaxies merge, their central black holes can form a bound pair. Interactions with surrounding stars and gas drain orbital energy and draw the holes inward. At smaller separations, gravitational waves carry off energy (Section~\ref{cs:sec:gw}). Consider a circular pair of total mass $M=10^9\,M_\odot$ separated by $0.01\,\mathrm{pc}$.
\begin{align}
	f_{\rm GW}
	&\eqby{1} 2\times\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{GM}{(0.01\,\mathrm{pc})^3}}
	\eqby{2} \frac1\pi\sqrt{\frac{1.33\times10^{29}}{2.94\times10^{43}}}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \frac{6.72\times10^{-8}}{\pi}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}
	= 2.1\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{Hz} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The wave frequency $f_{\rm GW}$ is twice the orbital frequency, Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:f_gw} (the $f$ there, with a subscript here because $f$ is the photon occupation number of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:kompaneets}), and the orbital angular speed is $\sqrt{GM/(\text{separation})^3}$ by Kepler's third law, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kepler3}.
	\item $GM=6.674\times10^{-11}\times1.989\times10^{39}=1.33\times10^{29}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$, and $(0.01\,\mathrm{pc})^3=(3.086\times10^{14}\,\mathrm m)^3=2.94\times10^{43}\,\mathrm{m^3}$.
	\item $\sqrt{4.52\times10^{-15}}=6.72\times10^{-8}$.
\end{explain}
The orbit takes $3.0$ years, so the wave period is $1.5$ years and its frequency is $21$ nanohertz. For two equal holes, Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:t_merge} gives a remaining time of $(5/256)\,c^5(0.01\,\mathrm{pc})^4/(G^3m_1m_2M)$. Using $c^5=2.42\times10^{42}$, $(0.01\,\mathrm{pc})^4=9.07\times10^{57}$, $G^3=2.97\times10^{-31}$, and $m_1m_2M=1.97\times10^{117}$ in SI units gives $7.3\times10^{11}\,\mathrm s$, about $2\times10^4$ years. Many pairs can radiate at such periods at once, adding to a background of low-frequency gravitational waves. Pulsar timing arrays search for waves with periods of years (Section~\ref{cs:sec:gw_detectors}). Several arrays reported evidence for a background in 2023; its sources and their numbers remain under study.

\subsection{Problem 1: X-rays From a Cluster of Galaxies}
\label{fp:prob:cluster}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Ionised hydrogen fills a galaxy cluster out to radius $1\,\mathrm{Mpc}=3.09\times10^{22}\,\mathrm m$. Its density is $n_e=n_i=10^3\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, its temperature is $T=7\times10^7\,\mathrm K$, and $\bar g=1$. Find (a) the radiated power per unit volume, (b) the total X-ray luminosity, (c) the cooling time compared with the universe's age of $1.4\times10^{10}$ years, and (d) the photon energy where the spectrum falls to $1/e$ of its low-frequency value.

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The cooling rate $1.4\times10^{-40}\,n_en_i\sqrt T\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}$, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:brems_total}.
	\item The thermal energy $3nk_BT$ per unit volume, and the spectrum $\propto e^{-h\nu/k_BT}$ of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:brems_jnu}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Power per volume}. $1.4\times10^{-40}\times10^6\times\sqrt{7\times10^7}$.
	\[
	\boxed{1.2\times10^{-30}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Luminosity}. The volume is $\tfrac43\pi(3.09\times10^{22})^3=1.2\times10^{68}\,\mathrm{m^3}$.
	\[
	\boxed{L_X\simeq1.5\times10^{38}\,\mathrm W.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Cooling time}. $3nk_BT=2.9\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$, divided by the power per volume.
	\[
	\boxed{t_{\rm cool}\simeq2.4\times10^{18}\,\mathrm s\simeq8\times10^{10}\ \text{years}.}
	\]
	The cooling time exceeds the age of the universe by more than a factor of five. Most of the gas can still hold the heat it gained when the cluster formed.
	\item \textbf{Turn-down energy}. The spectrum falls by $e$ at $h\nu=k_BT$.
	\[
	\boxed{h\nu=6.0\,\mathrm{keV},\qquad \nu=1.5\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{Hz}.}
	\]
	The energy where the X-ray spectrum turns down measures the gas temperature. Together with Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:hse}, that temperature helps determine the cluster mass.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 2: Electrons in a Radio Lobe}
\label{fp:prob:lobe}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: A radio lobe has magnetic field $B=1\,\mathrm{nT}$. At $1.4\,\mathrm{GHz}$ its spectrum follows $j_\nu\propto\nu^{-0.75}$. Find (a) the Lorentz factor of electrons radiating at that frequency, using $\nu=\tfrac32\gamma_L^2\nu_g$, (b) their synchrotron lifetime, (c) the electron index $p$, and (d) the effect of scattering the microwave background, whose energy density is $u_{\rm rad}=4.2\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$.

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\nu_g=2.80\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{Hz}\,(B/1\,\mathrm T)$, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:gyro}.
	\item $t_{\rm sync}=3m_ec/(4\sigma_T\gamma_Lu_B)$ with $u_B=B^2/2\mu_0$, and the inverse Compton power has $u_{\rm rad}$ in place of $u_B$ (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:ic_power}).
	\item $\alpha=(p-1)/2$, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:sync_index}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Lorentz factor}. $\nu_g=28.0\,\mathrm{Hz}$, so $\gamma_L=\sqrt{1.4\times10^9/(1.5\times28.0)}$.
	\[
	\boxed{\gamma_L\simeq5.8\times10^3,\qquad \gamma_Lm_ec^2\simeq3\,\mathrm{GeV}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Lifetime}. $u_B=(10^{-9})^2/(2\times1.26\times10^{-6})=4.0\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$.
	\[
	\boxed{t_{\rm sync}=\frac{3\times9.11\times10^{-31}\times3.00\times10^8}{4\times6.65\times10^{-29}\times5.8\times10^3\times4.0\times10^{-13}}\,\mathrm s\simeq1.3\times10^{15}\,\mathrm s\simeq4\times10^7\ \text{years}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Electron index}. $0.75=(p-1)/2$.
	\[
	\boxed{p=2.5.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Scattering the background}. The two losses add, so the lifetime is $t_{\rm sync}$ divided by $1+u_{\rm rad}/u_B=1+0.105$.
	\[
	\boxed{t\simeq3.8\times10^7\ \text{years}.}
	\]
	Synchrotron cooling dominates at $1\,\mathrm{nT}$. Below $0.32\,\mathrm{nT}$, scattering the background would dominate and produce more X-rays relative to the lobe's radio light.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 3: Photons Kicked and Thrown}
\label{fp:prob:compton}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: (a) A $511\,\mathrm{keV}$ gamma ray scatters through $90^\circ$ from a stationary electron. Find its outgoing energy. (b) Electrons with $\gamma_L=10^4$ scatter microwave-background photons of mean energy $6.3\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{eV}$. Find the mean outgoing energy and the maximum for a photon that began at the mean energy. (c) Check the Thomson limit for part (b). (d) For the cluster of Problem~\ref{fp:prob:cluster}, find $y$ across $1\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ and the low-frequency temperature dip of the background, taking $T_{\rm CMB}=2.725\,\mathrm K$.

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $h\nu_1=h\nu/[1+(h\nu/m_ec^2)(1-\cos\psi)]$, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:compton_shift}.
	\item Mean gain $\tfrac43\gamma_L^2$ and largest gain $4\gamma_L^2$ (Eqs.~\eqref{fp:eq:ic_boost} and~\eqref{fp:eq:ic_power}), valid while $\gamma_Lh\nu\ll m_ec^2$.
	\item $y=(k_BT/m_ec^2)\,n_e\sigma_TL$ and $\Delta T/T=-2y$, Eqs.~\eqref{fp:eq:compton_y} and~\eqref{fp:eq:sz}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Compton shift}. $h\nu/m_ec^2=1$ and $1-\cos90^\circ=1$.
	\[
	\boxed{h\nu_1=\frac{511}{2}\,\mathrm{keV}\simeq256\,\mathrm{keV}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Inverse Compton}. $\tfrac43\times10^8\times6.3\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{eV}$, and $4\times10^8\times6.3\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{eV}$.
	\[
	\boxed{\avg{h\nu_1}\simeq85\,\mathrm{keV},\qquad h\nu_{1,\max}\simeq250\,\mathrm{keV}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Thomson limit}. In the electron's frame the photon has about $\gamma_Lh\nu=6.3\,\mathrm{eV}$, far below $511\,\mathrm{keV}$.
	\[
	\boxed{\text{Yes, by a factor of about }10^5.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Sunyaev–Zel'dovich dip}. $\tau=10^3\times6.65\times10^{-29}\times3.09\times10^{22}=2.1\times10^{-3}$, and $k_BT/m_ec^2=6.0/511=0.0118$.
	\[
	\boxed{y\simeq2.4\times10^{-5},\qquad \Delta T=-2yT_{\rm CMB}\simeq-130\,\mu\mathrm K.}
	\]
	The gas produces both the X-rays of Problem~\ref{fp:prob:cluster}, proportional to $n_e^2$, and this microwave dip, proportional to $n_e$. Together the signals constrain gas density and depth. The angular size then helps determine the cluster distance.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 4: Feeding Compact Stars}
\label{fp:prob:accretion}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: A neutron star has mass $1.4\,M_\odot$, radius $12\,\mathrm{km}$, and accretes gas. Find (a) its efficiency compared with hydrogen burning, (b) its luminosity at $\dot M=10^{-9}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ as a fraction of the Eddington limit, and (c) its temperature if the whole surface radiates as a blackbody. (d) A $10\,M_\odot$ black hole accretes at $10^{-8}\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ through a thin disk with $R_{\rm in}=6GM/c^2$. Find the temperature of its hottest ring.

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\eta=GM/(Rc^2)$ and $L=GM\dot M/R$, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:accretion_L}. Hydrogen burning gives $0.007$.
	\item $L_{\rm Edd}=1.26\times10^{31}\,\mathrm W\,(M/M_\odot)$, Eq.~\eqref{rad:eq:eddington}.
	\item $L=4\pi R^2\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4$, and the hottest ring of a disk at $0.488\,T_*$ (Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:disk_T}).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Efficiency}. $6.67\times10^{-11}\times2.79\times10^{30}/(1.2\times10^4\times9.0\times10^{16})$.
	\[
	\boxed{\eta\simeq0.17,\quad\text{about }25\text{ times hydrogen burning}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Luminosity}. $\dot M=6.3\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$, so $L=0.17\,\dot Mc^2$.
	\[
	\boxed{L\simeq9.8\times10^{29}\,\mathrm W\simeq2.5\times10^3\,L_\odot\simeq0.06\,L_{\rm Edd}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Surface temperature}. $T=[L/(4\pi R^2\sigma_{\rm SB})]^{1/4}$.
	\[
	\boxed{T\simeq1.0\times10^7\,\mathrm K,\qquad k_BT\simeq0.85\,\mathrm{keV}.}
	\]
	The light comes out in X-rays, which is how accreting neutron stars in binaries were found.
	\item \textbf{Disk of a black hole}. $R_{\rm in}=89\,\mathrm{km}$ and $T_*=(3GM\dot M/8\pi\sigma_{\rm SB}R_{\rm in}^3)^{1/4}=7.1\times10^6\,\mathrm K$.
	\[
	\boxed{T_{\max}=0.488\,T_*\simeq3.5\times10^6\,\mathrm K,\qquad k_BT\simeq0.3\,\mathrm{keV}.}
	\]
	Both the stellar black hole and the neutron star glow in soft X-rays at these inflow rates. The much larger disks around quasar black holes are cooler and glow in the ultraviolet.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 5: The Jet of M87}
\label{fp:prob:m87}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Knots in M87's jet appear to move across the sky at about $6c$. Find (a) the smallest Lorentz factor and true speed consistent with this apparent speed, (b) the largest angle the jet can make with our line of sight, and (c) the two viewing angles that give $\beta_{\rm app}=6$ if $\beta=0.99$.

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\beta_{\rm app}=\beta\sin\theta/(1-\beta\cos\theta)$, at most $\beta\gamma_L$, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:superluminal}.
	\item As $\beta\to1$, $\beta_{\rm app}\to\sin\theta/(1-\cos\theta)=\cot(\theta/2)$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Smallest Lorentz factor}. $\beta\gamma_L\ge6$, and $\beta^2\gamma_L^2=\gamma_L^2-1$, so $\gamma_L\ge\sqrt{37}$.
	\[
	\boxed{\gamma_L\ge6.1,\qquad \beta\ge6/\sqrt{37}=0.986.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Largest angle}. Even at $\beta\to1$, $\cot(\theta/2)\ge6$ requires $\theta\le2\,\mathrm{arccot}\,6$.
	\[
	\boxed{\theta\le19^\circ.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Angles for $\beta=0.99$}. Solving $0.99\sin\theta/(1-0.99\cos\theta)=6$,
	\[
	\boxed{\theta\simeq4.6^\circ\ \text{or}\ \theta\simeq14.4^\circ.}
	\]
	Below the peak $\beta\gamma_L=7.0$, the same apparent speed occurs at two angles, one on either side of $\cos\theta=\beta$. The motion alone limits $\gamma_L$ from below and $\theta$ from above. The brightness ratio of the approaching and receding jets helps narrow them further.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 6: Weighing a Black Hole by its Echo}
\label{fp:prob:echo}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: In a Seyfert 1 nucleus, broad hydrogen lines follow continuum changes $12$ days later. Their line-of-sight speed spread is $\sigma=2000\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and the nucleus shines at $4\times10^{37}\,\mathrm W$. Find (a) the cloud distance, (b) the black-hole mass, (c) the Eddington fraction, and (d) the inner edge of dust that survives below $1500\,\mathrm K$. Compare the dust edge with the cloud distance.

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The mean echo delay of a shell of clouds is $r/c$, and the virial theorem gives $M=3\sigma^2c\,\Delta t/G$, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:reverb_mass}.
	\item $L_{\rm Edd}=1.26\times10^{31}\,\mathrm W\,(M/M_\odot)$, Eq.~\eqref{rad:eq:eddington}.
	\item $r_{\rm dust}=\sqrt{L/(16\pi\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4)}$, Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:dust_radius}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Distance}. $r=c\,\Delta t=3.00\times10^8\times12\times86400\,\mathrm m$.
	\[
	\boxed{r\simeq3.1\times10^{14}\,\mathrm m\simeq0.010\,\mathrm{pc}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Mass}. $3\sigma^2=1.2\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$, so $M=1.2\times10^{13}\times3.1\times10^{14}/(6.67\times10^{-11})\,\mathrm{kg}=5.6\times10^{37}\,\mathrm{kg}$.
	\[
	\boxed{M\simeq2.8\times10^7\,M_\odot.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Eddington fraction}. $L_{\rm Edd}=1.26\times10^{31}\times2.8\times10^7\,\mathrm W=3.5\times10^{38}\,\mathrm W$.
	\[
	\boxed{L/L_{\rm Edd}\simeq0.11.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Inner edge of the dust}. With $16\pi\sigma_{\rm SB}(1500\,\mathrm K)^4=1.44\times10^7\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$,
	\[
	\boxed{r_{\rm dust}=\sqrt{\frac{4\times10^{37}}{1.44\times10^7}}\,\mathrm m\simeq1.7\times10^{15}\,\mathrm m\simeq64\ \text{light-days}.}
	\]
	The dust edge is about $5$ times farther out than the broad-line clouds. From the side, the torus can therefore hide those clouds while the more distant narrow-line gas remains visible.
\end{enumerate}
