Distant galaxies are moving away from us, and the farther they are, the faster they go. Run the film backwards and everything was once closer, denser and hotter. The whole history of the universe on the largest scales follows from the same principles that built a star. Energy conservation for a shell of matter and the gravity of a sphere (P1, P2) give the expansion. Counting states (P3) gives the light left over from the hot beginning. Balance of rates against the rate of expansion (P4, P6) decides when atoms formed and how much helium was made. Gravity working against expansion decides how galaxies grew from tiny ripples.

General relativity, which the reader knows, gives the same equations for a uniform universe. The Newtonian route is used here because it shows where each term comes from with the least machinery.

\section{Hubble's Law and the Scale Factor}
\label{un:sec:hubble}

Radial velocities from the Doppler shift were derived in Chapter~\ref{ch:astrometry} (Eq.~\eqref{eq:doppler_classical}). Measured for distant galaxies, these velocities are almost all away from us. A single velocity for each galaxy says nothing about what the whole universe is doing.

Think of raisins in a rising loaf of bread. As the dough swells, every raisin moves away from every other raisin. A raisin twice as far away moves away twice as fast, and every raisin sees the same pattern, so none of them is the centre. The universe expands like the dough. All distances between galaxies grow by the same factor at the same time. A uniform stretching makes velocity proportional to distance, which is Hubble's law, and it stretches light too, which is the cosmological redshift.

In numbers, today's expansion rate is $H_0=67.4\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$. A galaxy $100\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ away recedes at $6740\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The time $1/H_0$ is $\SixteenHubbleTime$ billion years, the age the universe would have if it had always expanded at today's speed. The picture is a grid drawn on a balloon. Inflating the balloon carries every grid point away from every other, while each point keeps its place on the grid. The same uniform stretching appears in the dough, in an expanding gas and in any self-similar flow. It gives Hubble's law and the redshift in terms of one function of time, the scale factor.

\paragraph{The scale factor.} Give every galaxy fixed coordinates $\vec x$ on the stretching grid, its \textbf{comoving} position. The true distance between two galaxies is then $a(t)$ times their comoving separation, where $a(t)$ is the \textbf{scale factor}, set to $1$ today. A uniform universe has only this one function of time.

\paragraph{Hubble's law.} For a galaxy at true distance $d=a\,x$,
\begin{align}
	\dv{d}{t}
	&\eqby{1} \dv{a}{t}\,x \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \frac{\dot a}{a}\,d .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The comoving separation $x$ does not change.
	\item $x=d/a$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{v=H\,d,\qquad H(t)\equiv\frac{\dot a}{a}.}
	\label{un:eq:hubble}
\end{equation}
The velocity is proportional to the distance, with the same $H$ for every galaxy. Every observer finds the same law, so it picks out no centre.

\paragraph{Redshift as stretching.} Light travels on the grid at $c$ in true distance, so in comoving distance it covers $c\,dt/a(t)$ in a time $dt$. Two crests leave a galaxy a time $\Delta t_{\rm e}$ apart, at scale factor $a_{\rm e}$, and arrive a time $\Delta t_0$ apart today. Both crests cover the same comoving distance, so
\begin{align}
	\int_{t_{\rm e}}^{t_0}\frac{c\,dt}{a}
	&= \int_{t_{\rm e}+\Delta t_{\rm e}}^{t_0+\Delta t_0}\frac{c\,dt}{a} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \frac{\Delta t_{\rm e}}{a_{\rm e}}
	&\eqby{1} \frac{\Delta t_0}{a_0} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Subtract the common part of the two integrals. What is left are two short pieces, of length $\Delta t_{\rm e}$ at the start and $\Delta t_0$ at the end, during which $a$ hardly changes.
\end{explain}
The wavelength is $c$ times the time between crests, so it grows in proportion to the scale factor,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{1+z\equiv\frac{\lambda_{\rm obs}}{\lambda_{\rm emit}}=\frac{a_0}{a_{\rm e}}=\frac{1}{a_{\rm e}}.}
	\label{un:eq:redshift}
\end{equation}
The redshift measures how much the universe has grown since the light left, not a velocity. For small distances it reduces to the Doppler form $z\approx v/c$ with $v=Hd$.

\section{The Friedmann Equations}
\label{un:sec:friedmann}

Hubble's law describes the expansion today. Chapter~\ref{ch:twobody} and Section~\ref{sg:sec:freefall} followed a single body or a single shell moving under gravity. The expansion is not free. Every galaxy pulls on every other, so gravity should slow the expansion, and how $a(t)$ changes depends on what the universe contains.

Throw a ball straight up. If it goes fast enough it escapes the Earth, and if not it falls back. The answer depends only on its energy, kinetic against the depth of the gravitational well. Take any sphere in a uniform universe. By P2, a shell on its surface feels only the mass inside, so it moves exactly like the thrown ball, with energy conserved (P1). Writing that energy in terms of the scale factor gives the Friedmann equation, the equation of motion of the whole universe.

The ball in numbers. The escape speed from the Earth is $11.2\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. For the universe the same balance defines a critical density, $3H_0^2/(8\pi G)=8.5\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, about five hydrogen atoms per cubic metre. The picture is a sphere carved out of the universe, its surface shell flying outward and slowing under the pull of what it contains. The same equation comes out of general relativity for any uniform universe, with the energy constant reinterpreted as the curvature of space. It gives the expansion rate, how each kind of content dilutes, whether the expansion speeds up, and the age.

\paragraph{The energy of a shell.} Take a sphere of comoving radius $x$, true radius $r=a\,x$ and density $\rho$. The mass inside, $M=\tfrac43\pi\rho a^3x^3$, stays constant as the shell moves with the expansion. Energy conservation per unit mass of the shell (P1, with P2 for the gravity) reads
\begin{align}
	\frac12\dot r^2-\frac{GM}{r}
	&= \text{constant} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \frac12\dot a^2x^2-\frac43\pi G\rho\,a^2x^2
	&\eqby{1} \text{constant} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2
	&\eqby{2} \frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\rho-\frac{kc^2}{a^2} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $r=ax$ and $M=\tfrac43\pi\rho a^3x^3$.
	\item Divide by $\tfrac12a^2x^2$, and write the constant as $-kc^2/(2x^2)$, which defines $k$. It is the same for every shell because the universe is uniform.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{H^2=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\rho-\frac{kc^2}{a^2}.}
	\label{un:eq:friedmann}
\end{equation}
This is the \textbf{Friedmann equation}. General relativity gives exactly this form, with $\rho$ the total energy density divided by $c^2$ and $k$ the curvature of space. The universe is observed to be flat to good accuracy, so from here on $k=0$, and then the density is fixed by the expansion rate,
\begin{equation}
	\rho_{\rm crit}=\frac{3H^2}{8\pi G},\qquad \Omega_i\equiv\frac{\rho_{i,0}}{\rho_{\rm crit,0}} .
	\label{un:eq:critical}
\end{equation}
Each content $i$ (matter, radiation, the cosmological constant) is described by its share $\Omega_i$ of the critical density today.

\paragraph{How each content dilutes.} The Friedmann equation needs $\rho(a)$. Take a comoving volume $V\propto a^3$. Its energy $\rho c^2V$ changes only by the work done by its pressure as it expands (P1, the first law with no heat flowing in a uniform universe),
\begin{align}
	d(\rho c^2a^3)
	&= -P\,d(a^3) \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \dot\rho+3\frac{\dot a}{a}\left(\rho+\frac{P}{c^2}\right)
	&\eqby{1} 0 .
	\label{un:eq:fluid}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Divide by $c^2a^3\,dt$ and use $d(a^3)=3a^2\,da$.
\end{explain}
With $P=w\rho c^2$ for a fixed $w$, this integrates to $\rho\propto a^{-3(1+w)}$. Three cases matter.
\begin{itemize}
	\item Matter, $w=0$ (pressure negligible), $\rho\propto a^{-3}$. The particles are only spread over a larger volume.
	\item Radiation, $w=\tfrac13$ (Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:P_u}), $\rho\propto a^{-4}$. Each photon is also redshifted, losing one more power of $a$.
	\item A cosmological constant, $w=-1$, $\rho=$ constant. Its energy density does not dilute at all.
\end{itemize}
So for a flat universe
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{H^2=H_0^2\left(\frac{\Omega_m}{a^3}+\frac{\Omega_r}{a^4}+\Omega_\Lambda\right),\qquad \Omega_m+\Omega_r+\Omega_\Lambda=1.}
	\label{un:eq:friedmann_omegas}
\end{equation}
Radiation rules when $a$ is small, then matter, then the cosmological constant. Matter and radiation were equal at $1+z=\Omega_m/\Omega_r$, $z\approx\SixteenZeqMR$, when the universe was about $\SixteenTeqMRyr$ years old.

\paragraph{Does the expansion slow down?} Differentiate Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:friedmann} (with $k=0$) in time and use the fluid equation,
\begin{align}
	2\frac{\dot a}{a}\left(\frac{\ddot a}{a}-\frac{\dot a^2}{a^2}\right)
	&\eqby{1} \frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\dot\rho \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} -8\pi G\,\frac{\dot a}{a}\left(\rho+\frac{P}{c^2}\right) \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \frac{\ddot a}{a}
	&\eqby{3} -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho+\frac{3P}{c^2}\right).
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $\dv*{}{t}(\dot a/a)=\ddot a/a-\dot a^2/a^2$.
	\item The fluid equation \eqref{un:eq:fluid}.
	\item Divide by $2\dot a/a$ and replace $\dot a^2/a^2$ by $(8\pi G/3)\rho$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\frac{\ddot a}{a}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho+\frac{3P}{c^2}\right).}
	\label{un:eq:acceleration}
\end{equation}
Ordinary matter and radiation slow the expansion. Pressure adds to the pull, as it did in the TOV equation (Section~\ref{cs:sec:relativistic}). A content with $w<-\tfrac13$ makes $\rho+3P/c^2<0$ and speeds the expansion up. A cosmological constant, $w=-1$, does.

\paragraph{Single-content universes.} With one content at a time, Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:friedmann_omegas} integrates directly. For matter only, $\dot a=H_0a^{-1/2}$, so $a^{1/2}da=H_0\,dt$ and
\begin{equation}
	a=\left(\tfrac32H_0t\right)^{2/3},\qquad t_0=\frac{2}{3H_0}=\SixteenAgeMatterOnly\ \text{Gyr}.
\end{equation}
For radiation only, $a=(2H_0t)^{1/2}$ and $t_0=1/(2H_0)$. For a cosmological constant only, $a=e^{H_0(t-t_0)}$, with no beginning at all. Expansion that is always slowing has an age shorter than $1/H_0$.

\paragraph{Our universe.} For the Planck values $H_0=67.4\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$, $\Omega_m=0.315$, $\Omega_r=9.1\times10^{-5}$ and $\Omega_\Lambda=\SixteenOmegaL$, the age is found by integrating $dt=da/(aH)$ from $a=0$ to $1$ on the cluster,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{t_0=\int_0^1\frac{da}{a\,H(a)}=\SixteenAge\ \text{Gyr}.}
	\label{un:eq:age}
\end{equation}
This is longer than the matter-only age, because the cosmological constant has sped up the expansion lately, and close to $1/H_0$ by coincidence. Figure~\ref{un:fig:scale_factor} compares the four histories. The oldest stars, about $13$ billion years old, fit inside this age, as they must.

\section{The Microwave Background and Recombination}
\label{un:sec:cmb}

Light in thermal equilibrium has the Planck spectrum (Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:planck}), and the universe was once dense and hot. Today the universe is cold, thin and transparent. If its early light is still around, it has been stretched by expansion for billions of years, and it is not obvious that it should still look thermal, or when it last met matter.

Think of a guitar string in a box that slowly grows. Each note the string can play gets lower as the box grows, but the set of notes keeps the same pattern, only shifted. Light in an expanding universe is like that. Every mode is stretched by the same factor. The early universe was full of light in equilibrium with matter. As it expanded, every photon's wavelength grew with $a$, the number of photons in each mode stayed the same (P1), and the Planck spectrum stayed a Planck spectrum at a temperature falling as $1/a$ (P3). When the universe cooled enough for electrons to join protons, the light was set free. The temperature at which that happened is set by counting states (P3), detailed balance (P4) and one ratio, the enormous number of photons per atom (P6).

In numbers, the light is seen today at $2.7255\,\mathrm{K}$, $411$ photons per cubic centimetre. It was released at about $3000\,\mathrm K$, far below the $1.58\times10^5\,\mathrm K$ at which a typical photon carries hydrogen's ionisation energy. The picture is a fog that suddenly clears. Before, light scatters off free electrons at every step. After, the electrons are locked in atoms and the light flies straight, carrying a snapshot of the moment the fog lifted. The same counting gives the ionisation of gas in stellar atmospheres and nebulae, and the same photon excess sets when the first nuclei formed (Section~\ref{un:sec:bbn}). It gives the temperature law, the photon density and the Saha equation.

\paragraph{A blackbody stays a blackbody.} Between interactions, each photon's momentum falls as $1/a$, by Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:redshift}. The number of photons in a given set of modes does not change, and the modes themselves are carried along with $p\propto1/a$. The occupation of a mode, $f=1/(e^{pc/k_BT}-1)$ in equilibrium (Section~\ref{pr:sec:p3}), is therefore unchanged if $pc/k_BT$ stays the same for each mode,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{T\propto\frac1a,\qquad T=T_0(1+z).}
	\label{un:eq:T_scaling}
\end{equation}
The spectrum keeps the Planck form exactly, at a temperature falling as the universe grows. It is measured today at $T_0=2.7255\,\mathrm K$, the most perfect blackbody known. Hot gas in galaxy clusters scatters a small part of it to higher energies, so at low frequencies clusters appear as faint cool spots on the microwave sky, the Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect of Eq.~\eqref{fp:eq:sz}.

\paragraph{How many photons.} With Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:dn}, two polarisations and $x=pc/k_BT$,
\begin{align}
	n_\gamma
	&\eqby{1} \int_0^\infty\frac{2}{h^3}\,\frac{4\pi p^2\,dp}{e^{pc/k_BT}-1} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} 8\pi\left(\frac{k_BT}{hc}\right)^3\int_0^\infty\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x-1} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} 16\pi\zeta(3)\left(\frac{k_BT}{hc}\right)^3 .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Counting states, Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:dn}, with the Planck occupation.
	\item The substitution $x=pc/k_BT$.
	\item $\int_0^\infty x^2dx/(e^x-1)=2\zeta(3)=2.404$, where $\zeta(3)=1.202$.
\end{explain}
At $T_0=2.7255\,\mathrm K$ this gives $n_\gamma=411\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. The energy density is $a_{\rm rad}T_0^4$ (Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:photon_gas}, with the radiation constant written $a_{\rm rad}$, since $a$ is now the scale factor), which is a share $\Omega_\gamma=5.4\times10^{-5}$ of the critical density. Neutrinos add $68\%$ more, giving the $\Omega_r=9.1\times10^{-5}$ used above. Ordinary matter, $\Omega_b=0.049$, has $\eta=n_b/n_\gamma=6.1\times10^{-10}$ baryons per photon. This tiny ratio decides the next result.

\paragraph{The Saha equation.} Hydrogen is ionised and re-formed by $\mathrm p+\mathrm e\rightleftharpoons\mathrm H+\gamma$. In equilibrium, each process is balanced by its reverse (P4), so the populations take their equilibrium ratio from counting states (P3). A bound atom in its ground state has energy $-B$, with $B=13.6\,\mathrm{eV}$. Ionising it frees an electron, which can then take any of the translational states available to it. An electron sharing space with the other free electrons has a volume $1/n_e$ to itself, and by Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:states} the number of its momentum states up to a thermal momentum is about $(2\pi m_ek_BT/h^2)^{3/2}/n_e$ (the Maxwell distribution integrated over $d^3p/h^3$). The Boltzmann factor for the energy $B$ completes the ratio (the spin states of electron, proton and atom cancel, $2\times2/4=1$),
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\frac{n_pn_e}{n_{\rm H}}=\left(\frac{2\pi m_ek_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}e^{-B/k_BT}.}
	\label{un:eq:saha}
\end{equation}
This is the \textbf{Saha equation}. With the ionised fraction $x=n_e/n_{\rm H,tot}$ and $n_p=n_e$, it becomes
\[
\frac{x^2}{1-x}=\frac{1}{n_{\rm H,tot}}\left(\frac{2\pi m_ek_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}e^{-B/k_BT},\qquad n_{\rm H,tot}=(1-Y)\,\eta\,n_\gamma ,
\]
with $Y=0.245$ the helium mass fraction (Section~\ref{un:sec:bbn}).

\paragraph{Why so cold?} The bracket grows as $T^{3/2}$, while $n_{\rm H,tot}=(1-Y)\eta n_\gamma$ grows as $T^3$, so the right side is roughly
\[
\frac{1}{\eta}\left(\frac{m_ec^2}{k_BT}\right)^{3/2}e^{-B/k_BT}\times\text{(a number of order one)}.
\]
The factor $1/\eta\approx1.6\times10^9$ is huge. Each atom that forms is surrounded by over a billion photons, and even far out on their Planck tail enough of them carry $13.6\,\mathrm{eV}$ to break it again. Hydrogen stays ionised until $e^{-B/k_BT}$ has fallen far enough to beat $10^9$ (P6). The script solves for $x$ against $z$ (Figure~\ref{un:fig:saha}). The fraction falls through one half at $z=\SixteenZhalf$, $T=\SixteenThalf\,\mathrm K$, where $B/k_BT=\SixteenBoverkT$, against the $\SixteenTionise\,\mathrm K$ at which $k_BT=B$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch16_saha.pdf}
	\caption{The ionised fraction of hydrogen against redshift from the Saha equation. The dashed lines mark $x=\tfrac12$. The dotted line marks $z=1090$.}
	\label{un:fig:saha}
\end{figure}

Real recombination lags a little behind Saha's equilibrium. Each atom that forms in its ground state emits a photon that can ionise its neighbour, so the last stages are slowed and the light decouples at $z\approx1090$, $T\approx3000\,\mathrm K$, where the photons' mean free path grows past the size of the universe (P6, as in Section~\ref{rad:sec:transfer}). That moment, about $380\,000$ years after the start, is what the microwave background shows.

\section{Freeze-Out and the First Three Minutes}
\label{un:sec:bbn}

Recombination happened when a reaction balance shifted as the universe cooled (Section~\ref{un:sec:cmb}). Earlier, at temperatures of billions of kelvin, the same kind of balance governed the nuclei. One more effect then matters. A reaction can only keep its balance if it runs faster than the universe changes.

Think of a party where guests keep swapping partners. While the room is crowded, swaps are frequent and partners stay mixed. As guests leave and the room empties, encounters become rare, and at some point everyone is stuck with whoever they last danced with. The mix freezes in at the moment encounters become rarer than the room changes. In the early universe, neutrons and protons turned into each other through weak reactions with electrons and neutrinos. While those reactions ran faster than the expansion (P4), the neutron-to-proton ratio followed its equilibrium value from the Boltzmann factor (P3). When the expansion overtook them (P6), the ratio froze, and almost all the neutrons that remained ended up in helium.

In numbers, the ratio froze at about one neutron for five protons. Some neutrons decayed before nuclei could form, leaving one for seven, which makes a quarter of the mass into helium. The picture is two curves crossing, the reaction rate falling steeply as the universe cools and the expansion rate falling gently, with the freeze-out where they cross. The same freeze-out decides how much dark matter is left over if it is a particle, when neutrinos stopped interacting, and the amounts of deuterium and lithium. It gives the time–temperature relation, the freeze-out ratio, the helium fraction and the deuterium delay.

\paragraph{Time and temperature.} Early on, radiation dominates and $\rho\propto a^{-4}$, so $\dot a/a\propto a^{-2}$ gives $a\propto t^{1/2}$ and $H=1/(2t)$. The Friedmann equation then ties the time to the temperature. With $\rho c^2=g\,\tfrac12a_{\rm rad}T^4$, where $g=10.75$ counts photons, electrons, positrons and three kinds of neutrino at a few MeV (fermions counting $\tfrac78$ of a boson),
\begin{align}
	\frac{1}{4t^2}
	&\eqby{1} \frac{8\pi G}{3}\,\frac{g\,a_{\rm rad}T^4}{2c^2} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad t
	&= \left(\frac{3c^2}{16\pi G\,g\,a_{\rm rad}}\right)^{1/2}\frac{1}{T^2}
	\eqby{2} 0.74\,\mathrm s\left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{MeV}}{k_BT}\right)^2 .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $H=1/(2t)$ in the Friedmann equation, with each species' share of the radiation energy density.
	\item Numbers for $g=10.75$.
\end{explain}
A second after the beginning the temperature was about $10^{10}\,\mathrm K$, and three minutes after it about $10^9\,\mathrm K$.

\paragraph{The freeze-out condition.} The weak reactions $\mathrm n+\nu\rightleftharpoons\mathrm p+\mathrm e$ and $\mathrm n+\mathrm e^+\rightleftharpoons\mathrm p+\bar\nu$ have a cross-section that grows as the square of the energy, so their rate per nucleon grows as $T^5$ (density $\propto T^3$, speed $c$, cross-section $\propto T^2$). The expansion rate grows only as $T^2$. The ratio of the two falls as $T^3$, and the reactions stop keeping up when
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\Gamma\approx H.}
	\label{un:eq:freeze}
\end{equation}
With the measured strength of the weak interaction this happens at $k_BT_f\approx0.8\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

\paragraph{Neutrons and helium.} Before freeze-out the reactions in each direction balance (P4), and the Boltzmann factor of Eq.~\eqref{pr:eq:detailed_balance} gives
\[
\frac np=e^{-Q/k_BT},\qquad Q=(m_n-m_p)c^2=1.293\,\mathrm{MeV}.
\] At freeze-out this is $e^{-1.293/0.8}=0.20$. Free neutrons then decay, with a mean life of $879\,\mathrm s$. Almost every neutron left when nuclei finally form, at about $t\approx200\,\mathrm s$, ends up in helium-4, which holds two neutrons and two protons. With $n/p$ the ratio at that moment, the mass fraction in helium is
\begin{align}
	Y
	&\eqby{1} \frac{4\,(n/2)}{n+p}
	= \frac{2\,(n/p)}{1+n/p}
	\eqby{2} \frac{2\times\tfrac17}{1+\tfrac17}=0.25 .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Each helium nucleus uses two neutrons and has four times the nucleon mass, so the helium mass is $4\times n/2$ in units of the nucleon mass, out of a total $n+p$.
	\item After freeze-out and decay, $n/p\approx\tfrac17$.
\end{explain}
The measured primordial helium fraction is $0.245$. A crude model with freeze-out at $0.8\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and nuclei forming at $180\,\mathrm s$ gives $n/p=0.16$ and $Y=0.28$. The full calculation, which follows the reaction rates through the freeze-out instead of cutting them off, gives $n/p\approx\tfrac17$ and agrees with the measurement.

\paragraph{The deuterium bottleneck.} Helium forms through deuterium, bound by only $2.22\,\mathrm{MeV}$. As with hydrogen in Section~\ref{un:sec:cmb}, a billion photons per nucleon can break every deuteron as long as enough photons on the Planck tail carry $2.22\,\mathrm{MeV}$. Deuterium survives only once $e^{-B_D/k_BT}$ has fallen below about $\eta$,
\begin{equation}
	k_BT\approx\frac{B_D}{\ln(1/\eta)}=\frac{2.22\,\mathrm{MeV}}{21.2}\approx0.1\,\mathrm{MeV},
\end{equation}
and the full Saha-type balance, which adds the slowly varying factors, lowers this to $0.07\,\mathrm{MeV}$, about $8\times10^8\,\mathrm K$. By the time-temperature relation that is about three minutes, the time the neutrons had to decay. The same photon excess that delayed recombination delayed the first nuclei. The tunnelling that makes fusion possible at these energies is that of Section~\ref{st:sec:eos}.

\section{The Growth of Structure}
\label{un:sec:growth}

The Jeans criterion (Section~\ref{sg:sec:jeans}) showed that in a static gas, large enough overdensities grow exponentially. The universe is not static. Expansion pulls every overdense region apart even as its own gravity pulls it together, and the microwave background shows that at $z\approx1090$ the density varied by only one part in $10^5$. Something must turn such tiny ripples into galaxies.

Think of trying to roll a snowball while someone keeps stretching the field it rolls on. It still grows, but much more slowly than on a fixed field. An overdense sphere in an expanding universe is a small closed universe inside a larger one. It expands a little more slowly than its surroundings, so its density contrast grows, by P1 and P2 for its surface shell. In the matter era it grows only in proportion to $a$, not exponentially.

In numbers, from $z=1090$ to today $a$ grows by $1091$, so a contrast of $10^{-5}$ would grow only to about $10^{-2}$, far short of the value near one at which galaxies form. The picture is two spheres expanding side by side, the denser one falling slowly behind. The same equation describes the growth of every structure in the universe, from galaxy clusters to the cosmic web, and its numbers are part of the case for dark matter. It gives the growth equation, its solution in the matter era, and why ordinary matter alone cannot explain galaxies.

\paragraph{A slightly overdense sphere.} Take a sphere whose density is $\bar\rho(1+\delta)$, with $\delta\ll1$ and $\bar\rho$ the mean density. Its mass is the mean-universe mass of its comoving region, so its surface sits at $r=a\,x(1-\delta/3)$, slightly inside where it would be in the mean universe, since $r^3(1+\delta)$ must equal $a^3x^3$. By P2 the surface obeys $\ddot r=-GM/r^2$, with $M=\tfrac43\pi\bar\rho a^3x^3$. Expanding to first order in $\delta$,
\begin{align}
	\ddot r
	&\eqby{1} \ddot a\,x\left(1-\tfrac{\delta}{3}\right)-\tfrac23\dot a\,x\,\dot\delta-\tfrac13a\,x\,\ddot\delta , \nonumber\\
	-\frac{GM}{r^2}
	&\eqby{2} -\frac43\pi G\bar\rho\,a\,x\left(1+\tfrac{2\delta}{3}\right).
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Differentiate $r=ax(1-\delta/3)$ twice.
	\item $(1-\delta/3)^{-2}\approx1+2\delta/3$.
\end{explain}
At zeroth order the two sides give $\ddot a=-\tfrac43\pi G\bar\rho\,a$, the mean universe (Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:acceleration} for matter). At first order, after multiplying by $-3/(ax)$ and using that result,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\ddot\delta+2H\dot\delta=4\pi G\bar\rho\,\delta .}
	\label{un:eq:growth}
\end{equation}
Without the term $2H\dot\delta$ this is the Jeans growth of Section~\ref{sg:sec:jeans} with no pressure, $\delta\propto e^{\sqrt{4\pi G\bar\rho}\,t}$. The expansion adds a drag.

\paragraph{Growth in the matter era.} With matter only, $a\propto t^{2/3}$, $H=2/(3t)$ and $4\pi G\bar\rho=\tfrac32H^2=2/(3t^2)$. Trying $\delta\propto t^s$ in Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:growth},
\begin{align}
	s(s-1)+\tfrac43s
	&\eqby{1} \tfrac23 \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad s
	&= \tfrac23\quad\text{or}\quad s=-1 .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Each term of Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:growth} is a number times $t^{s-2}$.
\end{explain}
The growing solution is $\delta\propto t^{2/3}\propto a$. Density contrasts grow only as fast as the universe expands.

\paragraph{Why ordinary matter is not enough.} Before recombination, ordinary matter is tied to the light by Thomson scattering. Its pressure is that of the radiation, its Jeans length is larger than any region in causal contact, and by Section~\ref{sg:sec:jeans} it oscillates instead of growing. Only after $z\approx1090$ can it grow, and then by at most a factor $1091$ by today, from the measured $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-2}$. Matter that does not feel the light, dark matter, can grow from the equality of matter and radiation at $z\approx\SixteenZeqMR$, and by recombination its contrast is already large. Ordinary matter then falls into the dark matter's wells. Galaxies exist today because most of the matter does not interact with light, the same conclusion that rotation curves reach in Chapter~\ref{ch:galaxies}.

\paragraph{The cosmic web.} Once a region's contrast nears one, it stops expanding, turns around and collapses (the free fall of Section~\ref{sg:sec:freefall}). What forms depends on the scale, and the result is the hierarchy of Section~\ref{ga:sec:hierarchy} (Figure~\ref{ga:fig:hierarchy}). Galaxies gather into groups and clusters, held together by their own gravity. These lie in superclusters, which are not, and the superclusters lie along filaments and walls around nearly empty voids. Maps of galaxy redshifts show this cosmic web (Figure~\ref{ga:fig:sdss}), and computer simulations of dark matter growing by Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:growth} reproduce it. Two facts explain its shape. The small scales grow first, because the dark-matter ripples are largest on small scales, so small halos form first and merge into larger ones over time. And a slightly uneven region collapses fastest along its shortest axis, so it becomes a sheet first, then a filament where sheets meet, then a knot, a cluster, where filaments meet. On scales above a few hundred megaparsecs the web averages out and the universe is uniform, as the Friedmann equations assume.

\section{The Lyman-\texorpdfstring{$\alpha$}{alpha} Forest}
\label{un:sec:forest}

Most of the ordinary matter in the universe lies between the galaxies, in thin gas that gives off almost no light of its own. Gas that does not shine can still be seen if something bright lies behind it. A distant quasar is such a lamp, and the hydrogen in front of it absorbs the Lyman~$\alpha$ line, the jump of its electron from $n=1$ to $n=2$ at $\lambda_\alpha=121.567\,\mathrm{nm}$ (Section~\ref{sec:ism_lines}). But the gas lies at many distances, each with its own redshift, and it is not obvious where in the quasar's spectrum each part of it will show.

Think of a strip of elastic with a ruler printed on it, stretched steadily while you watch. A friend walks along it and cuts a notch at the mark $121.6$ at several places along the way. Every notch is cut at the same mark, but the stretching goes on after each cut, so the notches cut first have been stretched the most by the time you look. Each cloud absorbs only at $121.6\,\mathrm{nm}$ in its own frame. The light reaching it left the quasar bluer than that and has been stretched on the way, so the cloud removes the photons that happen to be passing through $121.6\,\mathrm{nm}$ as they cross it. After the cloud, those missing photons keep being stretched along with the rest. Today the gap sits at $\lambda_\alpha(1+z_{\rm c})$, set by the redshift $z_{\rm c}$ of the cloud, not of the quasar. Every cloud cuts its own dip, at its own place.

In numbers, a quasar at $z=\SixteenLyaZq$ shows its own Lyman~$\alpha$ emission at $\SixteenLyaQSO\,\mathrm{nm}$. Clouds at $z=2.55$, $2.75$ and $2.90$ in front of it absorb at $\SixteenLyaA$, $\SixteenLyaB$ and $\SixteenLyaC\,\mathrm{nm}$. A real line of sight crosses hundreds of clouds, $\SixteenLyaLines$ in the computed example, and their dips crowd together into a ``forest''. The picture is Figure~\ref{un:fig:forest_three}, three clouds that each absorb at the same wavelength in their own frame and leave their dips at three different places in the spectrum we see.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch16_lya_three.pdf}
	\caption{Top, a quasar at $z=\SixteenLyaZq$ and three hydrogen clouds between it and us, each with the spectrum it sees in its own frame, absorbed at $121.6\,\mathrm{nm}$. Bottom, the spectrum of the quasar that reaches us. Each cloud's dip lies at $121.6\,(1+z)\,\mathrm{nm}$ for that cloud's redshift $z$, and the quasar's own Lyman~$\alpha$ emission at $121.6\,(1+z_{\rm q})\,\mathrm{nm}$.}
	\label{un:fig:forest_three}
\end{figure}

The same absorption maps the gas between the galaxies, measures how the dark matter clumps on small scales, tests whether dark matter is cold and how heavy neutrinos are, and shows when the universe was reionised. It gives where each dip falls, how much a neutral universe would absorb, and what the dips trace.

\paragraph{Where each dip falls.} A photon leaves the quasar at scale factor $a_{\rm q}$ with wavelength $\lambda_{\rm e}$. By Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:redshift} its wavelength grows in proportion to the scale factor, so when it crosses a cloud at scale factor $a_{\rm c}$ its wavelength is $\lambda_{\rm e}a_{\rm c}/a_{\rm q}$. The cloud absorbs it only if that equals $\lambda_\alpha$. A photon that survives would arrive today, $a=1$, with $\lambda_{\rm e}/a_{\rm q}$. So the photons removed by the cloud are those that would have arrived at
\begin{align}
	\lambda_{\rm obs}
	&\eqby{1} \frac{\lambda_{\rm e}}{a_{\rm q}}
	\eqby{2} \frac{\lambda_\alpha}{a_{\rm c}}
	\eqby{3} \lambda_\alpha(1+z_{\rm c}) .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Wavelength grows as $a$ from $a_{\rm q}$ to $1$.
	\item Absorption at the cloud, $\lambda_{\rm e}=\lambda_\alpha a_{\rm q}/a_{\rm c}$.
	\item $1+z_{\rm c}=1/a_{\rm c}$, Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:redshift}.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\lambda_{\rm obs}=\lambda_\alpha\,(1+z_{\rm c}).}
	\label{un:eq:forest_lambda}
\end{equation}
The quasar's redshift has dropped out. Three consequences follow.
\begin{itemize}
	\item Every cloud lies between us and the quasar, $z_{\rm c}<z_{\rm q}$, so every dip lies blueward of the quasar's own Lyman~$\alpha$ emission at $\lambda_\alpha(1+z_{\rm q})$. Light redward of it was redder than $\lambda_\alpha$ when it left and only got redder, so no cloud can absorb it, and the spectrum there is clean.
	\item Each wavelength in the forest belongs to one redshift, and so to one distance along the line of sight. The spectrum is a map of the hydrogen along a line through the universe, read as a function of wavelength.
	\item The other Lyman lines (Lyman~$\beta$ at $102.6\,\mathrm{nm}$ and so on) make dips of their own further blue. Between the quasar's Lyman~$\beta$ and Lyman~$\alpha$ emission, only Lyman~$\alpha$ dips appear, which is the region usually used.
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{un:fig:forest_real} shows the forest of a quasar at $z=\SixteenLyaZq$ computed for a realistic population of clouds. On average a fraction $\SixteenLyaMeanT$ of the light gets through between its Lyman~$\beta$ and Lyman~$\alpha$ emission, and the spectrum redward of its Lyman~$\alpha$ peak is untouched.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch16_lya_forest.pdf}
	\caption{The spectrum of a quasar at $z=\SixteenLyaZq$ seen through $\SixteenLyaLines$ hydrogen clouds between $z=2$ and $z=\SixteenLyaZq$, with column densities and numbers per unit redshift as observed. The grey curve is the quasar's light without the clouds.}
	\label{un:fig:forest_real}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{How much a neutral universe would absorb.} Suppose the hydrogen between the galaxies were spread evenly, with $n_{\rm HI}$ neutral atoms per unit volume. A photon seen today at frequency $\nu_0$ had frequency $\nu=\nu_0(1+z)$ at redshift $z$. By Eq.~\eqref{rad:eq:kappa_sigma} the fraction it loses over a path $dl$ is $n_{\rm HI}\sigma(\nu)\,dl$, where $\sigma(\nu)$ is the cross-section of the line. The path covered while the redshift drops by $dz$ is $dl=c\,dt=c\,dz/[(1+z)H]$, since $1+z=1/a$ gives $dz/dt=-(1+z)H$. So
\begin{align}
	\tau(\nu_0)
	&\eqby{1} \int n_{\rm HI}\,\sigma\big(\nu_0(1+z)\big)\,\frac{c\,dz}{(1+z)H} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} \frac{n_{\rm HI}\,c}{H\,\nu_\alpha}\int\sigma(\nu)\,d\nu
	\eqby{3} \frac{e^2f}{4\varepsilon_0m_ec}\,\frac{\lambda_\alpha\,n_{\rm HI}}{H} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Add up the fractions lost along the whole path.
	\item The line is so narrow that $\sigma$ is non-zero only over a tiny range of $z$ around the redshift where $\nu_0(1+z)=\nu_\alpha$. Over that range $n_{\rm HI}$, $H$ and $1+z$ are constant. Change variable to $\nu=\nu_0(1+z)$, so $dz=(1+z)\,d\nu/\nu$ with $\nu\approx\nu_\alpha$.
	\item $c/\nu_\alpha=\lambda_\alpha$, and the area under the line's cross-section, from atomic physics, is $\int\sigma\,d\nu=(e^2/4\varepsilon_0m_ec)\,f$ with oscillator strength $f=0.416$ for Lyman~$\alpha$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\tau_{\rm GP}=\frac{e^2f}{4\varepsilon_0m_ec}\,\frac{\lambda_\alpha\,n_{\rm HI}(z)}{H(z)} .}
	\label{un:eq:gunn_peterson}
\end{equation}
This is the \textbf{Gunn--Peterson} optical depth. The shape of the line does not matter, only its area. Each wavelength samples the gas in a slab whose depth is set by how fast the expansion sweeps the line across it, which is why $H$ appears. If all the hydrogen between the galaxies were neutral, with the measured density of ordinary matter, $\tau_{\rm GP}$ would be $\SixteenTauGPthree$ at $z=3$ and $\SixteenTauGPsix$ at $z=6$. Not one photon in a million million would get through blueward of the quasar's Lyman~$\alpha$ line. Yet quasars at $z=3$ let most of that light through. So the gas between the galaxies is almost completely ionised. A neutral fraction of only $\SixteenXHIone$ already gives $\tau=1$ at $z=3$.

\paragraph{Reionisation.} After recombination (Section~\ref{un:sec:cmb}) the gas was neutral. Something ionised it again, the ultraviolet light of the first stars, galaxies and quasars. In quasars beyond $z\approx6$ the forest merges into a band of total darkness, a Gunn--Peterson trough. The neutral fraction there has risen above about $10^{-4}$. The trough marks the end of reionisation, about a billion years after the Big Bang.

\paragraph{What the dips trace.} Today's ionised gas is held in balance by two rates (P4). Ultraviolet light ionises each neutral atom at a rate $\Gamma_{\rm ion}$, and protons and electrons recombine at a rate $\alpha_{\rm rec}(T)\,n_e$ per proton. Balancing ionisations against recombinations in nearly fully ionised gas, $n_e\approx n_p\approx n_{\rm H}$,
\[
\Gamma_{\rm ion}\,n_{\rm HI}=\alpha_{\rm rec}(T)\,n_{\rm H}^2\quad\implies\quad n_{\rm HI}=\frac{\alpha_{\rm rec}(T)}{\Gamma_{\rm ion}}\,n_{\rm H}^2\propto(1+\delta)^2 ,
\]
where $\delta$ is the density contrast of Section~\ref{un:sec:growth}. The neutral hydrogen, and so the local optical depth, grows as the square of the density. Each dip is an overdense place on the line of sight, and the depth of the absorption at each wavelength measures the density of gas at that distance. The forest is a map of the density of the universe along a line, at redshifts $2$ to $5$, where no galaxy survey reaches so finely.

\paragraph{Dark matter and neutrinos.} On scales larger than its Jeans length (Section~\ref{sg:sec:jeans}), a few hundred kiloparsecs for gas at $10^4\,\mathrm K$, the gas has fallen into the wells of the dark matter and traces them (Section~\ref{un:sec:growth}). So the dips trace the clumping of the dark matter itself, on scales from about one to a hundred megaparsecs. Three things follow from measuring how strongly the forest fluctuates on each scale.
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Dark matter is cold.} Particles born with random speeds fly straight out of any overdensity smaller than the distance they travel, and erase it before it can grow. Fast, light particles erase small structures. The forest shows structure down to about a megaparsec, so the dark-matter particles were slow. This rules out warm dark matter lighter than a few $\mathrm{keV}$.
	\item \textbf{Neutrinos are light.} Neutrinos with mass behave as hot dark matter and smooth out small-scale growth in the same way. The forest, combined with the microwave background, limits the sum of the three neutrino masses to about $0.1\,\mathrm{eV}$.
	\item \textbf{The expansion history.} With many quasars close together on the sky, the forests can be compared across lines of sight, mapping the gas in three dimensions. The clustering of the gas has a preferred scale, $150\,\mathrm{Mpc}$, set by sound waves before recombination. Seen at $z\approx2.3$, its apparent size measures $H(z)$ and the distance to that redshift, a standard ruler for the expansion at an epoch no supernova reaches.
\end{itemize}
The widths of the narrowest dips give one more number. Their Doppler width is set by the thermal speed of the hydrogen atoms (Section~\ref{sec:ism_lines}), and it gives the temperature of the gas, about $10^4\,\mathrm K$, set by the heating of photoionisation.

\section{The Accelerating Expansion}
\label{un:sec:acceleration}

The acceleration equation, Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:acceleration}, said that ordinary matter slows the expansion and a cosmological constant speeds it up. Which wins today cannot be read off from the expansion rate alone. It needs distances to objects far enough away that the expansion history has changed along the way.

Think of judging whether a car is speeding up by looking at two milestones far apart. If the second is reached sooner than the first speed predicts, the car was slowing. If it takes longer, the car was speeding up. A distant galaxy at a given redshift appears fainter if the universe has been speeding up, because it is farther away. Type Ia supernovae are such milestones. They are white dwarfs pushed to the Chandrasekhar mass (Section~\ref{cs:sec:wd}), so they explode with nearly the same peak luminosity (Section~\ref{cs:sec:snIa}), and their brightness gives their distance. At a given redshift they are fainter than a slowing universe predicts.

In numbers, at $z=1$ the luminosity distance in the Planck universe is $\SixteenDlumOne\,\mathrm{Mpc}$, against $5211\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ in a matter-only universe with the same $H_0$. A supernova there is $0.58$ magnitudes fainter, an effect first seen in 1998. The picture is a scale factor curve that bends upward in its recent part (Figure~\ref{un:fig:scale_factor}). The same deceleration parameter is measured with the sizes of galaxy clustering patterns and with gravitational-wave sources of known loudness (Section~\ref{cs:sec:gw170817}). It gives the deceleration parameter and when the acceleration began.

\paragraph{The deceleration parameter.} The dimensionless measure of whether the expansion slows is $q\equiv-\ddot aa/\dot a^2$. Dividing Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:acceleration} by $H^2$, with $\rho_i=\Omega_i\rho_{\rm crit}$ today and $P=w\rho c^2$,
\begin{align}
	q_0
	&\eqby{1} \frac{4\pi G}{3H_0^2}\sum_i\rho_i(1+3w_i)
	\eqby{2} \frac12\sum_i\Omega_i(1+3w_i) \nonumber\\
	&= \frac{\Omega_m}{2}+\Omega_r-\Omega_\Lambda .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:acceleration} with $P_i=w_i\rho_ic^2$, divided by $H_0^2$.
	\item $\rho_{\rm crit}=3H_0^2/(8\pi G)$, Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:critical}.
\end{explain}
For the Planck values $q_0=\SixteenQzero$, so the expansion is speeding up today. It began to speed up when $\Omega_ma^{-3}/2=\Omega_\Lambda$, at $z=\SixteenZacc$, a little before the cosmological constant came to dominate the density at $z=\SixteenZeqML$.

\section{Problem Set: The Expanding Universe}

\subsection{Problem 1: Hubble Time and Age}
\label{un:prob:age}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: (a) Convert $H_0=67.4\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ into a Hubble time $1/H_0$ in years. (b) What is the age of a flat matter-only universe with this $H_0$? (c) Why is it a problem that the oldest stars are about $13$ billion years old, and how does the cosmological constant solve it?

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $1\,\mathrm{Mpc}=3.086\times10^{19}\,\mathrm{km}$. Matter-only age $2/(3H_0)$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Hubble time}. $1/H_0=3.086\times10^{19}/67.4\,\mathrm s=4.58\times10^{17}\,\mathrm s$.
	\[
	\boxed{1/H_0\simeq\SixteenHubbleTime\ \text{Gyr}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Matter only}.
	\[
	\boxed{t_0=\tfrac23\,\SixteenHubbleTime\ \text{Gyr}\simeq\SixteenAgeMatterOnly\ \text{Gyr}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Old stars}. A universe younger than its oldest stars is impossible. The cosmological constant has sped up the recent expansion, so for the same $H_0$ today the past expansion was slower and took longer, giving $\SixteenAge$ Gyr (Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:age}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 2: How Thin Is the Universe?}
\label{un:prob:density}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Find the critical density today in $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and in protons per cubic metre. How many protons per cubic metre are there in ordinary matter ($\Omega_b=0.049$)?

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\rho_{\rm crit}=3H_0^2/(8\pi G)$, Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:critical}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\[
\boxed{\rho_{\rm crit}=8.5\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}=5.1\ \text{protons per m}^3,\qquad \text{ordinary matter}\ 0.25\ \text{per m}^3.}
\]
Ordinary matter averages one proton in four cubic metres, while each cubic metre holds $4.1\times10^8$ photons of the microwave background, giving $\eta=6.1\times10^{-10}$.

\subsection{Problem 3: The Light at Recombination}
\label{un:prob:cmb}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: (a) What was the temperature of the microwave background at $z=1090$? (b) What was the mean energy of its photons, $2.70\,k_BT$? (c) Compare with the ionisation energy of hydrogen, and explain why hydrogen could recombine.

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $T=T_0(1+z)$, Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:T_scaling}.
	\item The photon excess $1/\eta$ in the Saha equation.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Temperature}.
	\[
	\boxed{T=2.7255\times1091\,\mathrm K\simeq2970\,\mathrm K.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Mean photon energy}. $2.70\times(8.617\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV\,K^{-1}})\times2970\,\mathrm K$.
	\[
	\boxed{\bar E\simeq0.69\,\mathrm{eV}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Why recombination happens}. The mean photon has only $\tfrac{1}{20}$ of the $13.6\,\mathrm{eV}$ needed. The fraction of photons above $13.6\,\mathrm{eV}$ falls as $e^{-B/k_BT}$, and once it is below $\eta\approx6\times10^{-10}$ there are fewer ionising photons than atoms. At higher temperatures, a fraction as small as $10^{-9}$ of a billion photons per atom still sufficed to keep hydrogen ionised.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 4: Helium From the Neutron Ratio}
\label{un:prob:helium}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: (a) If the neutron-to-proton ratio is $\tfrac16$ when nuclei form, what is the helium mass fraction? (b) If neutrons did not decay, with $n/p=\tfrac15$, what would it be? (c) Why does a faster expansion rate (more kinds of light particles) raise $Y$?

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $Y=2(n/p)/(1+n/p)$.
	\item Freeze-out at $\Gamma\approx H$, Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:freeze}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{With decay}.
	\[
	\boxed{Y=\frac{2/6}{7/6}\simeq0.29.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Without decay}.
	\[
	\boxed{Y=\frac{2/5}{6/5}\simeq0.33.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Faster expansion}. A larger $H$ at a given temperature makes $\Gamma\approx H$ happen at a higher temperature, when $e^{-Q/k_BT}$ is larger, and leaves less time for decay. Both raise $n/p$ and $Y$. The measured $Y$ was used this way to count the light neutrino species before particle accelerators did.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 5: Growth Since Recombination}
\label{un:prob:growth}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: In a matter-dominated universe, by what factor does a density contrast grow from $z=1090$ to $z=0$? Starting from $10^{-5}$, does ordinary matter alone reach $\delta\sim1$ today? From when could dark matter grow?

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\delta\propto a$ in the matter era, Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:growth}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\[
\boxed{\frac{\delta_0}{\delta_{1090}}=\frac{a_0}{a_{1090}}=1091,\qquad \delta_0\simeq10^{-2}.}
\]
This is far below one, and the cosmological constant, which slows growth in the last few billion years, makes the gap larger. Dark matter is not held up by the light, so it can grow from matter–radiation equality, $z\approx\SixteenZeqMR$. By recombination it has grown by a further factor of a few and its wells are ready for ordinary matter to fall into.
