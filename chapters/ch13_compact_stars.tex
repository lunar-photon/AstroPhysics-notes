\chapter{Degenerate and Compact Stars}
\label{ch:compact}

\input{results/ch13/mass_radius.tex}

When a star has burned what it can, its core is left without a source of heat. It contracts, and by Section~\ref{sg:sec:negheat} it heats, until something other than heat holds it up. Two things can. The degeneracy pressure of electrons holds up white dwarfs, and that of neutrons holds up neutron stars. When neither suffices, nothing does, and the core becomes a black hole.

The word relativistic is used here in two separate senses, and keeping them apart is most of the chapter. Relativistic particles, electrons moving near the speed of light, soften the pressure to $\gamma=\tfrac43$, and that alone, in plain Newtonian gravity, sets the maximum mass of a white dwarf. Relativistic gravity changes the structure equations themselves, and it matters only when a star is compact enough. General relativity itself is not recapped. The relativistic forms below are quoted from it.

\section{Compactness}
\label{cs:sec:compactness}

The equations of Chapter~\ref{ch:structure} used Newtonian gravity throughout.

They cannot hold for every star. A body compact enough must be described by general relativity, and the question is where the line falls.

Think of a ball thrown up from the surface of a small asteroid. A gentle throw sends it away for good. On the Earth the same throw brings it straight back. How hard gravity grips a body at the surface depends on how much mass is packed into how small a radius.

The grip of gravity, measured against the speed of light, is the ratio $GM/(Rc^2)$, the \textbf{compactness}. It compares the escape energy per unit mass at the surface with $c^2$. Every correction that general relativity makes to the structure of a star is of this size, so it decides whether Newtonian gravity is good enough (P6).

In numbers, the Sun has $GM/(Rc^2)=2.1\times10^{-6}$, the white dwarf Sirius B $2.6\times10^{-4}$, and a neutron star of $1.4\,M_\odot$ and $12\,\mathrm{km}$ radius $0.17$.

The picture is a scale running from $0$ to $\tfrac12$. The Sun sits at the bottom, white dwarfs a little above, neutron stars a third of the way up, and a black hole at the top.

The same ratio sets the gravitational redshift of light leaving a surface, the bending of starlight, and the share of rest mass released when matter falls onto a compact object. It gives the size of every relativistic correction.

\section{White Dwarfs}
\label{cs:sec:wd}

The pressure of degenerate electrons is a power of the density alone, $\rho^{5/3}$ for slow electrons and $\rho^{4/3}$ for fast ones (Eq.~\eqref{st:eq:degenerate_P}). A star held up by it is therefore a polytrope (Section~\ref{st:sec:polytropes}).

A white dwarf of the Sun's mass is the size of the Earth. As its mass grows, its electrons are squeezed harder and move faster. The polytrope of slow electrons cannot describe the heaviest ones.

Think of a crowd in a hall, pressed toward the front by a slope. Each person's push on the person behind grows the harder they are squeezed, at first. If everyone is already pushing as hard as they can, adding more people down the slope only crushes the front, and no new push appears to stop it.

Electrons near the speed of light behave like that crowd. Their momentum can keep growing, but their speed cannot exceed $c$, so their pressure grows more slowly with density, $\gamma\to\tfrac43$. By the virial theorem (Section~\ref{sg:sec:virial}), a star with $\gamma=\tfrac43$ is barely bound, and by the mass–radius relation \eqref{st:eq:mass_radius} only one mass fits. Above that mass, degenerate electrons cannot hold a star up. Counting states (P3) and the virial balance of a sphere (P1, P2) give that mass.

In numbers, the limit is $\ThirteenMchFormula\,M_\odot$ for the carbon and oxygen of white dwarfs, with two atomic mass units per electron, $\mu_e=2$.

The picture is Figure~\ref{cs:fig:wd}, the radius of white dwarfs shrinking as their mass grows, down to zero at the limit.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch13_wd_mass_radius.pdf}
	\caption{The mass of white dwarfs against their radius, computed from the exact pressure of degenerate electrons with $\mu_e=2$. The dashed line marks $\ThirteenMchFormula\,M_\odot$. The dot marks the observed mass and radius of Sirius B.}
	\label{cs:fig:wd}
\end{figure}

The same limit sets the mass of the iron core of a massive star at the moment it collapses into a neutron star, and the mass at which a white dwarf gaining matter from a companion explodes as a type Ia supernova (Section~\ref{cs:sec:snIa}). It gives the mass–radius relation of white dwarfs and the Chandrasekhar mass, by two routes.

\paragraph{Slow electrons.} A light white dwarf has slow electrons, so $\gamma=\tfrac53$ and, by Eq.~\eqref{st:eq:mass_radius},
\begin{equation}
	R\propto M^{-1/3}.
\end{equation}
Adding mass makes it smaller. The degenerate pressure depends on the density alone, so a heavier star must be denser to hold itself up.

\paragraph{Fast electrons, the Chandrasekhar mass.} For fast electrons, $P=\mathcal K\rho^{4/3}$ with
\[
\mathcal K=\frac{hc}{8}\left(\frac{3}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}\frac{1}{(\mu_em_{\rm u})^{4/3}}
\]
from Eq.~\eqref{st:eq:degenerate_P} and $n_e=\rho/(\mu_em_{\rm u})$, where $m_{\rm u}$ is the atomic mass unit. With $\gamma=\tfrac43$, Eq.~\eqref{st:eq:r0} gives $r_0^2=\mathcal K\rho_c^{-2/3}/(\pi G)$, so $r_0^3\rho_c=(\mathcal K/\pi G)^{3/2}$ does not depend on $\rho_c$, and the mass formula of Section~\ref{st:sec:polytropes} gives one mass only,
\begin{align}
	M_{\rm Ch}
	&\eqby{1} 4\pi\Big[-\xi^2\dv{\theta}{\xi}\Big]_{\xi_1}\left(\frac{\mathcal K}{\pi G}\right)^{3/2} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} 4\pi\times\TwelveOmegaThree\times\left(\frac{hc}{8\pi G}\right)^{3/2}\left(\frac3\pi\right)^{1/2}\frac{1}{(\mu_em_{\rm u})^2} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The mass of a polytrope, $4\pi r_0^3\rho_c[-\xi^2\theta']_{\xi_1}$, with $r_0^3\rho_c=(\mathcal K/\pi G)^{3/2}$.
	\item The Lane–Emden value for $\gamma=\tfrac43$ and $\mathcal K$ written out.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{M_{\rm Ch}=\frac{5.83}{\mu_e^2}\,M_\odot=\ThirteenMchFormula\,M_\odot\quad(\mu_e=2).}
	\label{cs:eq:chandrasekhar}
\end{equation}
The mass contains $h$, $c$, $G$ and the mass per electron, and nothing about the star's history. Quantum mechanics ($h$), relativity ($c$) and gravity ($G$) together fix a mass of the order of the Sun's.

\paragraph{The same mass by an energy argument.} Landau found the limit by comparing two energies of one electron in a star of $N$ electrons and radius $R$. Its share of gravitational energy is about $-GM\mu_em_{\rm u}/R$, with $M=N\mu_em_{\rm u}$. A fast electron confined to a spacing $R/N^{1/3}$ has, by the uncertainty principle, a momentum of about $\hbar N^{1/3}/R$ and so a kinetic energy of about $\hbar cN^{1/3}/R$. Both scale as $1/R$, so shrinking the star changes their ratio not at all (P6). If gravity wins at one radius, it wins at every radius, and the star collapses. The two are equal when
\begin{align}
	\frac{\hbar c\,N^{1/3}}{R}
	&= \frac{G\,N(\mu_em_{\rm u})^2}{R} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad N
	&= \left(\frac{\hbar c}{G(\mu_em_{\rm u})^2}\right)^{3/2},\qquad
	M=N\mu_em_{\rm u}=\left(\frac{\hbar c}{G}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{(\mu_em_{\rm u})^2}.
\end{align}
This is $0.47\,M_\odot$ for $\mu_e=2$. The estimate has the right combination of constants and falls short of Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:chandrasekhar} only by a number, $3.1$, that the full polytrope supplies.

\paragraph{The full curve.} Real white dwarfs have electrons that are neither slow nor at the speed of light. Figure~\ref{cs:fig:wd} uses the exact pressure of degenerate electrons at any speed, integrated in Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium. Light white dwarfs follow $R\propto M^{-1/3}$. As the mass rises toward $\ThirteenMchFormula\,M_\odot$ the radius falls to zero. Sirius B, $1.02\,M_\odot$, lies on the curve at $\ThirteenRsiriusB\,\mathrm{km}$, against $5840\,\mathrm{km}$ measured, close enough given that the model ignores temperature and composition details. Throughout, $GM/(Rc^2)<10^{-3}$, so Newtonian gravity is enough. The limit comes from the particles being relativistic, not gravity.

\subsection{Type Ia Supernovae}
\label{cs:sec:snIa}

A white dwarf of carbon and oxygen has no fuel it can burn at its present density, and left alone it only cools. Two things can push it toward the limit \eqref{cs:eq:chandrasekhar}. It can take gas from a companion star that has swollen enough to spill onto it. Or it can merge with a second white dwarf, after the two have spiralled together by radiating gravitational waves (Section~\ref{cs:sec:gw}).

\paragraph{Ignition without a thermostat.} As the mass climbs toward the limit, the radius shrinks (Figure~\ref{cs:fig:wd}) and the centre is squeezed and heated. Near $1.4\,M_\odot$ the centre reaches a few $10^{12}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and a few $10^8\,\mathrm K$, and carbon nuclei begin to fuse. Two carbon nuclei, $Z_1=Z_2=6$ with a reduced mass of six atomic mass units, have a Gamow energy $E_G=7.6\,\mathrm{GeV}$. At $5\times10^8\,\mathrm K$, Eq.~\eqref{st:eq:gamow} puts the Gamow peak at $E_0=1.5\,\mathrm{MeV}=35\,k_BT$, and the slope $-\tfrac23+E_0/k_BT$ of Section~\ref{st:sec:eos} makes the rate grow as about $T^{35}$. A rise of one percent in temperature raises the burning by forty percent.

In an ordinary star such burning is held steady. The extra heat raises the pressure, the core expands and cools, and the burning slows (Section~\ref{sg:sec:negheat}). In a white dwarf the pressure is that of degenerate electrons, which does not depend on the temperature (Eq.~\eqref{st:eq:degenerate_P}). The heat does not expand the core, so nothing cools it, and the thermostat fails (Section~\ref{st:sec:eos}). Hotter gas burns faster, and faster burning makes it hotter. The burning runs away and sweeps through the whole star in about a second, a few times the free-fall time of so dense a star (Section~\ref{sg:sec:freefall}). By the time the gas is hot enough for its pressure to respond, the fuel is spent.

\paragraph{Nothing is left.} Burning carbon and oxygen to iron-group nuclei such as nickel-56 raises the binding energy from about $7.8$ to $8.6\,\mathrm{MeV}$ per nucleon. About one solar mass burns. A white dwarf near the limit has electrons with $\gamma$ close to $\tfrac43$, so by Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:E_gamma} its total energy $E$ is close to zero, and its binding energy is only about $5\times10^{43}\,\mathrm J$. With $M=1.4\,M_\odot$ and $v$ the mean speed of the debris, energy conservation (P1) gives
\begin{align}
	\tfrac12Mv^2
	&\eqby{1} \frac{0.82\,\mathrm{MeV}}{m_{\rm u}}\times1\,M_\odot-|E| \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} 1.6\times10^{44}\,\mathrm J-0.5\times10^{44}\,\mathrm J
	= 1.1\times10^{44}\,\mathrm J, \nonumber\\
	v
	&\eqby{3} \sqrt{\frac{2\times1.1\times10^{44}\,\mathrm J}{2.8\times10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}}}
	\approx 9\times10^{3}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}} .
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The burning releases $0.82\,\mathrm{MeV}$ per nucleon, and a nucleon has a mass of about $m_{\rm u}$. Unbinding the star costs $|E|$.
	\item $0.82\,\mathrm{MeV}/m_{\rm u}=7.9\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$.
	\item Solve for $v$.
\end{explain}
The burning releases more than the star's binding energy, so the whole star flies apart and no remnant is left. The speeds in the spectra of these supernovae, about $10^4\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, agree.

\paragraph{The light of radioactive nickel.} The heat of the explosion itself is spent in the expansion, as the debris grows from the size of the Earth to that of the Solar System. The light seen for months comes from radioactivity. Carbon and oxygen nuclei have equal numbers of protons and neutrons, and so does nickel-56, the most tightly bound nucleus that can be made from them in a second. About $0.6\,M_\odot$ of it is made. It decays to cobalt-56 with a mean life of $8.8$ days, releasing $1.75\,\mathrm{MeV}$ per decay, and cobalt-56 decays to iron-56 with a mean life of $111$ days. With $0.6\,M_\odot/(56\,m_{\rm u})=1.3\times10^{55}$ nickel nuclei, the nickel heats the debris at the rate
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1.3\times10^{55}\times1.75\,\mathrm{MeV}}{8.8\,\mathrm d}\,e^{-t/8.8\,\mathrm d}
	=4.8\times10^{36}\,\mathrm W\times e^{-t/8.8\,\mathrm d}.
\end{equation}
The light has to diffuse out through the debris (Section~\ref{rad:sec:diffusion}), which takes about three weeks, so the light curve peaks then. The peak luminosity is close to the heating at that moment, from nickel and cobalt together, about $10^{36}\,\mathrm W$, or $3\times10^9\,L_\odot$. One star outshines its whole galaxy for weeks. After the peak the light follows the cobalt. If all the decay energy stayed in the debris, the light would fall by $1.0857\times100/111=0.98$ magnitudes per 100 days (the factor $1.0857$ of Eq.~\eqref{ph:eq:extinction_tau}). Real tails fall a little faster, because the debris thins and more of the gamma rays escape without being absorbed.

\paragraph{A standard candle.} Every such explosion starts from a white dwarf of carbon and oxygen close to the same mass, so it makes a similar amount of nickel and reaches a similar peak, $M_B\approx-19.3$ (Section~\ref{as:sec:ladder}). The peaks still spread by a few tenths of a magnitude. The spread follows the shape of the light curve. Brighter explosions fade more slowly (the Phillips relation), because more nickel makes hotter, more opaque debris from which light leaks out more slowly. Once each peak is corrected by the width of its light curve, the spread falls to about $0.15$ magnitudes, $7\%$ in distance.

No type Ia supernova is close enough for a parallax, so the peak luminosity is calibrated in galaxies that have hosted a type Ia supernova and also contain Cepheids, whose distances come from the parallax of nearby Cepheids (Section~\ref{as:sec:ladder}). At its peak it is $10^5$ to $10^6$ times more luminous than a Cepheid and is seen $500$ to $1000$ times farther, Eq.~\eqref{as:eq:reach}. Cepheids reach about $30\,\mathrm{Mpc}$, and type Ia supernovae reach redshifts of one and more, far enough to show that the expansion of the universe is speeding up (Section~\ref{un:sec:acceleration}).

\section{The Relativistic Structure Equations}
\label{cs:sec:relativistic}

The four structure equations of Section~\ref{st:sec:four_equations} assumed Newtonian gravity.

A neutron star has $GM/(Rc^2)\approx0.2$. Corrections of that size cannot be dropped, and each equation must be replaced by its general-relativistic form.

Think of weighing a box of hot gas. Heat the gas and the box gets heavier, because energy has mass. Squeeze the gas and its pressure adds to the weight as well. In everyday life the extra weight is far too small to notice.

In a neutron star it is not small. The pressure that holds the star up is itself a source of gravity, so pushing harder also pulls harder. General relativity, with conservation of energy and momentum (P1) in curved space-time, gives the equations. They are quoted here from general relativity, not derived again.

In numbers, the pressure at the centre of a neutron star is between about a tenth and a half of its energy density $\rho c^2$. At the centre of the Sun the same ratio is about $2\times10^{-6}$.

The picture is the Newtonian structure equations of Section~\ref{st:sec:four_equations}, each with a few correction factors, every one of them close to one when $GM/(rc^2)$ and $P/(\rho c^2)$ are small.

The same equations, in the form given by Thorne (1977), are used for neutron stars, for the hottest proto-neutron stars just after a supernova, and for any star compact enough. They give the full relativistic set, side by side with the Newtonian one.

\paragraph{The metric.} A static spherical star has the metric
\begin{equation}
	ds^2=-e^{2\Phi/c^2}c^2dt^2+\left(1-\frac{2Gm(r)}{rc^2}\right)^{-1}dr^2+r^2\big(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\big),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi(r)$ becomes the Newtonian potential of Section~\ref{pr:sec:p2} far from the star, and $m(r)$ is the mass inside $r$.

\begin{table}[htbp]
	\centering
	\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{2.0}
	\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
		\toprule
		& \textbf{Newtonian} & \textbf{General relativity} \\
		\midrule
		Mass & $\dv{m}{r}=4\pi r^2\rho$ & $\dv{m}{r}=4\pi r^2\rho$, \ $\rho c^2=$ total energy density \\
		Momentum & $\dv{P}{r}=-\dfrac{Gm\rho}{r^2}$ & $\dv{P}{r}=-\dfrac{G\big(\rho+\frac{P}{c^2}\big)\big(m+\frac{4\pi r^3P}{c^2}\big)}{r^2\big(1-\frac{2Gm}{rc^2}\big)}$ \\
		Potential & $\dv{\Phi}{r}=\dfrac{Gm}{r^2}$ & $\dv{\Phi}{r}=-\dfrac{c^2}{\rho c^2+P}\,\dv{P}{r}$ \\
		Energy & $\dv{L}{r}=4\pi r^2\rho\,\varepsilon$ & $\dv{r}\Big(L\,e^{2\Phi/c^2}\Big)=\dfrac{4\pi r^2\rho\,\varepsilon\,e^{2\Phi/c^2}}{\sqrt{1-2Gm/rc^2}}$ \\
		Transport & $L=-\dfrac{16\pi ac\,r^2T^3}{3\kappa\rho}\,\dv{T}{r}$ & $L=-\dfrac{16\pi ac\,r^2T^3}{3\kappa\rho}\,\sqrt{1-\tfrac{2Gm}{rc^2}}\;e^{-\Phi/c^2}\,\dv{r}\Big(T\,e^{\Phi/c^2}\Big)$ \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabular}
	\caption{The structure equations of a static star in Newtonian gravity and in general relativity (energy equation in a star that does not change).}
	\label{cs:tab:relativistic}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Reading the corrections.} The momentum equation is the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff (TOV) equation. Each of its three new factors is one physical effect.
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\rho+P/c^2$ in place of $\rho$. Pressure has inertia. A parcel under pressure is harder to hold up.
	\item $m+4\pi r^3P/c^2$ in place of $m$. Pressure gravitates. The pressure that holds the star up adds to the pull it must resist.
	\item $(1-2Gm/rc^2)^{-1}$. Space inside the star is curved, and the radial distance is longer than $dr$.
\end{itemize}
All three make gravity stronger. Raising the pressure to support more mass also raises gravity, so beyond some mass no equation of state can win. This is why neutron stars, unlike white dwarfs, have a maximum mass set by gravity itself, whatever the particles.

The energy equation conserves the luminosity as seen from far away, $L\,e^{2\Phi/c^2}$, where one factor $e^{\Phi/c^2}$ is the redshift of each photon's energy and the other is the slowing of clocks deep in the potential. The square root turns $dr$ into a proper volume.

\paragraph{Equilibrium in a gravitational field.} With no heat flow, $L=0$, the transport equation gives not a uniform $T$ but
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{T\,e^{\Phi/c^2}=\text{constant}.}
	\label{cs:eq:tolman}
\end{equation}
This is the Tolman–Ehrenfest relation. In a gravitational field, a body in full thermal equilibrium is hotter at the bottom, by the same factor by which light climbing out is redshifted. Heat flows only when $T\,e^{\Phi/c^2}$ changes from place to place. The statement in Section~\ref{sg:sec:rotation} that global equilibrium means one temperature throughout is the Newtonian limit of this. For the Sun the difference is two parts in a million. Across a neutron star it is about twenty percent.

\paragraph{Mass and binding.} In general relativity $m(r)$ counts the total energy inside $r$, including the negative gravitational binding energy. The mass $M$ that governs an orbit around the star is smaller than the rest mass of all its particles, by about $GM/(Rc^2)$ of it. For a neutron star the difference is about a tenth of the rest mass, $3\times10^{46}\,\mathrm J$, released when the core collapses, almost all of it as neutrinos (Problem~\ref{cs:prob:binding}).

\section{Computing a Neutron Star}
\label{cs:sec:ns}

Table~\ref{cs:tab:relativistic} gives the equations, and the core of a collapsed star gives the material, neutrons packed to nuclear density.

How are the mass and radius of a neutron star actually found, and what does general relativity do to them?

Think of stacking sand into a cone. Pour more and the cone grows wider and taller. With dry sand the slope holds, and the cone can grow as large as the sand allows. With wet sand the weight of the top crushes the bottom past some height. Then the pile only spreads, and adding sand does not make it taller.

A neutron star is built in the same spirit, one central density at a time. Each central density gives one star, with its own mass and radius. Going to higher central density gives heavier stars, up to a point beyond which the extra gravity of the extra pressure wins and the mass falls. That point is the maximum mass. Finding it is the TOV equation (P1 and P2 in general relativity) solved with the pressure of degenerate neutrons (P3).

In numbers, an ideal gas of neutrons in general relativity reaches at most $\ThirteenMovMax\,M_\odot$, at a radius of $\ThirteenRovMax\,\mathrm{km}$. The same gas in Newtonian gravity reaches about $\ThirteenMnsNewtonLargest\,M_\odot$, eight times more.

The picture is Figure~\ref{cs:fig:ns}, two mass–radius curves for the same neutron gas, one turning over at a small mass.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch13_ns_mass_radius.pdf}
	\caption{Mass against radius for stars of an ideal degenerate neutron gas, computed with the TOV equation (solid) and with Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium (dashed). Each point is one central density.}
	\label{cs:fig:ns}
\end{figure}

The same procedure, with the best equations of state from nuclear physics, gives the mass–radius curves that are tested against measured neutron-star masses and radii. It gives the procedure and what it shows.

\paragraph{The procedure.} A cold neutron star has no burning and no heat flow that matter for its structure, so only the mass and momentum equations of Table~\ref{cs:tab:relativistic} are needed, with an equation of state $P(\rho)$.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Choose a central density $\rho_c$, so $P(0)=P(\rho_c)$ and $m(0)=0$.
	\item Integrate $\dv*{m}{r}$ and the TOV equation outward until $P=0$. That radius is $R$ and $m(R)$ is $M$.
	\item Repeat for a range of $\rho_c$. Each gives one point $(R,M)$.
\end{enumerate}
Both boundary conditions sit at the centre, unlike the two-ended problem of Section~\ref{st:sec:computing}, so a single outward integration finishes each star. Along the curve, stars with $\dv*{M}{\rho_c}>0$ are stable. Past the maximum, where $\dv*{M}{\rho_c}<0$, a small squeeze grows instead of rebounding.

\paragraph{What the curves show.} Figure~\ref{cs:fig:ns} uses the exact pressure of an ideal degenerate neutron gas, and its total energy density as the source of gravity. At low mass, where the star is large and $GM/(Rc^2)$ small, the two curves agree. As the mass grows they part. The Newtonian curve climbs toward the Chandrasekhar-like limit for neutrons, about $5.7\,M_\odot$, the $\mu_e=1$ value of Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:chandrasekhar}. The relativistic curve turns over at $\ThirteenMovMax\,M_\odot$, the Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit, where $GM/(Rc^2)=\ThirteenCompactOV$. General relativity has cut the maximum mass by a factor of eight. Pressure that gravitates defeats itself.

\paragraph{Real neutron stars.} Pulsars in binaries are weighed with the timing methods of Chapter~\ref{ch:twobody} (Section~\ref{sec:romer}), and some are found at about $2\,M_\odot$, far above $\ThirteenMovMax\,M_\odot$. Neutrons are not an ideal gas. Nuclear forces repel them strongly at the highest densities and stiffen the pressure, and the best equations of state give maximum masses of about $2$ to $2.3\,M_\odot$. The maximum mass itself, and its derivation in general relativity, are taken here from the theory of compact objects, not derived again.

\section{Black Holes}
\label{cs:sec:bh}

Above the maximum mass of a neutron star, no pressure can stop a collapse. By the reading of the TOV equation, every increase of pressure adds to the pull it fights.

The collapse continues until the matter has passed inside its own Schwarzschild radius, $r_s=2GM/c^2$, $3\,\mathrm{km}$ for the Sun's mass and $30\,\mathrm{km}$ for ten solar masses, where the compactness reaches $\tfrac12$ and nothing, not even light, comes back out. Stars that end their lives with cores well above the neutron-star limit leave black holes. Their properties belong to general relativity and are not repeated here.

\section{Gravitational Waves from Compact Binaries}
\label{cs:sec:gw}

Two stars in a binary move on a Kepler ellipse, and their orbital energy never changes. With the reduced mass written out as $m_1m_2/M$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:visviva_E} gives it as $E=-Gm_1m_2/(2a)$.

General relativity says this is not quite true. Masses that move send out waves in the geometry of space, and the waves carry energy away. For the Earth and the Sun the loss is far too small to matter. For two neutron stars or black holes close together it decides how the binary ends.

Think of two children holding hands and whirling round in the middle of a pool. Ripples run out from them to the edge and rock a toy boat floating there. Every ripple carries away a little energy, and that energy comes from the whirling children.

A binary of compact stars is the pair of children, space is the water, and the ripples are \textbf{gravitational waves}, small changes in the distances between free bodies that travel outward at the speed of light. Einstein predicted them from general relativity in 1916 and 1918. The idea is that the energy they carry is taken from the orbit. The orbit shrinks, and a bound orbit that loses energy speeds up, just as a star that loses energy heats up (Section~\ref{sg:sec:negheat}). So the waves rise in frequency and in strength until the two bodies merge. The rising tone is called a \textbf{chirp}.

In numbers, the Earth going round the Sun radiates $196\,\mathrm W$ in gravitational waves, and at that rate its orbit would take $10^{23}$ years to shrink to nothing. Two black holes of $36$ and $29\,M_\odot$, seen in 2015 as GW150914, radiated about $3\,M_\odot c^2$ in a fraction of a second, with a peak power of about $4\times10^{49}\,\mathrm W$.

The picture is Figure~\ref{cs:fig:gw_ring}, a ring of free particles that the passing wave stretches in one direction and squeezes in the other.

The same energy balance shrinks the orbits of satellites through drag (Section~\ref{tb:sec:planar_pert}). What can radiate is decided by conservation (P1). The orbit and its frequency come from the gravity of a sphere through Kepler's third law (P2). The orbit changes slowly compared with one turn, and the mass of the binary sets the frequency of its waves (P6). The same waves give the masses of black holes, a speed of gravity, and distances to galaxies without any ladder. It gives the reason the waves need a changing quadrupole, how they stretch space, the power of a binary, the shrinking orbit and the chirp, and how the waves have been seen.

\subsection{Why a Quadrupole}

\paragraph{The moments of the mass.} An accelerated charge radiates through its changing electric dipole moment $\sum_ke_k\vec x_k$, the Larmor formula \eqref{fp:eq:larmor}. Far from a source, its field can be sorted in the same way by the moments of the mass. The particles are labelled $k$, with masses $m_k$, positions $\vec x_k$ and speeds $\vec v_k$.
\begin{itemize}
	\item The \textbf{monopole} is the total mass $\sum_km_k$, which is conserved (P1). Its field never changes, so it cannot radiate.
	\item The \textbf{mass dipole} is $\sum_km_k\vec x_k$. Its rate of change is $\sum_km_k\vec v_k$, the total momentum, which is conserved (P1). So its second derivative is zero and it cannot radiate. For charges there is no such rule, because an electron and a proton have different ratios of charge to mass, and $\sum_ke_k\vec x_k$ can accelerate while the centre of mass does not.
	\item The other dipole, $\sum_km_k\vec x_k\times\vec v_k$, is the total angular momentum, also conserved (P1).
\end{itemize}
The first moment that can change in an isolated system is the second one, the matrix
\begin{equation}
	I_{ij}=\sum_km_k\,x_{k,i}\,x_{k,j},\qquad Q_{ij}=I_{ij}-\tfrac13\,\delta_{ij}\sum_lI_{ll},
	\label{cs:eq:Iij}
\end{equation}
where $i,j$ run over the three directions. The trace $\sum_lI_{ll}=\sum_km_k|\vec x_k|^2$ is the second moment $I$ of Route B of the virial theorem (Section~\ref{sg:sec:virial}). A change of the trace alone is a sphere breathing in and out, and that does not radiate either. What radiates is $Q_{ij}$, the \textbf{mass quadrupole}, the part of $I_{ij}$ with the trace removed.

\paragraph{The quadrupole formula.} General relativity gives the power radiated by a slowly moving source,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{L_{\rm GW}=\frac{G}{5c^5}\sum_{i,j}\avg{\dddot Q_{ij}^{\,2}},}
	\label{cs:eq:quadrupole}
\end{equation}
quoted here, with the average taken over several wave periods. A distance $d$ away, along the $z$ axis, the wave appears as the $2\times2$ matrix
\begin{equation}
	\begin{pmatrix} h_+ & h_\times\\ h_\times & -h_+\end{pmatrix}
	=\frac{2G}{c^4d}
	\begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12\big(\ddot Q_{xx}-\ddot Q_{yy}\big) & \ddot Q_{xy}\\ \ddot Q_{xy} & -\tfrac12\big(\ddot Q_{xx}-\ddot Q_{yy}\big)\end{pmatrix},
	\label{cs:eq:strain_Q}
\end{equation}
also quoted, with $Q_{ij}$ taken at the earlier time $t-d/c$. Only the part across the line of sight appears, and with its trace removed. The two numbers $h_+$ and $h_\times$ are the \textbf{strains} of the two polarisations. They are always written with their subscripts, since a bare $h$ is Planck's constant in Section~\ref{cs:sec:wd}.

The factor $G/c^5$ is tiny. Its inverse, $c^5/G=3.6\times10^{52}\,\mathrm W$, is the natural unit of power for gravitational waves, and a source comes near it only when its parts move near the speed of light.

\subsection{How the Wave Stretches Space}

\paragraph{The ring of particles.} Free particles sit on a ring in the $xy$ plane, across the wave. A particle at $(x,y)$ from the centre of the ring is moved by the wave to
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\begin{pmatrix} x'\\ y'\end{pmatrix}
	=\left[\Id+\frac12\begin{pmatrix} h_+ & h_\times\\ h_\times & -h_+\end{pmatrix}\right]
	\begin{pmatrix} x\\ y\end{pmatrix},}
	\label{cs:eq:ring}
\end{equation}
where the positions are measured by the travel time of light, the way a detector measures them. This too is quoted from general relativity. The wave acts as a tidal field that changes in time, like the pull of the Moon that stretches the Earth along one line and squeezes it across.

With $h_\times=0$, a particle on the $x$ axis moves from $(x,0)$ to $\big((1+\tfrac12h_+)x,\,0\big)$, and one on the $y$ axis from $(0,y)$ to $\big(0,\,(1-\tfrac12h_+)y\big)$. One length grows by the fraction $\tfrac12h_+$ and the length across it shrinks by the same fraction. The difference of two perpendicular lengths, divided by the length, is $h_+$. That is what the word strain means here. As $h_+$ swings between positive and negative values the ring becomes an ellipse along $x$, then a circle, then an ellipse along $y$ (Figure~\ref{cs:fig:gw_ring}, top). Particles along the line of sight are not moved, so the wave is transverse.

	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch13_gw_ring.pdf}
	\caption{A ring of free particles, drawn from Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:ring}, at four moments of one wave period, for the $+$ polarisation (top) and the $\times$ polarisation (bottom). The wave travels out of the page. The strain is drawn as $h_0=0.4$. The dashed circle is the ring with no wave.}
	\label{cs:fig:gw_ring}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Two polarisations, $45^\circ$ apart.} To compare the two patterns, we turn the $+$ pattern through an angle $\psi$ about the line of sight. A matrix that acts on positions turns as $R(\psi)\,(\cdot)\,R(\psi)^{\mathsf T}$, where $R(\psi)$ is the rotation matrix written out below, so
\begin{align}
	R(\psi)\begin{pmatrix} h_+ & 0\\ 0 & -h_+\end{pmatrix}R(\psi)^{\mathsf T}
	&= \begin{pmatrix} \cos\psi & -\sin\psi\\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{pmatrix} h_+ & 0\\ 0 & -h_+\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{pmatrix} \cos\psi & \sin\psi\\ -\sin\psi & \cos\psi\end{pmatrix} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{1} h_+\begin{pmatrix} \cos\psi & \sin\psi\\ \sin\psi & -\cos\psi\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{pmatrix} \cos\psi & \sin\psi\\ -\sin\psi & \cos\psi\end{pmatrix} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{2} h_+\begin{pmatrix} \cos2\psi & \sin2\psi\\ \sin2\psi & -\cos2\psi\end{pmatrix}.
	\label{cs:eq:pol_rotate}
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Multiply the first two matrices.
	\item Multiply out, with $\cos^2\psi-\sin^2\psi=\cos2\psi$ and $2\sin\psi\cos\psi=\sin2\psi$.
\end{explain}
At $\psi=45^\circ$ the result is $\begin{pmatrix}0&h_+\\h_+&0\end{pmatrix}$, a pure $\times$ wave. At $\psi=90^\circ$ it is the $+$ wave again with its sign reversed. So the two independent patterns are $45^\circ$ apart, and the pattern comes back to itself after a turn of $180^\circ$ (Figure~\ref{cs:fig:gw_ring}, bottom). For light, the electric field is a vector, $R(\psi)\,(E,0)^{\mathsf T}=E\,(\cos\psi,\sin\psi)^{\mathsf T}$. Its two independent polarisations are $90^\circ$ apart, and it comes back to itself only after $360^\circ$. The angle doubles because the wave of gravity is a matrix, not a vector.

\subsection{The Power of a Binary}

\paragraph{The quadrupole of a circular orbit.} Two stars of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, with $M=m_1+m_2$, move on a circle of radius $a$ (the semi-major axis of Chapter~\ref{ch:twobody}) about each other with angular speed $\omega$ (the mean motion $n$ of Chapter~\ref{ch:twobody}, renamed because $n$ is a number density here). In the orbital plane $xy$, the separation is $\vec r=a(\cos\omega t,\ \sin\omega t,\ 0)$. About the centre of mass the two stars sit at $\vec x_1=(m_2/M)\vec r$ and $\vec x_2=-(m_1/M)\vec r$ (Eq.~\eqref{tb:eq:body_paths}). Their second moment, from Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:Iij}, is
\begin{align}
	I_{ij}
	&= m_1\frac{m_2^2}{M^2}\,r_ir_j+m_2\frac{m_1^2}{M^2}\,r_ir_j
	= \frac{m_1m_2}{M}\,r_ir_j , \nonumber\\
	\begin{pmatrix} I_{xx} & I_{xy}\\ I_{xy} & I_{yy}\end{pmatrix}
	&= \frac{m_1m_2a^2}{M}\begin{pmatrix} \cos^2\omega t & \cos\omega t\sin\omega t\\ \cos\omega t\sin\omega t & \sin^2\omega t\end{pmatrix}
	\eqby{1} \frac{m_1m_2a^2}{2M}\begin{pmatrix} 1+\cos2\omega t & \sin2\omega t\\ \sin2\omega t & 1-\cos2\omega t\end{pmatrix},
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item $\cos^2\omega t=\tfrac12(1+\cos2\omega t)$, $\sin^2\omega t=\tfrac12(1-\cos2\omega t)$, $\cos\omega t\sin\omega t=\tfrac12\sin2\omega t$.
\end{explain}
and every entry with a $z$ is zero. The trace is $m_1m_2a^2/M$, constant in time, so $Q_{ij}$ and $I_{ij}$ have the same time derivatives. Everything that changes goes as $2\omega t$. The wave has \emph{twice} the orbital frequency, because the binary looks the same after half a turn,
\begin{equation}
	f=\frac{2\omega}{2\pi}=\frac{\omega}{\pi}.
	\label{cs:eq:f_gw}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The power.} Differentiating three times, with $\frac{d^3}{dt^3}\cos2\omega t=8\omega^3\sin2\omega t$ and $\frac{d^3}{dt^3}\sin2\omega t=-8\omega^3\cos2\omega t$,
\begin{align}
	\begin{pmatrix} \dddot Q_{xx} & \dddot Q_{xy}\\ \dddot Q_{xy} & \dddot Q_{yy}\end{pmatrix}
	&= \frac{4m_1m_2a^2\omega^3}{M}\begin{pmatrix} \sin2\omega t & -\cos2\omega t\\ -\cos2\omega t & -\sin2\omega t\end{pmatrix}, \nonumber\\
	\sum_{i,j}\dddot Q_{ij}^{\,2}
	&\eqby{1} \left(\frac{4m_1m_2a^2\omega^3}{M}\right)^2\big(2\sin^22\omega t+2\cos^22\omega t\big)
	= 32\left(\frac{m_1m_2}{M}\right)^2a^4\omega^6, \nonumber\\
	L_{\rm GW}
	&\eqby{2} \frac{32}{5}\,\frac{G}{c^5}\left(\frac{m_1m_2}{M}\right)^2a^4\omega^6
	\eqby{3} \frac{32}{5}\,\frac{G^4m_1^2m_2^2M}{c^5a^5}.
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item The four entries squared and added. The sum is the same at every moment, so no average is needed.
	\item The quadrupole formula \eqref{cs:eq:quadrupole}.
	\item Kepler's third law, $\omega^2a^3=GM$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:kepler3}), so $\omega^6=G^3M^3/a^9$.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{L_{\rm GW}=\frac{32}{5}\,\frac{G^4m_1^2m_2^2M}{c^5a^5}
	=\frac{32}{5}\,\frac{c^5}{G}\left(\frac{m_1m_2}{M^2}\right)^2\left(\frac{GM}{ac^2}\right)^5.}
	\label{cs:eq:L_gw}
\end{equation}
The second form shows the compactness of Section~\ref{cs:sec:compactness}, now of the orbit, to the fifth power. For the Earth and the Sun $GM/(ac^2)=10^{-8}$ and the power is $196\,\mathrm W$. When two compact stars are a few of their own radii apart, $GM/(ac^2)$ is a tenth or more and the power approaches $c^5/G$.

\subsection{The Shrinking Orbit and the Chirp}

\paragraph{The orbit shrinks.} The energy carried off by the waves is taken from the orbit (P1), $\dv*{E}{t}=-L_{\rm GW}$. In one turn the orbit loses only a tiny part of its energy, so it stays close to a circle whose radius slowly falls (P6, as for the slow drifts of Section~\ref{tb:sec:averaged}). With $E=-Gm_1m_2/(2a)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:visviva_E},
\begin{align}
	\dv{E}{t}
	&\eqby{1} \frac{Gm_1m_2}{2a^2}\,\dv{a}{t}
	\eqby{2} -\frac{32}{5}\,\frac{G^4m_1^2m_2^2M}{c^5a^5} \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad \dv{a}{t}
	&= -\frac{64}{5}\,\frac{G^3m_1m_2M}{c^5a^3}.
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Differentiate $E=-Gm_1m_2/(2a)$, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_a}.
	\item $\dv*{E}{t}=-L_{\rm GW}$ with Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:L_gw}.
\end{explain}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{\dv{a}{t}=-\frac{64}{5}\,\frac{G^3m_1m_2M}{c^5a^3}.}
	\label{cs:eq:adot}
\end{equation}
The shrinking speeds up as $a^{-3}$, so most of the time is spent far apart.

\paragraph{Time to merge.} Separating the variables and integrating from the separation $a_0$ now to zero at the merger, a time $t_{\rm merge}$ later,
\begin{align}
	\int_{a_0}^{0}a^3\,da
	&= -\frac{64}{5}\,\frac{G^3m_1m_2M}{c^5}\int_0^{t_{\rm merge}}dt \nonumber\\
	\implies\quad -\frac{a_0^4}{4}
	&= -\frac{64}{5}\,\frac{G^3m_1m_2M}{c^5}\,t_{\rm merge},
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{t_{\rm merge}=\frac{5}{256}\,\frac{c^5a_0^4}{G^3m_1m_2M}.}
	\label{cs:eq:t_merge}
\end{equation}
The steep $a_0^4$ decides everything. The Earth and the Sun need $10^{23}$ years. Two neutron stars of $1.4\,M_\odot$ a solar radius apart need $3\times10^7$ years, short enough that such pairs, born in the Galaxy, merge within its lifetime.

\paragraph{The chirp.} The frequency follows from the separation through Kepler's third law, $\omega=(GM)^{1/2}a^{-3/2}$. As $a$ falls, $\omega$ rises,
\begin{align}
	\dv{\omega}{t}
	&\eqby{1} -\frac32\,\frac{\omega}{a}\,\dv{a}{t}
	\eqby{2} \frac{96}{5}\,\frac{G^3m_1m_2M}{c^5}\,\frac{\omega}{a^4} \nonumber\\
	&\eqby{3} \frac{96}{5}\,\frac{G^{5/3}}{c^5}\,\frac{m_1m_2}{M^{1/3}}\,\omega^{11/3}.
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Differentiate $\omega\propto a^{-3/2}$.
	\item Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:adot}.
	\item Kepler's third law \eqref{eq:kepler3} gives $a^4=(GM)^{4/3}\omega^{-8/3}$.
\end{explain}
The two masses enter only as $m_1m_2/M^{1/3}$, a mass to the power $\tfrac53$. Its $\tfrac35$ power is the \textbf{chirp mass},
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{M_{\rm chirp}=\frac{(m_1m_2)^{3/5}}{M^{1/5}},\qquad
	\dv{\omega}{t}=\frac{96}{5}\,\frac{(GM_{\rm chirp})^{5/3}}{c^5}\,\omega^{11/3}.}
	\label{cs:eq:chirp_omega}
\end{equation}
For two equal masses $m$, $M_{\rm chirp}=m/2^{1/5}=0.87\,m$.

\paragraph{Weighing by listening.} A detector measures the wave frequency $f=\omega/\pi$ (Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:f_gw}) and how fast it rises. With $\omega=\pi f$ in Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:chirp_omega},
\begin{equation}
	\pi\dv{f}{t}=\frac{96}{5}\,\frac{(GM_{\rm chirp})^{5/3}}{c^5}\,(\pi f)^{11/3}
	\implies
	\boxed{M_{\rm chirp}=\frac{c^3}{G}\left[\frac{5}{96}\,\pi^{-8/3}f^{-11/3}\,\dv{f}{t}\right]^{3/5}.}
	\label{cs:eq:mchirp}
\end{equation}
Only $c$ and $G$ enter. Neither the distance nor the orientation of the binary is needed. This is how the masses of merging black holes are found. The two masses separately need the smaller terms of higher order in $v/c$ that general relativity adds to Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:chirp_omega}.

\paragraph{Time left, a second route.} Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:chirp_omega} gives
\[
\dv{t}\left(\omega^{-8/3}\right)=-\frac83\,\omega^{-11/3}\,\dv{\omega}{t}=-\frac{256}{5}\,\frac{(GM_{\rm chirp})^{5/3}}{c^5},
\]
a constant. So $\omega^{-8/3}$ falls in a straight line and reaches zero at the merger, a time
\begin{equation}
	t_{\rm merge}=\frac{5}{256}\,\frac{c^5}{(GM_{\rm chirp})^{5/3}\,\omega^{8/3}}
	=\frac38\,f\left(\dv{f}{t}\right)^{-1}
	\label{cs:eq:t_merge_f}
\end{equation}
later. Putting $\omega^{8/3}=(GM)^{4/3}/a_0^4$ and $M_{\rm chirp}^{5/3}M^{4/3}=m_1m_2M$ gives Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:t_merge} again.

\paragraph{The strain of a binary.} For an observer on the $z$ axis, who sees the orbit face on, differentiating $I_{ij}$ twice and using Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:strain_Q},
\begin{align}
	\begin{pmatrix} \ddot Q_{xx} & \ddot Q_{xy}\\ \ddot Q_{xy} & \ddot Q_{yy}\end{pmatrix}
	&= -\frac{2m_1m_2a^2\omega^2}{M}\begin{pmatrix} \cos2\omega t & \sin2\omega t\\ \sin2\omega t & -\cos2\omega t\end{pmatrix}, \nonumber\\
	\begin{pmatrix} h_+ & h_\times\\ h_\times & -h_+\end{pmatrix}
	&\eqby{1} -h_0\begin{pmatrix} \cos2\omega t & \sin2\omega t\\ \sin2\omega t & -\cos2\omega t\end{pmatrix},
	\qquad h_0=\frac{4Gm_1m_2a^2\omega^2}{c^4dM},
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:strain_Q}. This $2\times2$ block already has zero trace, so $\tfrac12(\ddot Q_{xx}-\ddot Q_{yy})=\ddot Q_{xx}$.
\end{explain}
with the time $t$ read as $t-d/c$. By Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:pol_rotate} this is a $+$ pattern of size $h_0$ turned through the angle $\psi=\omega t$ (and the sign reversed). The stretching axis follows the two stars round. With $a^2\omega^2=(GM)^{2/3}\omega^{2/3}$ from Kepler's third law and $\omega=\pi f$,
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{h_0=\frac{4\,(GM_{\rm chirp})^{5/3}\,(\pi f)^{2/3}}{c^4d}.}
	\label{cs:eq:h0}
\end{equation}
An orbit seen at an angle gives a smaller strain. Seen edge on, $h_+$ is half as large and $h_\times$ is zero. As the frequency rises the strain grows as $f^{2/3}$. Figure~\ref{cs:fig:gw_chirp} shows the result, a wave whose frequency and size both grow.

	\centering
	\includegraphics{fig_ch13_gw_chirp.pdf}
	\caption{The strain $h_+$ of a circular binary seen face on, from Eqs.~\eqref{cs:eq:chirp_omega} and~\eqref{cs:eq:h0}, while the orbital frequency triples. The curve ends at the last stable orbit of an equal-mass pair. The dashed lines are $\pm h_0$.}
	\label{cs:fig:gw_chirp}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{GW150914 in numbers.} The first wave detected, on 14 September 2015, came from black holes of about $36$ and $29\,M_\odot$, so $M_{\rm chirp}=28\,M_\odot$, at $d=410\,\mathrm{Mpc}$. Its frequency rose from $35$ to about $150\,\mathrm{Hz}$ in $0.2\,\mathrm s$. The leading-order formulas hold during the slow spiral, which for this pair ends near $70\,\mathrm{Hz}$ (Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:f_end}). The last rise to $150\,\mathrm{Hz}$ is the plunge and merger, where they no longer hold. With $GM_\odot/c^2=1477\,\mathrm m$, Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:h0} at the end of the spiral, $f=70\,\mathrm{Hz}$, gives
\begin{equation}
	h_0=\frac{4\,(28\times1477\,\mathrm m)^{5/3}\,(\pi\times70\,\mathrm{Hz}/c)^{2/3}}{410\times3.086\times10^{22}\,\mathrm m}
	=1.3\times10^{-21},
\end{equation}
against a measured peak of $1.0\times10^{-21}$. The orbit was not seen face on, and the detector does not respond fully to every direction. At $35\,\mathrm{Hz}$, Kepler's third law with $\omega=\pi f$ puts the two bodies $890\,\mathrm{km}$ apart, under five times their combined Schwarzschild radius, $2GM/c^2=192\,\mathrm{km}$, and the spiral goes on down to $6GM/c^2=576\,\mathrm{km}$. Nothing but black holes is that compact for its mass.

\subsection{The First Evidence, a Pulsar Binary}
\label{cs:sec:hulse_taylor}

In 1974 Hulse and Taylor found the pulsar PSR B1913+16 in a binary with another neutron star, with an orbital period $P_{\rm orb}=7.75$ hours and an eccentricity $e=0.617$. The arrival times of its pulses carry the Rømer delay of its orbit (Section~\ref{sec:romer}), and with the relativistic effects of timing, quoted from general relativity, they give both masses, $1.438$ and $1.390\,M_\odot$. With these masses fixed, the decay of the orbit is a prediction with nothing left to adjust.

\paragraph{The predicted decay.} For a circular orbit, $P_{\rm orb}\propto a^{3/2}$ by Kepler's third law, so
\begin{align}
	\frac{1}{P_{\rm orb}}\dv{P_{\rm orb}}{t}
	&\eqby{1} \frac32\,\frac1a\,\dv{a}{t}
	\eqby{2} -\frac{96}{5}\,\frac{G^3m_1m_2M}{c^5a^4},
\end{align}
\begin{explain}
	\item Differentiate $P_{\rm orb}\propto a^{3/2}$.
	\item Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:adot}.
\end{explain}
With $a=1.95\times10^6\,\mathrm{km}$ from Kepler's third law, this gives $\dv*{P_{\rm orb}}{t}=-2.03\times10^{-13}$. The real orbit is eccentric. The two stars radiate most strongly at their closest approach, and general relativity (Peters, 1964) multiplies the circular rate by
\[
\frac{1+\frac{73}{24}e^2+\frac{37}{96}e^4}{(1-e^2)^{7/2}}=11.86\qquad(e=0.617),
\]
quoted here. The prediction is $\dv*{P_{\rm orb}}{t}=-2.40\times10^{-12}$. The measured value, after a small correction for the acceleration of the pulsar in the Galaxy, agrees within a fraction of a percent. The orbit shrinks by about $3.5\,\mathrm m$ a year, and the period by $76\,\mu\mathrm s$ a year. The effect adds up. Over $30$ years the closest approach comes $39\,\mathrm s$ earlier than a fixed period would predict. This was the first evidence that gravitational waves exist, recognised by the Nobel Prize in 1993, and the two stars will merge in about $300$ million years. Many binary pulsars now test Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:quadrupole} in the same way.

\subsection{Detectors}
\label{cs:sec:gw_detectors}

\paragraph{Laser interferometers.} A detector on the ground is a Michelson interferometer with two arms at right angles. A laser beam is split, sent down both arms to mirrors that hang as pendulums and move freely along the arms, and the two returning beams are made to interfere. With the arms along $x$ and $y$, both of length $4\,\mathrm{km}$, Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:ring} moves the end mirrors to
\[
\begin{pmatrix} (1+\tfrac12h_+)\times4\,\mathrm{km}\\ \tfrac12h_\times\times4\,\mathrm{km}\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12h_\times\times4\,\mathrm{km}\\ (1-\tfrac12h_+)\times4\,\mathrm{km}\end{pmatrix}.
\]
To first order in the strain, the sideways shifts do not change the lengths. The $x$ arm grows by $\tfrac12h_+\times4\,\mathrm{km}$ and the $y$ arm shrinks by the same amount, so the difference of the two arms is $h_+\times4\,\mathrm{km}$. For $h_+=10^{-21}$ it is $4\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm m$, about $1/400$ of the diameter of a proton. The interferometer reads the difference, so it is blind to the $\times$ wave along its own arms. Detectors with arms turned to other angles see other combinations of $h_+$ and $h_\times$. The light bounces back and forth hundreds of times in each arm, which multiplies the effect. Below about $10\,\mathrm{Hz}$ the shaking of the ground swamps the signal, so these detectors hear from about $10\,\mathrm{Hz}$ to a few kilohertz.

The two LIGO detectors, at Hanford in Washington and Livingston in Louisiana, have $4\,\mathrm{km}$ arms and lie $3000\,\mathrm{km}$ apart, $10\,\mathrm{ms}$ for light. A real wave reaches both within that time, and the delay between them helps fix the direction of the source. Virgo in Italy and KAGRA in Japan have $3\,\mathrm{km}$ arms. KAGRA is underground and its mirrors are cooled. Planned detectors push further. Cosmic Explorer in the United States would have $40\,\mathrm{km}$ arms, the Einstein Telescope in Europe a triangle of $10\,\mathrm{km}$ arms underground, and NEMO in Australia aims at the kilohertz waves from the hot neutron-star matter left after a merger.

\paragraph{The mass sets the band.} The chirp ends near the last stable circular orbit, at $r=6GM/c^2$ (quoted from general relativity). With Kepler's third law and $f=\omega/\pi$, the frequency there is
\begin{equation}
	f_{\rm end}=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{GM}{(6GM/c^2)^3}}=\frac{c^3}{6^{3/2}\pi GM}=4.4\,\mathrm{kHz}\times\frac{M_\odot}{M}.
	\label{cs:eq:f_end}
\end{equation}
This marks the end of the slow spiral. The plunge and merger that follow carry the frequency up by a factor of two or so.
Heavier binaries sing lower (P6). A pair of neutron stars of $2.8\,M_\odot$ in all ends near $1.6\,\mathrm{kHz}$, and GW150914 near $70\,\mathrm{Hz}$ (its merger then reached $150\,\mathrm{Hz}$), both in the band of the ground detectors. Black holes of $10^6\,M_\odot$ in the centres of galaxies end at $4\,\mathrm{mHz}$, and pairs of $10^9\,M_\odot$ give nanohertz waves for thousands of years before they merge. Each band needs its own detector.

\paragraph{LISA.} The Laser Interferometer Space Antenna is three spacecraft at the corners of a triangle with sides of $2.5$ million kilometres, trailing the Earth in its orbit round the Sun. Lasers between them measure changes in the distances between test masses floating freely inside. A strain of $10^{-20}$ changes a side by $2.5\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm m$. Far from the shaking of the ground, LISA hears the millihertz band, the mergers of black holes of $10^4$ to $10^7\,M_\odot$ and many close pairs of white dwarfs in the Galaxy. It is planned for launch in the 2030s.

\paragraph{Pulsar timing arrays.} Millisecond pulsars are clocks steady enough that their pulse arrival times, corrected for the motion of the Earth and for the pulsar's own orbit (Section~\ref{sec:romer}), can be predicted to better than a microsecond over years. A wave passing between the Earth and a pulsar changes the travel time of the pulses by a fraction of order $h_+$, and the change builds up over about one wave period. The timing shift is of order $h_+/(2\pi f)$, $5\,\mathrm{ns}$ for $h_+=10^{-15}$ at one cycle per year. Many pairs of supermassive black holes add up to a random background of such waves. What marks it out is a definite pattern, the Hellings–Downs curve, in how the timing shifts of two pulsars are correlated, depending on the angle between them on the sky. In 2023 several timing arrays reported evidence for this background, at a strain of about $2\times10^{-15}$ at one cycle per year. The evidence is still being firmed up.

\subsection{GW170817, Light and Gravity Together}
\label{cs:sec:gw170817}

On 17 August 2017 LIGO and Virgo recorded a chirp lasting about a minute and a half in their band, with a chirp mass of $1.19\,M_\odot$. That puts both masses between about $1.2$ and $1.6\,M_\odot$, a pair of neutron stars. Then $1.7\,\mathrm s$ after the merger the Fermi satellite recorded a short burst of gamma rays from the same part of the sky. Eleven hours later telescopes found a new source in the galaxy NGC~4993, about $40\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ away. It was a \textbf{kilonova}. Neutron-rich matter thrown out in the merger forms heavy nuclei, whose radioactive decay makes the debris glow for days, like the nickel of a type Ia supernova. One event was seen in gravitational waves, gamma rays, and visible, infrared, ultraviolet, X-ray and radio light. Such events are called multi-messenger.

\paragraph{The speed of gravity.} The waves and the gamma rays travelled for $d/c=40\,\mathrm{Mpc}/c=4.1\times10^{15}\,\mathrm s$ and arrived $1.7\,\mathrm s$ apart. Even if the whole delay came from a difference of speed, with $v_{\rm GW}$ the speed of the waves,
\[
\frac{|v_{\rm GW}-c|}{c}\lesssim\frac{1.7\,\mathrm s}{4.1\times10^{15}\,\mathrm s}=4\times10^{-16}.
\]
Allowing for a delay of a few seconds in the launch of the burst, gravity travels at the speed of light to about one part in $10^{15}$.

\paragraph{A standard siren.} Eqs.~\eqref{cs:eq:mchirp} and~\eqref{cs:eq:h0} give the distance with no ladder at all. The frequency and its rate give $M_{\rm chirp}$, and then the measured strain gives $d$. A merger is a source of known loudness, a \textbf{standard siren}. Over cosmological distances the waves are stretched by $1+z$, so $f$ and $\dv*{f}{t}$ give $(1+z)M_{\rm chirp}$, and the strain then gives the luminosity distance. The redshift itself must come from the host galaxy. For GW170817 the waves alone gave a distance of $44\,\mathrm{Mpc}$, and NGC~4993 recedes at $3000\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so Hubble's law (Eq.~\eqref{un:eq:hubble}) gives $H_0\approx70\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$, uncertain by about $15\%$, mostly because the tilt of the orbit is poorly known. Many such sirens at larger distances will measure the expansion history as well (Section~\ref{un:sec:acceleration}).

\subsection{What Has Been Seen}

From 2015 to 2020 the ground detectors recorded about ninety mergers, and the run that began in 2023 has added more than two hundred candidates. Most are pairs of black holes, of about $5$ to $100\,M_\odot$ each, heavier on average than the black holes found in X-ray binaries. Two are pairs of neutron stars, GW170817 and GW190425. A few, such as GW200105 and GW200115, are a neutron star with a black hole. In each merger of black holes a few percent of the total mass leaves as waves, $3\,M_\odot$ of $65\,M_\odot$ for GW150914, and what is left is one spinning black hole.

\section{Problem Set: Degenerate and Compact Stars}

\subsection{Problem 1: Gravitational Redshifts}
\label{cs:prob:redshift}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Light leaving the surface of a static star is redshifted by $1+z=(1-2GM/Rc^2)^{-1/2}$. Find $z$ for (a) the Sun, (b) Sirius B ($1.02\,M_\odot$, $5840\,\mathrm{km}$), expressed as a Doppler velocity $cz$, and (c) a neutron star of $1.4\,M_\odot$ and $12\,\mathrm{km}$.

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item For small compactness, $z\approx GM/(Rc^2)$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Sun}.
	\[
	\boxed{z\simeq2.1\times10^{-6},\qquad cz\simeq0.64\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Sirius B}.
	\[
	\boxed{z\simeq2.6\times10^{-4},\qquad cz\simeq77\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
	\]
	The measured shift of Sirius B's lines, about $80\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, was one of the first confirmations that white dwarfs are as dense as the theory requires.
	\item \textbf{Neutron star}. $2GM/Rc^2=0.345$, so $1+z=(0.655)^{-1/2}$.
	\[
	\boxed{z\simeq0.24.}
	\]
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 2: The Chandrasekhar Mass for Other Compositions}
\label{cs:prob:mch}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: Using Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:chandrasekhar}, find the limiting mass of a degenerate core made of (a) helium, carbon or oxygen, (b) iron ($^{56}$Fe, 26 electrons), and (c) pure hydrogen, if such a white dwarf existed.

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $M_{\rm Ch}=5.83\,M_\odot/\mu_e^2$, where $\mu_e$ is the mass per electron in atomic mass units.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Helium, carbon, oxygen}. $\mu_e=2$.
	\[
	\boxed{M_{\rm Ch}\simeq1.46\,M_\odot.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Iron}. $\mu_e=56/26=2.15$.
	\[
	\boxed{M_{\rm Ch}\simeq1.26\,M_\odot.}
	\]
	An iron core grows in a massive star until it nears this mass, and then collapses.
	\item \textbf{Hydrogen}. $\mu_e=1$.
	\[
	\boxed{M_{\rm Ch}\simeq5.8\,M_\odot.}
	\]
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 3: The Energy of a Supernova}
\label{cs:prob:binding}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: A neutron star of $1.4\,M_\odot$ and radius $12\,\mathrm{km}$ forms from the collapse of a core. (a) Estimate the energy released, using the gravitational energy of a uniform sphere. (b) What fraction of the rest energy is this? (c) Compare with all the light the Sun gives off over $10^{10}$ years.

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $|U|=\tfrac35GM^2/R$ (Eq.~\eqref{sg:eq:U_uniform}). The core started far larger, so almost all of $|U|$ is released.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Energy}.
	\[
	\boxed{|U|\simeq\frac35\,\frac{6.674\times10^{-11}\times(2.785\times10^{30})^2}{1.2\times10^4}\,\mathrm J\simeq3\times10^{46}\,\mathrm J.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Fraction}. $Mc^2=2.5\times10^{47}\,\mathrm J$.
	\[
	\boxed{|U|/Mc^2\simeq0.10.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Against the Sun}. $L_\odot\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{yr}=1.2\times10^{44}\,\mathrm J$. A core collapse releases about two hundred times the Sun's entire lifetime output, within seconds. About $99\%$ leaves as neutrinos, which diffuse out of the hot core (P5) in about ten seconds, as was seen from supernova 1987A.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 4: Hotter at the Bottom}
\label{cs:prob:tolman}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: (a) In thermal equilibrium, by how much does the temperature at the bottom of a $10\,\mathrm{km}$ column of the Earth's atmosphere exceed that at the top, according to Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:tolman}? (b) Across a neutron star, with $\Phi/c^2$ changing by about $0.2$, how large is the effect?

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item For a small change of potential, $\Delta T/T\approx-\Delta\Phi/c^2$, with $\Delta\Phi=g\,\Delta h$ near a planet's surface.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Earth}. $\Delta\Phi/c^2=9.81\times10^4/(9.0\times10^{16})$.
	\[
	\boxed{\Delta T/T\simeq1.1\times10^{-12}.}
	\]
	Utterly negligible. The real temperature difference in the air comes from convection (Problem~\ref{st:prob:lapse}).
	\item \textbf{Neutron star}.
	\[
	\boxed{\Delta T/T\sim0.2.}
	\]
	Models of cooling neutron stars must use $T\,e^{\Phi/c^2}$, not $T$, as the quantity that evens out.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem 5: Weighing Two Black Holes by Their Chirp}
\label{cs:prob:chirp}

\textbf{Problem Statement}: A chirp like GW150914 passes $f=50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ while its frequency rises at $\dv*{f}{t}=290\,\mathrm{Hz\,s^{-1}}$. (a) Find the chirp mass. (b) If the two masses are equal, what is each? (c) How long is left before the merger? (d) How far apart are the two bodies at that moment, compared with $2GM/c^2$? (The chirp mass found this way is $(1+z)M_{\rm chirp}$, Section~\ref{cs:sec:gw170817}. For a source at $z=0.09$, as GW150914 was, it is about $9\%$ above the true $28\,M_\odot$. Ignore the difference here.)

\textbf{Essential Ideas}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The chirp mass from $f$ and $\dv*{f}{t}$, Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:mchirp}, and the time left, Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:t_merge_f}.
	\item The orbital angular speed is $\omega=\pi f$ (Eq.~\eqref{cs:eq:f_gw}), and Kepler's third law gives the separation.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detailed Solutions}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \textbf{Chirp mass}. $\tfrac{5}{96}\pi^{-8/3}f^{-11/3}\dv*{f}{t}=4.20\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm s^{5/3}$, whose $\tfrac35$ power is $1.49\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm s$. Dividing by $GM_\odot/c^3=4.93\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm s$,
	\[
	\boxed{M_{\rm chirp}\simeq30\,M_\odot.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Equal masses}. $M_{\rm chirp}=m/2^{1/5}$, so
	\[
	\boxed{m=2^{1/5}M_{\rm chirp}\simeq35\,M_\odot,\qquad M\simeq70\,M_\odot.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Time left}.
	\[
	\boxed{t_{\rm merge}=\frac38\times\frac{50}{290}\,\mathrm s\simeq0.065\,\mathrm s.}
	\]
	\item \textbf{Separation}. $\omega=\pi\times50\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}=157\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $a=(GM/\omega^2)^{1/3}$.
	\[
	\boxed{a\simeq720\,\mathrm{km}\simeq3.5\times\frac{2GM}{c^2}.}
	\]
	Two bodies of $35\,M_\odot$ this close, still orbiting, must be smaller than $360\,\mathrm{km}$ each. Only black holes fit. The last stable orbit of Section~\ref{cs:sec:gw_detectors} is reached at $63\,\mathrm{Hz}$, a few orbits later.
\end{enumerate}
